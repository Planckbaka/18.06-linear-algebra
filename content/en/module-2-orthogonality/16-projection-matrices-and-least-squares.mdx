In engineering measurements, physics experiments, and statistical modeling, we almost always face an **overdetermined system** with far more equations than unknowns: $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$, where $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ and $m \gt n$. Because real-world measurements inevitably carry noise and perturbations, the observation vector $\mathbf{b}$ almost never lands strictly inside the column space $\operatorname{Col}(A)$ spanned by the columns of $A$—so the system $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ has strictly no solution!

Faced with an unsolvable system, neither engineering nor mathematics gives up. Professor Gilbert Strang states a guiding principle that runs through the entire course:

> **"When $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ has no solution, do not despair and do not throw away equations arbitrarily—replace the unsolvable $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ with the closest solvable system $A\hat{\mathbf{x}} = \mathbf{p}$!"**

This closest solvable target $\mathbf{p}$ is precisely the **orthogonal projection** of $\mathbf{b}$ onto the column space $\operatorname{Col}(A)$, while the resulting error vector $\mathbf{e} = \mathbf{b} - \mathbf{p}$ lies strictly in the left nullspace $\operatorname{Null}(A^T)$. In this lecture, we start from the "dual geometric worlds" of data fitting, derive the **normal equations** along two independent paths—zero-calculus subspace Pythagoras and multivariable calculus gradients—thoroughly prove the cornerstone theorem $\operatorname{Null}(A^TA) = \operatorname{Null}(A)$, and develop the complete theory of the orthogonal projection matrix $P = A(A^TA)^{-1}A^T$.

## 1. Two Dual Geometric Worlds: Data Space vs. High-Dimensional Column Space

The biggest hurdle in truly understanding the **method of least squares** is that most students only remember the scatter plot from introductory calculus ("minimize the sum of squared vertical errors") without connecting it to orthogonal projection in high-dimensional subspaces.

In fact, the very same least-squares problem unfolds simultaneously in two completely different geometric worlds:

<LeastSquaresDualGeometryDiagram caption="Dual geometry of least squares: minimizing the sum of squared vertical residuals e_i = b_i - (C + D t_i) in the 2D data plane (left) is identical to projecting the vector b orthogonally onto the 2D column space Col(A) in R^m (right)." />

### Perspective ①: Data Space (The $t$-$b$ Scatter Plane in $\mathbb{R}^2$)

In the two-dimensional data-fitting plane, we are given $m$ observed data points $(t_1, b_1), (t_2, b_2), \dots, (t_m, b_m)$. We want to find the best-fitting straight line:

$$
b = C + Dt
$$

If all $m$ data points were strictly collinear, they would satisfy the linear system exactly:

$$
\begin{cases}
C + D t_1 = b_1 \\
C + D t_2 = b_2 \\
\quad \vdots \\
C + D t_m = b_m
\end{cases}
\iff
\begin{bmatrix}
1 & t_1 \\
1 & t_2 \\
\vdots & \vdots \\
1 & t_m
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix} C \\ D \end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix} b_1 \\ b_2 \\ \vdots \\ b_m \end{bmatrix}
$$

In reality, however, the data points inevitably scatter on both sides of any line. For any candidate parameter vector $\mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} C & D \end{bmatrix}^T$, the model prediction given by the line at each time $t_i$ is $p_i = C + D t_i$.

The discrepancy between the actual observation $b_i$ and the model prediction $p_i$ is called the **vertical residual of the $i$-th sample**:

$$
e_i = b_i - p_i = b_i - (C + D t_i)
$$

The optimization goal of least squares is to find the optimal parameter pair $\hat{\mathbf{x}} = \begin{bmatrix} \hat{C} & \hat{D} \end{bmatrix}^T$ that minimizes the **sum of squared vertical residuals** globally across all observations:

$$
E(C, D) = \sum_{i=1}^{m} e_i^2 = e_1^2 + e_2^2 + \cdots + e_m^2
$$

### Perspective ②: Subspace Geometry (Column Space Projection in $\mathbb{R}^m$)

Now switch to the grand perspective of linear algebra. Let the first column of the data matrix be the all-ones vector $\mathbf{a}_1 = \mathbf{1} = (1, 1, \dots, 1)^T \in \mathbb{R}^m$, the second column be the time vector $\mathbf{a}_2 = \mathbf{t} = (t_1, t_2, \dots, t_m)^T \in \mathbb{R}^m$, and the right-hand side be the observation vector $\mathbf{b} = (b_1, \dots, b_m)^T \in \mathbb{R}^m$.

- The entire system of equations reads $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$, where $A = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{a}_1 & \mathbf{a}_2 \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times 2}$.
- No matter how freely the parameters $\mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} C & D \end{bmatrix}^T$ vary, the matrix-vector product $A\mathbf{x} = C\mathbf{a}_1 + D\mathbf{a}_2$ **can only sweep out a two-dimensional plane living inside $\mathbb{R}^m$**—namely, the column space $\operatorname{Col}(A) = \operatorname{span}\{\mathbf{a}_1, \mathbf{a}_2\}$!
- When the data points do not lie on a single line, the target vector $\mathbf{b} \notin \operatorname{Col}(A)$ floats outside the column-space plane.
- The squared Euclidean norm of the error vector $\mathbf{e} = \mathbf{b} - A\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^m$ is literally:

$$
\|\mathbf{e}\|_2^2 = \|\mathbf{b} - A\mathbf{x}\|_2^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{m} (b_i - (C + D t_i))^2 = E(C, D)
$$

**Geometric intuition pierces through everything at once**:
To find a point $A\hat{\mathbf{x}}$ on the two-dimensional plane $\operatorname{Col}(A)$ that is closest to the external point $\mathbf{b}$, the unique shortest path in Euclidean geometry is to drop a **perpendicular** from $\mathbf{b}$ straight down onto the plane!

The foot of that perpendicular is the optimal prediction vector $\mathbf{p} = A\hat{\mathbf{x}} \in \operatorname{Col}(A)$, and the perpendicular segment itself is the residual vector $\mathbf{e} = \mathbf{b} - \mathbf{p}$. The perpendicularity condition directly demands that **the error vector $\mathbf{e}$ must be completely orthogonal to the column-space plane**!

## 2. The Only Way Out When $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ Has No Solution: Orthogonal Decomposition $\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{p} + \mathbf{e}$

Recall Part 2 of the Fundamental Theorem of Linear Algebra from Lecture 14: in the output space $\mathbb{R}^m$, the column space $\operatorname{Col}(A)$ and the left nullspace $\operatorname{Null}(A^T)$ are **orthogonal complements** of each other:

$$
\mathbb{R}^m = \operatorname{Col}(A) \oplus \operatorname{Null}(A^T), \quad \operatorname{Col}(A) \perp \operatorname{Null}(A^T)
$$

This guarantees that every vector $\mathbf{b} \in \mathbb{R}^m$ can be **uniquely decomposed into the sum of two mutually orthogonal components**:

$$
\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{p} + \mathbf{e}, \quad \text{where } \mathbf{p} \in \operatorname{Col}(A), \; \mathbf{e} \in \operatorname{Null}(A^T)
$$

<OrthogonalDecompositionPythagorasDiagram caption="Orthogonal direct-sum decomposition of b in R^m: b splits uniquely into a solvable column-space projection p in Col(A) and an unsolvable noise vector e in Null(A^T), forming a right triangle that obeys ||b||^2 = ||p||^2 + ||e||^2." />

- **The solvable component $\mathbf{p}$**: Lies inside the column space, so it can always be expressed as a linear combination of the columns of $A$—that is, there exists a parameter vector $\hat{\mathbf{x}} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ such that $A\hat{\mathbf{x}} = \mathbf{p}$ holds strictly!
- **The unsolvable error $\mathbf{e}$**: Lies inside the left nullspace, so no linear combination of the columns of $A$ can explain any part of it. It represents pure measurement noise or modeling discrepancy.

Because $\mathbf{p} \perp \mathbf{e}$, their inner product vanishes ($\mathbf{p}^T \mathbf{e} = 0$), and by the Pythagorean theorem, the squared norm of the observation vector splits cleanly:

$$
\|\mathbf{b}\|_2^2 = \|\mathbf{p}\|_2^2 + \|\mathbf{e}\|_2^2
$$

## 3. Dual Derivations of the Normal Equations $A^TA\hat{\mathbf{x}} = A^T\mathbf{b}$

The core algebraic system that determines the optimal parameters $\hat{\mathbf{x}}$ is called the **normal equations**. Let us derive it from two distinct yet complementary mathematical viewpoints.

### Path A: Subspace Orthogonality and the Pythagorean Theorem (Zero Calculus)

Because the error vector $\mathbf{e} = \mathbf{b} - A\hat{\mathbf{x}}$ is orthogonal to the entire column space $\operatorname{Col}(A)$, it must be perpendicular to every spanning vector of the column space—namely, to **every single column vector** $\mathbf{a}_1, \mathbf{a}_2, \dots, \mathbf{a}_n$ of $A$:

$$
\begin{cases}
\mathbf{a}_1^T \mathbf{e} = 0 \\
\mathbf{a}_2^T \mathbf{e} = 0 \\
\quad \vdots \\
\mathbf{a}_n^T \mathbf{e} = 0
\end{cases}
$$

Stacking these $n$ row-vector inner products vertically immediately yields the compact matrix equation:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}
\mathbf{a}_1^T \\
\mathbf{a}_2^T \\
\vdots \\
\mathbf{a}_n^T
\end{bmatrix}
\mathbf{e} = \mathbf{0} \iff A^T \mathbf{e} = \mathbf{0}
$$

Substituting the definition of the residual $\mathbf{e} = \mathbf{b} - A\hat{\mathbf{x}}$ into this equation gives:

$$
A^T (\mathbf{b} - A\hat{\mathbf{x}}) = \mathbf{0} \implies A^T A \hat{\mathbf{x}} = A^T \mathbf{b}
$$

#### Strict Optimality Proof via the Pythagorean Theorem (No Second Derivatives Needed)

For any other candidate parameter vector $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ (corresponding to any other point $A\mathbf{x} \in \operatorname{Col}(A)$ in the column space), we rewrite the discrepancy vector via a simple identity:

$$
A\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{b} = (A\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{p}) + (\mathbf{p} - \mathbf{b}) = (A\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{p}) - \mathbf{e}
$$

Notice that $(A\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{p}) \in \operatorname{Col}(A)$ while $\mathbf{e} \in \operatorname{Null}(A^T) = \operatorname{Col}(A)^\perp$. These two vectors are strictly orthogonal:

$$
(A\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{p})^T \mathbf{e} = 0
$$

Applying the Pythagorean theorem for the Euclidean norm:

$$
\|A\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{b}\|_2^2 = \|(A\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{p}) - \mathbf{e}\|_2^2 = \|A\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{p}\|_2^2 + \|\mathbf{e}\|_2^2
$$

Because a squared norm is always non-negative ($\|A\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{p}\|_2^2 \ge 0$), we immediately have:

$$
\|A\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{b}\|_2^2 \ge \|\mathbf{e}\|_2^2
$$

Equality holds if and only if $\|A\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{p}\|_2 = 0 \iff A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{p}$.

> [!TIP]
> **The Pure Power of Geometry**: Without taking a single derivative, partial derivative, or Hessian test from calculus, orthogonal subspace decomposition and the Pythagorean theorem prove with absolute rigor that the foot of the perpendicular $\mathbf{p}$ is the unique global closest point in the column space!

### Path B: Multivariable Calculus and Matrix Differentiation (Scalar & Vector Gradients)

Now let us cross-check using the gradient machinery of classical analysis. Expand the objective function as a **quadratic form** in the vector $\mathbf{x}$:

$$
E(\mathbf{x}) = \|A\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{b}\|_2^2 = (A\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{b})^T (A\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{b})
= \mathbf{x}^T A^T A \mathbf{x} - 2\mathbf{b}^T A \mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}^T \mathbf{b}
$$

#### Scalar Two-Variable Cross-Check

If we set $A = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{1} & \mathbf{t} \end{bmatrix}$ and $\mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} C \\ D \end{bmatrix}$, the objective function is a scalar quadratic function of two variables:

$$
E(C, D) = \sum_{i=1}^{m} (C + D t_i - b_i)^2
$$

Setting the partial derivatives with respect to the unknown coefficients $C$ and $D$ equal to zero:

$$
\frac{\partial E}{\partial C} = 2\sum_{i=1}^{m} (C + D t_i - b_i) = 0 \implies m C + \left(\sum_{i=1}^{m} t_i\right) D = \sum_{i=1}^{m} b_i
$$

$$
\frac{\partial E}{\partial D} = 2\sum_{i=1}^{m} t_i (C + D t_i - b_i) = 0 \implies \left(\sum_{i=1}^{m} t_i\right) C + \left(\sum_{i=1}^{m} t_i^2\right) D = \sum_{i=1}^{m} t_i b_i
$$

Writing this as a $2\times 2$ linear system:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}
m & \sum t_i \\
\sum t_i & \sum t_i^2
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix} C \\ D \end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix} \sum b_i \\ \sum t_i b_i \end{bmatrix}
$$

Observe that:

$$
A^T A = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{1}^T \\ \mathbf{t}^T \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{1} & \mathbf{t} \end{bmatrix}
= \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{1}^T\mathbf{1} & \mathbf{1}^T\mathbf{t} \\ \mathbf{t}^T\mathbf{1} & \mathbf{t}^T\mathbf{t} \end{bmatrix}
= \begin{bmatrix} m & \sum t_i \\ \sum t_i & \sum t_i^2 \end{bmatrix}
$$

$$
A^T \mathbf{b} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{1}^T \\ \mathbf{t}^T \end{bmatrix} \mathbf{b}
= \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{1}^T\mathbf{b} \\ \mathbf{t}^T\mathbf{b} \end{bmatrix}
= \begin{bmatrix} \sum b_i \\ \sum t_i b_i \end{bmatrix}
$$

The equations obtained from scalar partial derivatives match $A^TA\hat{\mathbf{x}} = A^T\mathbf{b}$ down to the last symbol!

#### Matrix-Vector Gradient Differentiation

Using the standard real matrix calculus identities:

$$
\nabla_{\mathbf{x}} (\mathbf{x}^T A^T A \mathbf{x}) = 2 A^T A \mathbf{x}, \quad \nabla_{\mathbf{x}} (2\mathbf{b}^T A \mathbf{x}) = 2 A^T \mathbf{b}
$$

Setting the gradient vector equal to the zero vector:

$$
\nabla_{\mathbf{x}} E(\mathbf{x}) = 2 A^T A \mathbf{x} - 2 A^T \mathbf{b} = \mathbf{0} \implies A^T A \hat{\mathbf{x}} = A^T \mathbf{b}
$$

The Hessian matrix of the objective function with respect to $\mathbf{x}$ is $\nabla^2 E(\mathbf{x}) = 2 A^T A$. Whenever $A^TA$ is positive definite (full rank), the stationary point is guaranteed to be the unique strict global minimum! Both derivation paths arrive at the exact same destination.

## 4. The Cornerstone Theorem: $\operatorname{Null}(A^TA) = \operatorname{Null}(A)$ and the Invertibility Criterion

To write $\hat{\mathbf{x}} = (A^TA)^{-1} A^T \mathbf{b}$ directly, the square matrix $A^TA \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$ must be invertible. Under what exact condition is $A^TA$ invertible, and how is it tied to the columns of the original matrix $A$?

<TheoremCard
  label="Theorem 16.1"
  title="Fundamental Nullspace Identity & Invertibility Criterion for AᵀA"
>

Let $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ be any real matrix (not necessarily square; $m$ and $n$ are arbitrary):

1. **Nullspace Identity**: The symmetric square matrix $A^T A \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$ and the original matrix $A$ share the exact same nullspace:

$$
\operatorname{Null}(A^T A) = \operatorname{Null}(A)
$$

2. **Rank Equality**: The rank of the square matrix $A^TA$ is strictly equal to the rank of the original matrix $A$:

$$
\operatorname{rank}(A^T A) = \operatorname{rank}(A)
$$

3. **Necessary and Sufficient Condition for Invertibility**: The $n\times n$ symmetric matrix $A^T A$ is invertible $\iff$ the columns of $A$ are linearly independent (i.e., $A$ has full column rank, $\operatorname{rank}(A) = n$).

</TheoremCard>

<ProofCollapse title="Rigorous Proof: Two-Way Nullspace Inclusion and Invertibility of AᵀA">

**Step 1: Prove $\operatorname{Null}(A^TA) = \operatorname{Null}(A)$**

We prove two-way set inclusion between the two subspaces:

- **Forward Inclusion ($\operatorname{Null}(A) \subseteq \operatorname{Null}(A^TA)$)**:

  Suppose $\mathbf{x} \in \operatorname{Null}(A)$, meaning $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$.
  Left-multiplying both sides by $A^T$ gives:

  $$
  A^T (A\mathbf{x}) = A^T \mathbf{0} = \mathbf{0} \implies (A^TA)\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}
  $$

  Hence $\mathbf{x} \in \operatorname{Null}(A^TA)$, which shows $\operatorname{Null}(A) \subseteq \operatorname{Null}(A^TA)$.

- **Reverse Inclusion ($\operatorname{Null}(A^TA) \subseteq \operatorname{Null}(A)$ — The Key Algebraic Trick!)**:

  Suppose $\mathbf{x} \in \operatorname{Null}(A^TA)$, meaning $(A^TA)\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$.
  **Left-multiply both sides by the row vector $\mathbf{x}^T$**:

  $$
  \mathbf{x}^T (A^TA\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{x}^T \mathbf{0} = 0
  $$

  Regroup using matrix associativity and the transpose rule $(A\mathbf{x})^T = \mathbf{x}^TA^T$:

  $$
  (\mathbf{x}^T A^T)(A\mathbf{x}) = (A\mathbf{x})^T (A\mathbf{x}) = 0
  $$

  Notice that $(A\mathbf{x})^T (A\mathbf{x})$ is precisely the squared Euclidean norm of the vector $A\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^m$:

  $$
  \|A\mathbf{x}\|_2^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{m} (A\mathbf{x})_i^2 = 0
  $$

  Over the **real field $\mathbb{R}$**, a sum of squares of real numbers is zero if and only if every individual term is zero, so we must have:

  $$
  A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}
  $$

  This proves $\mathbf{x} \in \operatorname{Null}(A)$, establishing $\operatorname{Null}(A^TA) \subseteq \operatorname{Null}(A)$.

Combining both inclusions, the two nullspaces are strictly identical: $\operatorname{Null}(A^TA) = \operatorname{Null}(A)$.

---

**Step 2: Prove $\operatorname{rank}(A^TA) = \operatorname{rank}(A)$**

Both $A$ and $A^TA$ have $n$ columns and act on the same input space $\mathbb{R}^n$.
By the Rank-Nullity Theorem:

$$
\operatorname{rank}(A) = n - \dim\operatorname{Null}(A)
$$

$$
\operatorname{rank}(A^TA) = n - \dim\operatorname{Null}(A^TA)
$$

Because $\operatorname{Null}(A^TA) = \operatorname{Null}(A)$, their dimensions are equal: $\dim\operatorname{Null}(A^TA) = \dim\operatorname{Null}(A)$. Therefore:

$$
\operatorname{rank}(A^TA) = \operatorname{rank}(A)
$$

---

**Step 3: Prove the Invertibility Criterion**

The square matrix $A^TA \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$ is invertible if and only if it has full rank $n$:

$$
A^TA \text{ is invertible} \iff \operatorname{rank}(A^TA) = n \iff \operatorname{rank}(A) = n
$$

And $\operatorname{rank}(A) = n$ is equivalent to the $n$ columns of $A$ being linearly independent (full column rank).

> [!NOTE]
> **Field Warning (Why Must the Entries Be Real Numbers in $\mathbb{R}$?)**:
> In Step 1 above, deducing $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$ from $\|A\mathbf{x}\|_2^2 = (A\mathbf{x})^T(A\mathbf{x}) = 0$ relies crucially on the fact that squares of real numbers are non-negative. If we work over the complex field $\mathbb{C}$ and use the unconjugated transpose $A^TA$, the theorem fails immediately!
> For example, take $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & i \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{C}^{1\times 2}$. For $\mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ i \end{bmatrix}$, we have $A\mathbf{x} = 1 + i^2 = 0$, whereas for $\mathbf{y} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -i \end{bmatrix}$, $A\mathbf{y} = 2 \neq 0$ even though $A^T A \mathbf{y} = \mathbf{0}$!
> Over the complex numbers $\mathbb{C}$, you must replace the ordinary transpose with the **Hermitian (conjugate) transpose $A^H A$** for the theorem to hold.

</ProofCollapse>

## 5. Algebraic Construction and Geometric Privileges of the Projection Matrix $P$

When $A$ has full column rank ($\operatorname{rank}(A) = n$), the square matrix $A^TA$ is strictly invertible. The normal equations then possess a unique solution:

$$
\hat{\mathbf{x}} = (A^TA)^{-1} A^T \mathbf{b}
$$

Multiplying the optimal parameter vector $\hat{\mathbf{x}}$ on the left by $A$ produces the projection vector $\mathbf{p}$ in the column space:

$$
\mathbf{p} = A\hat{\mathbf{x}} = A (A^TA)^{-1} A^T \mathbf{b}
$$

Defining the linear transformation matrix $P$ by $\mathbf{p} = P\mathbf{b}$, we arrive at the **orthogonal projection matrix** that projects any vector in $\mathbb{R}^m$ onto $\operatorname{Col}(A)$:

<DefinitionCard
  label="Definition 16.1"
  title="Orthogonal Projection Matrix"
>

Let $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ have linearly independent columns (full column rank). The **orthogonal projection matrix** that projects any vector $\mathbf{b} \in \mathbb{R}^m$ orthogonally onto the column space $\operatorname{Col}(A)$ is:

$$
P = A (A^T A)^{-1} A^T \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
$$

Its corresponding **complementary projection matrix**, which projects $\mathbf{b}$ orthogonally onto the left nullspace $\operatorname{Null}(A^T)$, is:

$$
I_m - P = I_m - A (A^T A)^{-1} A^T \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
$$

</DefinitionCard>

### Four Geometric Privileges of the Projection Matrix

The orthogonal projection matrix $P = A(A^TA)^{-1}A^T$ possesses an extraordinarily clean algebraic structure. Every student of linear algebra should know these four golden properties by heart:

#### 1. Idempotence: $P^2 = P$

Projecting a vector twice in a row produces no further movement. After the first projection, the vector already lies inside the column space, so projecting again leaves it right where it is:

$$
P^2 = \left(A (A^TA)^{-1} A^T\right) \left(A (A^TA)^{-1} A^T\right)
= A (A^TA)^{-1} \underbrace{\left(A^T A\right) (A^TA)^{-1}}_{= I_n} A^T
= A (A^TA)^{-1} A^T = P
$$

#### 2. Symmetry: $P^T = P$

Using the reverse-order law for transposes:

$$
P^T = \left(A (A^TA)^{-1} A^T\right)^T
= (A^T)^T \left((A^TA)^{-1}\right)^T A^T
= A \left((A^TA)^T\right)^{-1} A^T
= A (A^TA)^{-1} A^T = P
$$

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Geometric Meaning**: Idempotence ($P^2 = P$) only tells you that $P$ is *some* projection (possibly oblique); **symmetry ($P^T = P$) is the necessary and sufficient condition for the projection to be an *orthogonal* projection**!

#### 3. Fixed Points and Nullspace Absorption

- If $\mathbf{y} \in \operatorname{Col}(A)$, then $\mathbf{y} = A\mathbf{u}$ for some $\mathbf{u}$. Substituting directly gives:

  $$
  P\mathbf{y} = A (A^TA)^{-1} A^T (A\mathbf{u}) = A (A^TA)^{-1} (A^TA) \mathbf{u} = A\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{y}
  $$

  Every vector inside the column space is an eigenvector of $P$ with eigenvalue $\lambda = 1$!

- If $\mathbf{z} \in \operatorname{Null}(A^T)$, meaning $A^T\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{0}$, substituting directly gives:

  $$
  P\mathbf{z} = A (A^TA)^{-1} (A^T\mathbf{z}) = A (A^TA)^{-1} \mathbf{0} = \mathbf{0}
  $$

  Every vector in the left nullspace is annihilated (mapped to zero) by $P$, corresponding to eigenvalue $\lambda = 0$!

#### 4. Trace-Rank Identity

Using the cyclic property of the matrix trace, $\operatorname{tr}(XY) = \operatorname{tr}(YX)$:

$$
\operatorname{tr}(P) = \operatorname{tr}\left(A (A^TA)^{-1} A^T\right)
= \operatorname{tr}\left(A^T A (A^TA)^{-1}\right)
= \operatorname{tr}(I_n) = n = \operatorname{rank}(A)
$$

The trace of an orthogonal projection matrix equals the exact dimension of the target subspace!

## 6. Classic Worked Example by Hand: Complete Three-Point Line Fit

To turn abstract formulas into muscle memory, let us work out the classic three-point line fit shown in Figure 1 (Prof. Strang's signature classroom example) completely by hand.

Fit a straight line $b = C + Dt$ through the following three observed data points:

- $(t_1, b_1) = (1, 1)$
- $(t_2, b_2) = (2, 2)$
- $(t_3, b_3) = (3, 2)$

### Step 1: Set Up the Matrix $A$ and Vector $\mathbf{b}$

From the unsolvable overdetermined system $\begin{cases} C + 1D = 1 \\ C + 2D = 2 \\ C + 3D = 2 \end{cases}$, we write:

$$
A = \begin{bmatrix}
1 & 1 \\
1 & 2 \\
1 & 3
\end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{3\times 2}, \quad
\mathbf{b} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^3
$$

Clearly, the two columns of $A$ are not collinear, so $\operatorname{rank}(A) = 2$ (full column rank).

### Step 2: Compute $A^TA$ and $A^T\mathbf{b}$ for the Normal Equations

$$
A^T A = \begin{bmatrix}
1 & 1 & 1 \\
1 & 2 & 3
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
1 & 1 \\
1 & 2 \\
1 & 3
\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
1+1+1 & 1+2+3 \\
1+2+3 & 1+4+9
\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
3 & 6 \\
6 & 14
\end{bmatrix}
$$

$$
A^T \mathbf{b} = \begin{bmatrix}
1 & 1 & 1 \\
1 & 2 & 3
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
1 + 2 + 2 \\
1(1) + 2(2) + 3(2)
\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
5 \\
11
\end{bmatrix}
$$

### Step 3: Solve the Normal Equations $A^TA\hat{\mathbf{x}} = A^T\mathbf{b}$

Compute the $2\times 2$ inverse using $\det(A^TA) = 3(14) - 6(6) = 42 - 36 = 6 \neq 0$:

$$
(A^TA)^{-1} = \frac{1}{6} \begin{bmatrix} 14 & -6 \\ -6 & 3 \end{bmatrix}
$$

Solve for the optimal parameters $\hat{\mathbf{x}} = \begin{bmatrix} \hat{C} \\ \hat{D} \end{bmatrix}$:

$$
\hat{\mathbf{x}} = \frac{1}{6} \begin{bmatrix} 14 & -6 \\ -6 & 3 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 11 \end{bmatrix}
= \frac{1}{6} \begin{bmatrix} 14(5) - 6(11) \\ -6(5) + 3(11) \end{bmatrix}
= \frac{1}{6} \begin{bmatrix} 70 - 66 \\ -30 + 33 \end{bmatrix}
= \frac{1}{6} \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}
= \begin{bmatrix} 2/3 \\ 1/2 \end{bmatrix}
$$

Therefore, the best least-squares line is:

$$
\hat{b}(t) = \frac{2}{3} + \frac{1}{2}t
$$

### Step 4: Compute the Projection Vector $\mathbf{p}$ and Residual Vector $\mathbf{e}$

Substitute $\hat{C} = \frac{2}{3}$ and $\hat{D} = \frac{1}{2}$ back into the left-hand side $A\hat{\mathbf{x}}$:

$$
\mathbf{p} = A\hat{\mathbf{x}} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 2/3 \\ 1/2 \end{bmatrix}
= \begin{bmatrix}
2/3 + 1/2 \\
2/3 + 2/2 \\
2/3 + 3/2
\end{bmatrix}
= \begin{bmatrix}
7/6 \\
5/3 \\
13/6
\end{bmatrix}
= \frac{1}{6}\begin{bmatrix} 7 \\ 10 \\ 13 \end{bmatrix}
$$

Compute the residual vector $\mathbf{e} = \mathbf{b} - \mathbf{p}$:

$$
\mathbf{e} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix} 7/6 \\ 10/6 \\ 13/6 \end{bmatrix}
= \begin{bmatrix} -1/6 \\ 2/6 \\ -1/6 \end{bmatrix}
= \begin{bmatrix} -1/6 \\ 1/3 \\ -1/6 \end{bmatrix}
$$

### Step 5: Four-Fold Mathematical Verification (Closed-Loop Check)

Let us verify every single geometric property derived earlier:

1. **Residual orthogonal to the constant column (residuals sum to zero)**:

   $$
   \mathbf{e}^T \mathbf{a}_1 = \mathbf{e}^T \mathbf{1} = \left(-\frac{1}{6}\right) + \frac{1}{3} + \left(-\frac{1}{6}\right) = -\frac{1}{6} + \frac{2}{6} - \frac{1}{6} = 0 \quad \checkmark
   $$

2. **Residual orthogonal to the time column ($t$-weighted residuals sum to zero)**:

   $$
   \mathbf{e}^T \mathbf{a}_2 = \mathbf{e}^T \mathbf{t} = 1\left(-\frac{1}{6}\right) + 2\left(\frac{1}{3}\right) + 3\left(-\frac{1}{6}\right) = -\frac{1}{6} + \frac{4}{6} - \frac{3}{6} = 0 \quad \checkmark
   $$

3. **Projection vector orthogonal to the residual vector**:

   $$
   \mathbf{p}^T \mathbf{e} = \frac{7}{6}\left(-\frac{1}{6}\right) + \frac{10}{6}\left(\frac{2}{6}\right) + \frac{13}{6}\left(-\frac{1}{6}\right)
   = \frac{-7 + 20 - 13}{36} = 0 \quad \checkmark
   $$

4. **Pythagorean Theorem for squared norms holds exactly**:

   $$
   \|\mathbf{b}\|_2^2 = 1^2 + 2^2 + 2^2 = 9 = \frac{324}{36}
   $$

   $$
   \|\mathbf{p}\|_2^2 = \left(\frac{7}{6}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{10}{6}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{13}{6}\right)^2 = \frac{49 + 100 + 169}{36} = \frac{318}{36} = \frac{53}{6}
   $$

   $$
   \|\mathbf{e}\|_2^2 = \left(-\frac{1}{6}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{2}{6}\right)^2 + \left(-\frac{1}{6}\right)^2 = \frac{1 + 4 + 1}{36} = \frac{6}{36} = \frac{1}{6}
   $$

   $$
   \|\mathbf{p}\|_2^2 + \|\mathbf{e}\|_2^2 = \frac{318}{36} + \frac{6}{36} = \frac{324}{36} = 9 = \|\mathbf{b}\|_2^2 \quad \checkmark
   $$

All four identities close without a flaw!

## 7. Common Pitfalls & Transcript Errata

<PitfallAlert title="Pitfall ①: Never Mistake Left-Multiplication by Aᵀ for an 'Equivalent Transformation' that Preserves Solutions">

Many beginners fall into a dangerous misconception: they treat multiplying both sides of $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ on the left by $A^T$ to get $A^T A \hat{\mathbf{x}} = A^T \mathbf{b}$ as if it were high-school algebra ("multiply both sides by the same number"), thinking it is an equivalent operation that preserves the solution set.

**That is completely wrong!**

- The original system $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ is an $m$-row overdetermined system; when $\mathbf{b} \notin \operatorname{Col}(A)$, it has **no solution at all** (its solution set is the empty set $\emptyset$)!
- The normal system $A^T A \hat{\mathbf{x}} = A^T \mathbf{b}$, by contrast, is an $n\times n$ square system that **always has a solution**!
- How does left-multiplying by $A^T$ turn "no solution" into "solvable"?

  Because the entire reason $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ has no solution is the residual component $\mathbf{e} \in \operatorname{Null}(A^T)$; the very instant you multiply on the left by $A^T$, you perform a surgical subspace filter:

  $$
  A^T \mathbf{b} = A^T (\mathbf{p} + \mathbf{e}) = A^T \mathbf{p} + A^T \mathbf{e} = A^T \mathbf{p} + \mathbf{0} = A^T \mathbf{p}
  $$

  **Left-multiplying by $A^T$ completely annihilates the unsolvable component $\mathbf{e}$ living in the left nullspace!** This is not an equivalent algebraic manipulation—it is an irreversible orthogonal projection filter!

</PitfallAlert>

<PitfallAlert title="Pitfall ②: When Columns of A Are Dependent, AᵀA Is Singular and x̂ Is Not Unique—Yet the Projection p Remains Strictly Unique!">

If the columns of $A$ are linearly dependent ($\operatorname{rank}(A) \lt n$), then $A^TA$ is not invertible and the normal equations $A^TA\hat{\mathbf{x}} = A^T\mathbf{b}$ have infinitely many solutions. Specifically, if $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_0$ is one particular least-squares solution, then for any nonzero nullspace vector $\mathbf{x}_n \in \operatorname{Null}(A)$, $\hat{\mathbf{x}} = \hat{\mathbf{x}}_0 + \mathbf{x}_n$ is also a least-squares solution.

However, remember this crucial fact: **even though the parameter vector $\hat{\mathbf{x}}$ is not unique, the projection vector $\mathbf{p} = A\hat{\mathbf{x}}$ is still globally and uniquely determined!**

The proof takes a single line:

$$
A\hat{\mathbf{x}} = A(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_0 + \mathbf{x}_n) = A\hat{\mathbf{x}}_0 + A\mathbf{x}_n = A\hat{\mathbf{x}}_0 + \mathbf{0} = A\hat{\mathbf{x}}_0 = \mathbf{p}
$$

Geometrically, when you drop a perpendicular from a point $\mathbf{b}$ onto a fixed subspace $\operatorname{Col}(A)$, the foot of the perpendicular $\mathbf{p}$ is obviously unique; the only reason there are infinitely many parameter vectors $\hat{\mathbf{x}}$ is that the spanning vectors you chose for that subspace have redundant linear dependencies among themselves!

</PitfallAlert>

<PitfallAlert title="Pitfall ③: Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) vs. Total Least Squares (TLS / PCA) Measure Fundamentally Different Geometric Distances">

In statistics and data analysis, many students mistakenly believe that least-squares line fitting minimizes the *perpendicular Euclidean distance* from each data point to the fitted line in the 2D scatter plot.

**This is a classic conceptual confusion!**

- **Ordinary Least Squares (OLS)**: Minimizes the **vertical residuals** $|b_i - \hat{b}_i|$ measured parallel to the $b$-axis. Here we assume the independent variable $t_i$ is a precisely controlled, noise-free input, and all random measurement error occurs in the dependent variable $b_i$.
- **Total Least Squares (TLS) / Principal Component Analysis (PCA)**: If *both* $t_i$ and $b_i$ contain measurement noise, then we minimize the **perpendicular Euclidean distance** from each data point to the fitted line/manifold! That problem leads to eigenvalue decomposition of covariance matrices and the Singular Value Decomposition (SVD), which we will explore in Lecture 30.

</PitfallAlert>

2. Compute the projection vector $\mathbf{p} = P\mathbf{b}$ and the residual vector $\mathbf{e} = \mathbf{b} - \mathbf{p}$;
3. Verify orthogonality $\mathbf{p} \perp \mathbf{e}$ and the Pythagorean theorem $\|\mathbf{b}\|_2^2 = \|\mathbf{p}\|_2^2 + \|\mathbf{e}\|_2^2$.

<DualSolution
  triggerLabel="Show Full Dual Solution (Intuition + Rigorous Proof)"
  intuitionLabel="Geometric Intuition"
  rigorousLabel="Rigorous Proof / Construction"
>
<SolutionIntuition>

On a one-dimensional line, the projection vector $\mathbf{p}$ simply scales the direction vector $\mathbf{a}$ by the scalar projection coefficient $\hat{x} = \frac{\mathbf{a}^T\mathbf{b}}{\mathbf{a}^T\mathbf{a}}$. Geometrically, $\mathbf{b}$, its projection $\mathbf{p}$, and the residual $\mathbf{e}$ form a right triangle with hypotenuse $\mathbf{b}$ and legs $\mathbf{p}$ (along the line) and $\mathbf{e}$ (perpendicular to the line).

</SolutionIntuition>
<SolutionRigorous>

1. Compute the outer-product matrix and inner-product scalar:

   $$
   \mathbf{a}\mathbf{a}^T = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{a}^T\mathbf{a} = 1^2 + 2^2 = 5
   $$

   Hence the 1D orthogonal projection matrix is:

   $$
   P = \frac{1}{5} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.2 & 0.4 \\ 0.4 & 0.8 \end{bmatrix}
   $$

   The complementary projection matrix is:

   $$
   I_2 - P = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} - \frac{1}{5}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{5}\begin{bmatrix} 4 & -2 \\ -2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}
   $$

2. Compute the projection vector $\mathbf{p}$ and error vector $\mathbf{e}$:

   $$
   \mathbf{p} = P\mathbf{b} = \frac{1}{5} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{5} \begin{bmatrix} 1(3) + 2(1) \\ 2(3) + 4(1) \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{5} \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 10 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} = \mathbf{a}
   $$

   $$
   \mathbf{e} = \mathbf{b} - \mathbf{p} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}
   $$

3. Verify orthogonality and the Pythagorean norm identity:
   - Dot product check: $\mathbf{p}^T \mathbf{e} = 1(2) + 2(-1) = 2 - 2 = 0$, strictly orthogonal!
   - Squared norms:

     $$
     \|\mathbf{b}\|_2^2 = 3^2 + 1^2 = 10
     $$

     $$
     \|\mathbf{p}\|_2^2 = 1^2 + 2^2 = 5, \quad \|\mathbf{e}\|_2^2 = 2^2 + (-1)^2 = 5
     $$

     $$
     \|\mathbf{p}\|_2^2 + \|\mathbf{e}\|_2^2 = 5 + 5 = 10 = \|\mathbf{b}\|_2^2
     $$

   Every property holds exactly.

</SolutionRigorous>

<SolutionIntuition>

The right-hand side $A^T\mathbf{b}$ of the normal equations always lies in $\operatorname{Col}(A^T)$. Since $\operatorname{Col}(A^TA) = \operatorname{Col}(A^T)$ for any real matrix $A$, the normal equations **are always consistent (a solution always exists)**.
However, whether that solution is **unique** depends on whether the square matrix $A^TA$ is invertible. If the columns of $A$ are linearly dependent, there exist nonzero nullspace vectors $\mathbf{x}_n \in \operatorname{Null}(A)$, and the set of least-squares solutions forms an entire affine subspace $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_0 + \operatorname{Null}(A)$ with infinitely many solutions!

</SolutionIntuition>
<SolutionRigorous>

The statement is **False**.

**Explicit Counterexample**:
Take $m = 3, n = 2$:

$$
A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{b} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}
$$

Clearly, the two columns of $A$ are identical, so $\operatorname{rank}(A) = 1 \lt n = 2$.

Compute the normal system:

$$
A^TA = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 3 \\ 3 & 3 \end{bmatrix}, \quad A^T\mathbf{b} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}
$$

The normal equations are:

$$
\begin{bmatrix} 3 & 3 \\ 3 & 3 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \hat{x}_1 \\ \hat{x}_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \iff 3\hat{x}_1 + 3\hat{x}_2 = 1 \iff \hat{x}_1 + \hat{x}_2 = \frac{1}{3}
$$

This equation represents a line in $\mathbb{R}^2$ with **infinitely many solutions** (for instance, $\hat{\mathbf{x}} = \begin{bmatrix} 1/3 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1/3 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 1/6 \\ 1/6 \end{bmatrix}$, etc.)!

Notice that although there are infinitely many least-squares parameter vectors $\hat{\mathbf{x}}$, the resulting projection vector $\mathbf{p} = A\hat{\mathbf{x}}$ is still strictly unique:

$$
\mathbf{p} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \hat{x}_1 \\ \hat{x}_2 \end{bmatrix} = (\hat{x}_1 + \hat{x}_2) \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{3} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}
$$

Thus, the normal equations have a unique solution if and only if $A$ has full column rank ($\operatorname{rank}(A) = n$).

</SolutionRigorous>

<ExerciseCard
  id="l16-ex4"
  tier="L3"
  tierLabel="L3 · Zero-Calculation Subspace Reasoning"
  title="Deep Duality Between Householder Reflections and Orthogonal Projections"
>

Let $P \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times m}$ be the orthogonal projection matrix onto an $r$-dimensional subspace $S = \operatorname{Col}(A)$ (so $P^2 = P = P^T$). Define the matrix:

$$
R = I_m - 2P
$$

1. Prove that $R$ is a **symmetric orthogonal matrix**, meaning $R^T = R$ and $R^T R = I_m$;
2. Explain the geometric action of $R$, and determine all eigenvalues of $R$ along with their multiplicities;
3. Express the determinant $\det(R)$ as a function of the subspace dimension $r$.

<ProgressiveHint
  buttonPrefix="Unlock Hint"
  hints={[
    "Algebraically expand $(I - 2P)^T (I - 2P)$ directly and simplify using $P^2 = P$ and $P^T = P$.",
    "Decompose any vector as $\\mathbf{x} = \\mathbf{p} + \\mathbf{e}$ and inspect how $R\\mathbf{x}$ acts on each of the two orthogonal pieces."
  ]}
/>

<DualSolution
  triggerLabel="Show Full Dual Solution (Intuition + Rigorous Proof)"
  intuitionLabel="Geometric Intuition"
  rigorousLabel="Rigorous Proof / Construction"
>
<SolutionIntuition>

An orthogonal projection $P$ keeps the component inside $S$ and kills the perpendicular component; $R = I - 2P$ flips the sign of the component inside $S$ (multiplies by $-1$) while leaving the perpendicular component in $S^\perp$ untouched (multiplies by $+1$). That is a **mirror reflection** across $S^\perp$!
Because a reflection preserves lengths and angles, its matrix must be orthogonal. Every basis vector in the $r$-dimensional subspace $S$ gets reversed (eigenvalue $-1$), while every basis vector in the $(m-r)$-dimensional orthogonal complement $S^\perp$ stays fixed (eigenvalue $+1$).

</SolutionIntuition>
<SolutionRigorous>

1. **Prove Symmetry and Orthogonality**:
   - Symmetry:

     $$
     R^T = (I_m - 2P)^T = I_m^T - 2P^T = I_m - 2P = R
     $$

   - Orthogonality:

     $$
     R^T R = R^2 = (I_m - 2P)(I_m - 2P) = I_m - 4P + 4P^2
     $$

     Since $P^2 = P$, substituting gives:

     $$
     R^T R = I_m - 4P + 4P = I_m
     $$

     Thus $R$ is both symmetric and orthogonal ($R^{-1} = R^T = R$, an involution).

2. **Geometric Meaning and Eigenvalue Spectrum**:

   Decompose any vector $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^m$ orthogonally as $\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{x}_S + \mathbf{x}_{S^\perp}$, where $\mathbf{x}_S = P\mathbf{x} \in S$ and $\mathbf{x}_{S^\perp} = (I-P)\mathbf{x} \in S^\perp$. Then:

   $$
   R\mathbf{x} = (I_m - 2P)(\mathbf{x}_S + \mathbf{x}_{S^\perp}) = \mathbf{x}_S + \mathbf{x}_{S^\perp} - 2\mathbf{x}_S = -\mathbf{x}_S + \mathbf{x}_{S^\perp}
   $$

   The matrix $R$ reverses the component parallel to $S$ while preserving the component in $S^\perp$. This is a **Householder reflection** across the mirror subspace $S^\perp$.

   - For any nonzero $\mathbf{v} \in S$, $P\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v}$, so $R\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v} - 2\mathbf{v} = -\mathbf{v}$. Hence $\lambda = -1$ is an eigenvalue with eigenspace $S$ and multiplicity $\dim(S) = r$.
   - For any nonzero $\mathbf{w} \in S^\perp$, $P\mathbf{w} = \mathbf{0}$, so $R\mathbf{w} = \mathbf{w} - \mathbf{0} = \mathbf{w}$. Hence $\lambda = +1$ is an eigenvalue with eigenspace $S^\perp$ and multiplicity $\dim(S^\perp) = m - r$.

3. **Determinant Calculation**:

   The determinant of a matrix equals the product of all its eigenvalues (counted with multiplicity):

   $$
   \det(R) = \prod_{i=1}^{m} \lambda_i = (-1)^r \cdot (+1)^{m - r} = (-1)^r
   $$

   When the reflected dimension $r$ is odd, $\det(R) = -1$ (orientation-reversing); when $r$ is even, $\det(R) = +1$ (orientation-preserving composition of reflections).

</SolutionRigorous>
</DualSolution>

In statistics and machine learning, we fit a least-squares line $b = C + Dt$ to $m$ observed data points $(t_1, b_1), (t_2, b_2), \dots, (t_m, b_m)$. Denote the sample means of time and observations by:

$$
\bar{t} = \frac{1}{m} \sum_{i=1}^{m} t_i, \quad \bar{b} = \frac{1}{m} \sum_{i=1}^{m} b_i
$$

1. Using the first row of the normal equations $A^TA\hat{\mathbf{x}} = A^T\mathbf{b}$, prove that the optimal least-squares line must pass strictly through the **sample centroid (center of mass)** $(\bar{t}, \bar{b})$ of the dataset;
2. Now **center** the independent variable by shifting $T_i = t_i - \bar{t}$ and fitting the equivalent model $b = C' + DT$. Write down the new design matrix $A'$ and prove that $A'^T A'$ automatically becomes a **strictly diagonal matrix**;
3. Exploit the diagonal inverse to write decoupled closed-form formulas for the slope $D$ and new intercept $C'$, and explain from a subspace geometry perspective why centering eliminates coupling between parameter estimates.

<SolutionIntuition>

In the uncentered model, the first column is the all-ones vector $\mathbf{1}$ and the second column is the raw time vector $\mathbf{t}$. In general, these two vectors are not at a $90^\circ$ angle, so $A^TA$ is a dense matrix with nonzero off-diagonal entries, coupling the solve for the intercept $C$ and slope $D$.
When we subtract the mean $\bar{t}$ from each time coordinate, the entries of the centered vector $\mathbf{T}$ sum to zero—meaning the new second column $\mathbf{T}$ is strictly orthogonal to the all-ones column $\mathbf{1}$! Because the two columns are now perpendicular, the normal equations decouple into two independent 1D projections, allowing $C'$ and $D$ to be solved separately! This is the most natural prototype of Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization, which we study in the next lecture.

</SolutionIntuition>
<SolutionRigorous>

1. **Prove that the regression line passes through the centroid $(\bar{t}, \bar{b})$**:

   With $A = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{1} & \mathbf{t} \end{bmatrix}$, the normal equations are:

   $$
   A^T A \begin{bmatrix} C \\ D \end{bmatrix} = A^T \mathbf{b} \iff \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{1}^T \\ \mathbf{t}^T \end{bmatrix} (\mathbf{1}C + \mathbf{t}D) = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{1}^T \mathbf{b} \\ \mathbf{t}^T \mathbf{b} \end{bmatrix}
   $$

   Extracting the first row gives:

   $$
   \mathbf{1}^T (\mathbf{1}C + \mathbf{t}D) = \mathbf{1}^T \mathbf{b} \implies (\mathbf{1}^T \mathbf{1})C + (\mathbf{1}^T \mathbf{t})D = \mathbf{1}^T \mathbf{b}
   $$

   Since $\mathbf{1}^T \mathbf{1} = \sum_{i=1}^m 1 = m$, $\mathbf{1}^T \mathbf{t} = \sum_{i=1}^m t_i = m\bar{t}$, and $\mathbf{1}^T \mathbf{b} = \sum_{i=1}^m b_i = m\bar{b}$, substituting yields:

   $$
   m C + m\bar{t} D = m\bar{b}
   $$

   Dividing both sides by $m$:

   $$
   C + D\bar{t} = \bar{b}
   $$

   The left-hand side is precisely the line's prediction $\hat{b}(\bar{t})$ at $t = \bar{t}$, proving that the optimal line always passes through $(\bar{t}, \bar{b})$.

2. **Prove that $A'^T A'$ is diagonal**:

   Define the centered time vector $\mathbf{T} = \mathbf{t} - \bar{t}\mathbf{1} = \begin{bmatrix} t_1 - \bar{t} \\ \vdots \\ t_m - \bar{t} \end{bmatrix}$.
   The new design matrix is $A' = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{1} & \mathbf{T} \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times 2}$.
   Compute the inner product of its two columns:

   $$
   \mathbf{1}^T \mathbf{T} = \sum_{i=1}^{m} (t_i - \bar{t}) = \sum_{i=1}^{m} t_i - m\bar{t} = m\bar{t} - m\bar{t} = 0
   $$

   Because their dot product is zero, the columns $\mathbf{1}$ and $\mathbf{T}$ form an **orthogonal pair of vectors**!
   Computing the normal matrix:

   $$
   A'^T A' = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{1}^T \\ \mathbf{T}^T \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{1} & \mathbf{T} \end{bmatrix}
   = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{1}^T \mathbf{1} & \mathbf{1}^T \mathbf{T} \\ \mathbf{T}^T \mathbf{1} & \mathbf{T}^T \mathbf{T} \end{bmatrix}
   = \begin{bmatrix} m & 0 \\ 0 & \|\mathbf{T}\|_2^2 \end{bmatrix}
   $$

   The off-diagonal entries vanish identically, so $A'^T A'$ is strictly diagonal!

3. **Completely Decoupled Closed-Form Formulas**:

   Inverting a diagonal matrix simply reciprocates its diagonal entries:

   $$
   (A'^T A')^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{1}{m} & 0 \\ 0 & \frac{1}{\|\mathbf{T}\|_2^2} \end{bmatrix}
   $$

   The right-hand side is:

   $$
   A'^T \mathbf{b} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{1}^T \mathbf{b} \\ \mathbf{T}^T \mathbf{b} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} m\bar{b} \\ \sum_{i=1}^m (t_i - \bar{t})b_i \end{bmatrix}
   $$

   Multiplying directly, the two unknowns decouple into two independent 1D orthogonal projections:

   $$
   \hat{\mathbf{x}}' = \begin{bmatrix} C' \\ D \end{bmatrix} = (A'^T A')^{-1} A'^T \mathbf{b}
   = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{1}{m} \cdot m\bar{b} \\ \frac{\mathbf{T}^T \mathbf{b}}{\mathbf{T}^T \mathbf{T}} \end{bmatrix}
   = \begin{bmatrix} \bar{b} \\ \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{m} (t_i - \bar{t})b_i}{\sum_{i=1}^{m} (t_i - \bar{t})^2} \end{bmatrix}
   $$

   Since $\sum (t_i - \bar{t})\bar{b} = \bar{b}\sum (t_i - \bar{t}) = 0$, the numerator can be written symmetrically in terms of the sample covariance:

   $$
   D = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{m} (t_i - \bar{t})(b_i - \bar{b})}{\sum_{i=1}^{m} (t_i - \bar{t})^2} = \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(t, b)}{\operatorname{Var}(t)}
   $$

   $$
   C' = \bar{b} \implies C = C' - D\bar{t} = \bar{b} - D\bar{t}
   $$

   **Geometric Takeaway**: Centering the explanatory variable is geometrically equivalent to replacing the original basis $\{\mathbf{1}, \mathbf{t}\}$ of $\operatorname{Col}(A)$ with an orthogonal basis $\{\mathbf{1}, \mathbf{T}\}$. Whenever the basis vectors of a subspace are mutually orthogonal, multidimensional least-squares projection collapses into the sum of independent 1D projections along each basis direction—the exact geometric foundation of all classical linear regression formulas in statistics.
