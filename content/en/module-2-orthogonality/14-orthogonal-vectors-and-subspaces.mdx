In Module 1, we thoroughly analyzed linear systems $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$, Gaussian elimination, and the dimension theorem for the four fundamental subspaces ($r, n-r, r, m-r$). Yet across all those algebraic derivations, we never introduced the most fundamental geometric measurement of all: **when are vectors or subspaces orthogonal?**

In the MIT 18.06 lecture hall, Professor Gilbert Strang delivers one of the most passionate lines in the entire course: **"$90^\circ$ is the finest angle in all of mathematics!"**

When two vectors meet at a right angle, their dot product vanishes, the cross term in norm calculations disappears completely, and the Euclidean Pythagorean theorem holds in $n$-dimensional space. Orthogonal subspaces form a direct sum inside the ambient space. The sum fills the ambient space when the two subspaces are orthogonal complements. In this lecture, we formally unveil **Part II of the Fundamental Theorem of Linear Algebra**: geometrically, the row space and nullspace of a matrix—as well as its column space and left nullspace—are not merely pairs of subspaces whose dimensions add up; they are pairs of exact **orthogonal complements**. This insight forms the complete geometric bedrock for projection matrices, least squares, QR decomposition, and the Singular Value Decomposition (SVD).

## 1. Orthogonality of Vectors and the $n$-Dimensional Pythagorean Theorem

Before examining subspaces, we must translate our geometric intuition of perpendicularity in $\mathbb{R}^n$ into a rigorous algebraic definition.

### 1. Real Inner Product and Euclidean Norm

Let $\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ be two $n$-dimensional real column vectors. Their **standard dot product (real inner product)** is defined as the sum of the products of their corresponding coordinates:

$$
\mathbf{x}^T \mathbf{y} = \mathbf{y}^T \mathbf{x} = x_1 y_1 + x_2 y_2 + \cdots + x_n y_n = \sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i y_i
$$

The **Euclidean length (Euclidean norm / $L_2$ norm)** of a vector $\mathbf{x}$ is defined as the non-negative square root of its dot product with itself, so its squared length is simply the inner product of $\mathbf{x}$ with itself:

$$
\|\mathbf{x}\|_2 = \sqrt{\mathbf{x}^T \mathbf{x}} = \sqrt{x_1^2 + x_2^2 + \cdots + x_n^2} \implies \|\mathbf{x}\|_2^2 = \mathbf{x}^T \mathbf{x}
$$

<DefinitionCard label="Definition 14.1" title="Orthogonal Vectors">

Let $\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y} \in \mathbb{R}^n$. We say that $\mathbf{x}$ and $\mathbf{y}$ are **orthogonal** (or **perpendicular**), written $\mathbf{x} \perp \mathbf{y}$, if and only if their dot product is strictly zero:

$$
\mathbf{x} \perp \mathbf{y} \iff \mathbf{x}^T \mathbf{y} = 0
$$

</DefinitionCard>

Under this definition, **the zero vector $\mathbf{0}$ holds a uniquely special status**:
For any vector $\mathbf{x}$ in the entire space $\mathbb{R}^n$, since $\mathbf{0}^T \mathbf{x} = 0 \cdot x_1 + \cdots + 0 \cdot x_n = 0$, **the zero vector $\mathbf{0}$ is orthogonal to every vector in $\mathbb{R}^n$!** At the same time, the zero vector is the **only vector in the entire space that is orthogonal to itself** (since $\mathbf{x}^T \mathbf{x} = 0 \iff \|\mathbf{x}\|_2^2 = 0 \iff \mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$).

### 2. Algebraic Necessity and Sufficiency of the Pythagorean Theorem

In classical Greek geometry, the squared hypotenuse of a right triangle equals the sum of the squares of its two legs. This law remains true in any $n$-dimensional Euclidean space, and it is **strictly equivalent** to having a zero dot product.

<TheoremCard label="Theorem 14.1" title="Pythagorean Theorem in ℝⁿ">

Let $\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ be arbitrary real vectors. The vectors satisfy the Pythagorean relation $\|\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{y}\|_2^2 = \|\mathbf{x}\|_2^2 + \|\mathbf{y}\|_2^2$ if and only if $\mathbf{x}$ and $\mathbf{y}$ are orthogonal:

$$
\|\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{y}\|_2^2 = \|\mathbf{x}\|_2^2 + \|\mathbf{y}\|_2^2 \iff \mathbf{x}^T \mathbf{y} = 0 \iff \mathbf{x} \perp \mathbf{y}
$$

Similarly, the squared norm of the difference vector satisfies the exact same equivalence: $\|\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{y}\|_2^2 = \|\mathbf{x}\|_2^2 + \|\mathbf{y}\|_2^2 \iff \mathbf{x}^T \mathbf{y} = 0$.

</TheoremCard>

Two nonzero real vectors are orthogonal exactly when their angle is $90^\circ$. An angle with the zero vector is undefined. Over $\mathbb{C}$ the inner product uses $\mathbf{x}^H\mathbf{y}$, and the squared-norm expansion has cross term $2\operatorname{Re}(\mathbf{x}^H\mathbf{y})$. Thus the single Pythagorean identity over $\mathbb{C}$ only forces the real part of the inner product to vanish.

<ProofCollapse title="Rigorous Proof: Algebraic Derivation of the n-Dimensional Pythagorean Theorem">

Using matrix transposition and the bilinearity of the inner product, we expand the squared norm of the vector sum directly:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\|\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{y}\|_2^2 &= (\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{y})^T (\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{y}) \\
&= \mathbf{x}^T \mathbf{x} + \mathbf{x}^T \mathbf{y} + \mathbf{y}^T \mathbf{x} + \mathbf{y}^T \mathbf{y} \\
&= \|\mathbf{x}\|_2^2 + 2\mathbf{x}^T \mathbf{y} + \|\mathbf{y}\|_2^2
\end{aligned}
$$

Subtracting $\|\mathbf{x}\|_2^2 + \|\mathbf{y}\|_2^2$ from both sides immediately yields:

$$
\|\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{y}\|_2^2 - (\|\mathbf{x}\|_2^2 + \|\mathbf{y}\|_2^2) = 2\mathbf{x}^T \mathbf{y}
$$

Therefore, the left-hand side is zero if and only if the right-hand side $2\mathbf{x}^T \mathbf{y} = 0$, which means $\mathbf{x}^T \mathbf{y} = 0$.

By the same token, for the difference vector:

$$
\|\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{y}\|_2^2 = (\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{y})^T (\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{y}) = \|\mathbf{x}\|_2^2 - 2\mathbf{x}^T \mathbf{y} + \|\mathbf{y}\|_2^2
$$

Hence $\|\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{y}\|_2^2 = \|\mathbf{x}\|_2^2 + \|\mathbf{y}\|_2^2 \iff \mathbf{x}^T \mathbf{y} = 0$ as well. Moreover, when $\mathbf{x} \perp \mathbf{y}$, the two diagonals of the parallelogram spanned by $\mathbf{x}$ and $\mathbf{y}$ have strictly equal length: $\|\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{y}\|_2 = \|\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{y}\|_2$ (the $n$-dimensional generalization of the theorem that a rectangle has equal diagonals).

</ProofCollapse>

#### Worked Numerical Verification

Take two concrete integer vectors in $\mathbb{R}^3$:

$$
\mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{y} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}
$$

Compute their inner product:

$$
\mathbf{x}^T \mathbf{y} = 1(2) + 2(-1) + 3(0) = 2 - 2 + 0 = 0 \implies \mathbf{x} \perp \mathbf{y}
$$

Now compute the squared norms of each vector:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\|\mathbf{x}\|_2^2 &= 1^2 + 2^2 + 3^2 = 1 + 4 + 9 = 14 \\
\|\mathbf{y}\|_2^2 &= 2^2 + (-1)^2 + 0^2 = 4 + 1 + 0 = 5 \\
\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{y} &= \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 1 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} \implies \|\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{y}\|_2^2 = 3^2 + 1^2 + 3^2 = 9 + 1 + 9 = 19
\end{aligned}
$$

Checking the Pythagorean identity: $14 + 5 = 19$, an exact match!
Checking the difference vector norm as well:

$$
\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{y} = \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 3 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} \implies \|\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{y}\|_2^2 = (-1)^2 + 3^2 + 3^2 = 1 + 9 + 9 = 19
$$

Both diagonal lengths match completely.

## 2. Orthogonal Subspaces and Orthogonal Complements

When we elevate the concept of orthogonality from "a pair of vectors" to "two subspaces," we must maintain strict mathematical precision. Beginners frequently fall into intuitive traps here.

<DefinitionCard label="Definition 14.2" title="Orthogonal Subspaces">

Let $V$ and $W$ be two subspaces of the real vector space $\mathbb{R}^n$. We say that $V$ and $W$ are **orthogonal subspaces**, written $V \perp W$, if and only if **every vector** in $V$ is orthogonal to **every vector** in $W$:

$$
V \perp W \iff \mathbf{v}^T \mathbf{w} = 0 \quad (\forall \mathbf{v} \in V, \; \forall \mathbf{w} \in W)
$$

</DefinitionCard>

From this definition, we immediately obtain a crucial corollary regarding the intersection of orthogonal subspaces:

> **Intersection Corollary for Orthogonal Subspaces**: If $V \perp W$, then they must intersect only at the origin, i.e., $V \cap W = \{\mathbf{0}\}$.
> 
> *Proof*: Suppose $\mathbf{u} \in V \cap W$. Because $\mathbf{u} \in V$ and $\mathbf{u} \in W$, and $V \perp W$, the vector $\mathbf{u}$ must be orthogonal to itself: $\mathbf{u}^T \mathbf{u} = \|\mathbf{u}\|_2^2 = 0$. By the fact that a vector has zero norm only when it is zero, we must have $\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{0}$.

<PitfallAlert title="Common Pitfalls & Transcript Errata">

Students often conflate everyday conversational notions of "perpendicular" or direct sums with the mathematical definition of "orthogonal subspaces." Be sure to establish these two rigorous logical defenses:

1. **"Intersecting only at the zero vector" does NOT mean "orthogonal subspaces"!**
   - The statement "$V \cap W = \{\mathbf{0}\} \implies V \perp W$" is **completely false**!
   - *Counterexample*: In $\mathbb{R}^2$, take the $x$-axis subspace $V = \operatorname{span}([1, 0]^T)$ and the line $y = x$ subspace $W = \operatorname{span}([1, 1]^T)$. Clearly $V \cap W = \{\mathbf{0}\}$, yet the dot product of $[1, 0]^T$ and $[1, 1]^T$ is $1 \neq 0$ (their angle is $45^\circ$). A trivial intersection makes $V+W$ a direct sum. The dot product in this example rules out orthogonality.
2. **A "vertical wall" and "horizontal floor" meeting at $90^\circ$ in 3D are NOT orthogonal subspaces!**
   - In a lecture room, the horizontal floor (the $xy$-plane $z=0$) and a vertical wall (the $yz$-plane $x=0$) meet physically at a right angle.
   - However, viewed as two-dimensional subspaces of $\mathbb{R}^3$, they intersect along an entire line (the $y$-axis)! Their intersection $V \cap W$ is a whole line containing infinitely many nonzero vectors, such as $\mathbf{y} = (0, 1, 0)^T$.
   - That vector $\mathbf{y}$ lies in both the floor and the wall, and its dot product with itself is $1 \neq 0$. Therefore, **two perpendicular planes in $\mathbb{R}^3$ are never orthogonal subspaces**!

</PitfallAlert>

### Orthogonal Complement ($V^\perp$)

In $\mathbb{R}^3$, two perpendicular coordinate axes (such as the $x$-axis and $y$-axis) are indeed orthogonal subspaces, but their dimensions only add up to $1 + 1 = 2 \lt 3$—they do not fill the entire three-dimensional space. To capture the largest possible subspace perpendicular to $V$ so that the two together span the entire ambient space, we introduce the **orthogonal complement**.

<DefinitionCard label="Definition 14.3" title="Orthogonal Complement">

Let $V$ be a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^n$. The set of all vectors in $\mathbb{R}^n$ that are orthogonal to every vector in $V$ is called the **orthogonal complement** of $V$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$, denoted $V^\perp$ (read "$V$ perp"):

$$
V^\perp = \{ \mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n \mid \mathbf{x}^T \mathbf{v} = 0, \; \forall \mathbf{v} \in V \}
$$

</DefinitionCard>

The orthogonal complement possesses an exceptionally clean algebraic and geometric structure:

<TheoremCard label="Theorem 14.2" title="Fundamental Geometric and Dimensional Properties of Orthogonal Complements">

Let $V$ be any subspace of $\mathbb{R}^n$. Then:
1. **Subspace Closure**: $V^\perp$ is always a valid subspace of $\mathbb{R}^n$.
2. **Trivial Intersection**: $V \cap V^\perp = \{\mathbf{0}\}$.
3. **Complementary Dimensions**: $\dim(V) + \dim(V^\perp) = n$.
4. **Orthogonal Direct Sum Decomposition**: $V \oplus V^\perp = \mathbb{R}^n$. Every vector $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ can be **uniquely decomposed** as:
   $$
   \mathbf{x} = \mathbf{v} + \mathbf{w}, \quad \text{where } \mathbf{v} \in V, \; \mathbf{w} \in V^\perp
   $$
5. **Double Orthogonal Complement Involution**: $(V^\perp)^\perp = V$ (taking the orthogonal complement twice returns the exact original subspace).

</TheoremCard>

<ProofCollapse title="Rigorous Proof: Algebraic Proof of the Orthogonal Complement Properties">

**1. Proof that $V^\perp$ is a subspace**:
Take any $\mathbf{x}_1, \mathbf{x}_2 \in V^\perp$ and arbitrary scalars $c, d \in \mathbb{R}$. For every $\mathbf{v} \in V$:

$$
(c\mathbf{x}_1 + d\mathbf{x}_2)^T \mathbf{v} = c(\mathbf{x}_1^T \mathbf{v}) + d(\mathbf{x}_2^T \mathbf{v}) = c(0) + d(0) = 0
$$

Thus the linear combination $c\mathbf{x}_1 + d\mathbf{x}_2 \in V^\perp$. Combined with $\mathbf{0} \in V^\perp$, $V^\perp$ is closed under linear combinations and is therefore a valid subspace.

**2. Proof of the dimension formula $\dim(V) + \dim(V^\perp) = n$**:
Let $\dim(V) = k$, and choose a basis $\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2, \dots, \mathbf{v}_k \in \mathbb{R}^n$ for $V$.
Construct the matrix $M \in \mathbb{R}^{k\times n}$ whose rows are these basis vectors:

$$
M = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{v}_1^T \\ \mathbf{v}_2^T \\ \vdots \\ \mathbf{v}_k^T \end{bmatrix}
$$

By linearity, a vector $\mathbf{x} \in V^\perp$ if and only if $\mathbf{x}$ is orthogonal to every basis vector of $V$:

$$
\mathbf{v}_i^T \mathbf{x} = 0 \quad (\forall i = 1, \dots, k) \iff M\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0} \iff \mathbf{x} \in \operatorname{Null}(M)
$$

Hence $V^\perp$ is precisely the nullspace $\operatorname{Null}(M)$ of the matrix $M$!
Because $\mathbf{v}_1, \dots, \mathbf{v}_k$ are linearly independent, the rank of $M$ is $\operatorname{rank}(M) = k = \dim(V)$.
By the Rank-Nullity Theorem from Module 1:

$$
\dim(V^\perp) = \dim(\operatorname{Null}(M)) = n - \operatorname{rank}(M) = n - k = n - \dim(V)
$$

Rearranging terms gives $\dim(V) + \dim(V^\perp) = n$!

**3. Proof of the double orthogonal complement identity $(V^\perp)^\perp = V$**:
First, for any $\mathbf{v} \in V$ and any $\mathbf{w} \in V^\perp$, we always have $\mathbf{v}^T \mathbf{w} = 0$. By definition of the orthogonal complement of $V^\perp$, this means $\mathbf{v}$ is orthogonal to every vector in $V^\perp$, so $\mathbf{v} \in (V^\perp)^\perp$, which gives:

$$
V \subseteq (V^\perp)^\perp
$$

Next, we compute dimensions: applying the complementary dimension formula twice gives $\dim((V^\perp)^\perp) = n - \dim(V^\perp) = n - (n - \dim(V)) = \dim(V)$.
Since $V$ is a subspace contained inside $(V^\perp)^\perp$ in a finite-dimensional Euclidean space and both subspaces have the exact same dimension, they must be identical: $(V^\perp)^\perp = V$.

**4. Orthogonal direct sum**. The trivial intersection and the dimension formula imply $\dim(V+V^\perp)=n$, so $V+V^\perp=\mathbb{R}^n$. If $\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{v}'+\mathbf{w}'$ are two such decompositions, their difference gives $\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{v}' = \mathbf{w}'-\mathbf{w}\in V\cap V^\perp=\{\mathbf{0}\}$, proving uniqueness.

</ProofCollapse>

## 3. Fundamental Theorem of Linear Algebra, Part II: Orthogonal Complements of the Four Subspaces

In Lecture 10, we established **Part I of the Fundamental Theorem of Linear Algebra**: for any matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$, the dimensions of the row space and nullspace add up to $n$, while the dimensions of the column space and left nullspace add up to $m$. Strang writes $C(A)$ and $N(A)$ for $\operatorname{Col}(A)$ and $\operatorname{Null}(A)$, respectively.
Now we can finally reveal the complete geometric truth about the angles between these four subspaces—**each pair is orthogonally complementary in its own ambient space.**

### 1. Core Algebraic Insight: Why Is the Row Space Perpendicular to the Nullspace?

Examine the row-by-row expansion of the homogeneous system $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$. Let the rows of $A$ be $\mathbf{a}_1^T, \mathbf{a}_2^T, \dots, \mathbf{a}_m^T$:

$$
A\mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{a}_1^T \\ \mathbf{a}_2^T \\ \vdots \\ \mathbf{a}_m^T \end{bmatrix} \mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{a}_1^T \mathbf{x} \\ \mathbf{a}_2^T \mathbf{x} \\ \vdots \\ \mathbf{a}_m^T \mathbf{x} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ \vdots \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}
$$

This equation tells us directly: if $\mathbf{x} \in \operatorname{Null}(A)$, then $\mathbf{x}$ must have **zero dot product with every single row of $A$ simultaneously**:

$$
\mathbf{a}_1^T \mathbf{x} = 0, \quad \mathbf{a}_2^T \mathbf{x} = 0, \quad \dots, \quad \mathbf{a}_m^T \mathbf{x} = 0
$$

Meanwhile, any vector $\mathbf{v}$ in the row space $\operatorname{Row}(A) = \operatorname{Col}(A^T)$ is a linear combination of the rows of $A$, meaning there exists a coefficient vector $\mathbf{y} \in \mathbb{R}^m$ such that $\mathbf{v} = A^T \mathbf{y}$.
Now compute the dot product between the row space vector $\mathbf{v}$ and the nullspace vector $\mathbf{x}$:

$$
\mathbf{v}^T \mathbf{x} = (A^T \mathbf{y})^T \mathbf{x} = (\mathbf{y}^T A) \mathbf{x} = \mathbf{y}^T (A\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{y}^T \mathbf{0} = 0!
$$

Pause and admire that one-line derivation: **$(A^T \mathbf{y})^T \mathbf{x} = \mathbf{y}^T (A\mathbf{x})$**.
By the associativity of matrix multiplication and rules of transposition, $A$ shifts from multiplying $\mathbf{y}$ on the left to multiplying $\mathbf{x}$ on the right. Since $\mathbf{x}$ lies in the nullspace, $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$, causing the entire inner product to collapse to zero! This proves with irrefutable algebraic clarity that **every vector** in the row space is perpendicular to **every vector** in the nullspace.

<TheoremCard label="Theorem 14.3" title="Fundamental Theorem of Linear Algebra, Part II">

Let $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ be any real matrix of rank $\operatorname{rank}(A) = r$. The four fundamental subspaces governed by $A$ form orthogonal complements in the input and output spaces:

1. **Orthogonal Complements in the Input Space $\mathbb{R}^n$**:
   $$
   \operatorname{Null}(A) = (\operatorname{Row}(A))^\perp = (\operatorname{Col}(A^T))^\perp \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n
   $$
   Their orthogonal direct sum fills the entire input space: $\operatorname{Row}(A) \oplus \operatorname{Null}(A) = \mathbb{R}^n$, with dimensions $r + (n-r) = n$.

2. **Orthogonal Complements in the Output Space $\mathbb{R}^m$**:
   $$
   \operatorname{Null}(A^T) = (\operatorname{Col}(A))^\perp \subseteq \mathbb{R}^m
   $$
   Their orthogonal direct sum fills the entire output space: $\operatorname{Col}(A) \oplus \operatorname{Null}(A^T) = \mathbb{R}^m$, with dimensions $r + (m-r) = m$.

</TheoremCard>

<ProofCollapse title="Rigorous Proof: Complete Mutual Inclusion Proof of the Fundamental Theorem Part II">

We complete the rigorous argument in three steps:

**Step 1: Prove $\operatorname{Null}(A) \subseteq (\operatorname{Row}(A))^\perp$**
Let $\mathbf{x} \in \operatorname{Null}(A)$, so $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$.
For any vector $\mathbf{v} \in \operatorname{Row}(A)$, by definition of the row space, $\mathbf{v}$ can be written as a linear combination of the rows of $A$, meaning there exists $\mathbf{y} \in \mathbb{R}^m$ such that $\mathbf{v} = A^T \mathbf{y}$.
Taking the inner product gives:

$$
\mathbf{v}^T \mathbf{x} = (A^T \mathbf{y})^T \mathbf{x} = \mathbf{y}^T (A\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{y}^T \mathbf{0} = 0
$$

This proves that $\mathbf{x}$ is orthogonal to every vector in the row space. By definition of the orthogonal complement, we must have $\mathbf{x} \in (\operatorname{Row}(A))^\perp$. Therefore:

$$
\operatorname{Null}(A) \subseteq (\operatorname{Row}(A))^\perp
$$

**Step 2: Prove Exact Set Equality Using the Dimension Formula**
By Part I of the Fundamental Theorem, the dimension of the row space is $\dim(\operatorname{Row}(A)) = r$.
By the complementary dimension property in Theorem 14.2, the dimension of its orthogonal complement is:

$$
\dim((\operatorname{Row}(A))^\perp) = n - \dim(\operatorname{Row}(A)) = n - r
$$

Meanwhile, Part I also established that the dimension of the nullspace is $\dim(\operatorname{Null}(A)) = n - r$.
Since $\operatorname{Null}(A)$ is a subspace of $(\operatorname{Row}(A))^\perp$ and both finite-dimensional subspaces have the exact same dimension $n - r$, they must coincide completely:

$$
\operatorname{Null}(A) = (\operatorname{Row}(A))^\perp
$$

Taking the orthogonal complement of both sides and applying the double orthogonal complement theorem yields:

$$
(\operatorname{Null}(A))^\perp = ((\operatorname{Row}(A))^\perp)^\perp = \operatorname{Row}(A)
$$

**Step 3: Derive the Output Space Relationship by Duality**
Apply the exact result above to the transpose matrix $A^T \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times m}$ (which also has rank $r$):
The nullspace of $A^T$ is $\operatorname{Null}(A^T)$, and the row space of $A^T$ is the column space of $A$, $\operatorname{Row}(A^T) = \operatorname{Col}(A)$.
Replacing $A$ with $A^T$ in our first conclusion immediately yields:

$$
\operatorname{Null}(A^T) = (\operatorname{Row}(A^T))^\perp = (\operatorname{Col}(A))^\perp \subseteq \mathbb{R}^m
$$

Their orthogonal direct sum fills the output space: $\operatorname{Col}(A) \oplus \operatorname{Null}(A^T) = \mathbb{R}^m$, with dimensions satisfying $r + (m-r) = m$.

</ProofCollapse>

## 4. Orthogonal Direct Sum Decomposition, Minimum-Norm Solutions, and the Prelude to Projection

Part II of the Fundamental Theorem is far more than an aesthetic symmetry—it fundamentally restructures how we view the matrix mapping $\mathbf{x} \mapsto A\mathbf{x}$ and how we solve linear systems.

### 1. Unique Orthogonal Decomposition $\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{x}_r + \mathbf{x}_n$

Because $\mathbb{R}^n = \operatorname{Row}(A) \oplus \operatorname{Null}(A)$, **every input vector $\mathbf{x}$** in the domain can be uniquely split into a row space component and a nullspace component:

$$
\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{x}_r + \mathbf{x}_n, \quad \text{where } \mathbf{x}_r \in \operatorname{Row}(A), \; \mathbf{x}_n \in \operatorname{Null}(A)
$$

By the Pythagorean theorem, since the two components are mutually orthogonal, their lengths strictly satisfy:

$$
\|\mathbf{x}\|_2^2 = \|\mathbf{x}_r\|_2^2 + \|\mathbf{x}_n\|_2^2
$$

Now let the matrix $A$ act on this decomposition:

$$
A\mathbf{x} = A(\mathbf{x}_r + \mathbf{x}_n) = A\mathbf{x}_r + A\mathbf{x}_n = A\mathbf{x}_r + \mathbf{0} = A\mathbf{x}_r
$$

This simple algebraic equation encodes three unshakable geometric facts:

1. **The nullspace purely annihilates information**: The matrix $A$ crushes every vector in the nullspace to $\mathbf{0}$.
2. **The output is determined entirely by the row space component**: The image $A\mathbf{x}$ of any input vector $\mathbf{x}$ is governed solely by its row space component $\mathbf{x}_r$.
3. **An invertible one-to-one bijection between the row space and column space**:
   If we restrict the domain of $A$ strictly to the row space $\operatorname{Row}(A)$, then the linear transformation $A: \operatorname{Row}(A) \to \operatorname{Col}(A)$ is a **dimension-preserving, strictly one-to-one and onto (invertible) bijection**!
   *Proof*: Suppose $\mathbf{x}_r, \mathbf{x}_r' \in \operatorname{Row}(A)$ satisfy $A\mathbf{x}_r = A\mathbf{x}_r'$. Subtracting gives $A(\mathbf{x}_r - \mathbf{x}_r') = \mathbf{0}$, which means the difference vector $(\mathbf{x}_r - \mathbf{x}_r') \in \operatorname{Null}(A)$. Yet because the row space is a subspace, the difference vector also belongs to $\operatorname{Row}(A)$. Since $\operatorname{Row}(A) \cap \operatorname{Null}(A) = \{\mathbf{0}\}$, we must have $\mathbf{x}_r - \mathbf{x}_r' = \mathbf{0} \implies \mathbf{x}_r = \mathbf{x}_r'$. This proves injectivity. For every $\mathbf{b}\in\operatorname{Col}(A)$, some $\mathbf{x}$ satisfies $A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}$. Since $A\mathbf{x}=A\mathbf{x}_r$, the restriction is also surjective.

### 2. The "Minimum-Norm Solution" Among Infinitely Many Solutions

When a consistent system $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ has infinitely many solutions (because free variables exist), the general solution takes the form $\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{x}_{\text{particular}} + \mathbf{x}_n$.
Using the bijection between $\operatorname{Row}(A)$ and $\operatorname{Col}(A)$, there is a unique particular solution $\mathbf{x}_r \in \operatorname{Row}(A)$ such that $A\mathbf{x}_r = \mathbf{b}$, so every solution can be written as:

$$
\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{x}_r + \mathbf{x}_n
$$

Here, the row space component $\mathbf{x}_r$ is **uniquely fixed** across all solutions, while only the nullspace component $\mathbf{x}_n$ varies freely over $\operatorname{Null}(A)$.
By the Pythagorean theorem:

$$
\|\mathbf{x}\|_2^2 = \|\mathbf{x}_r\|_2^2 + \|\mathbf{x}_n\|_2^2 \ge \|\mathbf{x}_r\|_2^2
$$

Equality holds if and only if $\mathbf{x}_n = \mathbf{0}$!
This means that among the infinitely many solutions satisfying $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$, **the unique solution $\mathbf{x}_r$ that lies entirely inside the row space has the strictly smallest Euclidean norm (shortest length)**!
This shortest solution is of paramount importance in engineering and optimization—it is precisely the solution produced by the **Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse** in Lecture 34: $\mathbf{x}^+ = A^+ \mathbf{b}$.

### 3. Output Space Decomposition and the Redemption of Unsolvable Systems: Prelude to Lecture 15

Similarly, in the output space $\mathbb{R}^m$, we have the orthogonal direct sum decomposition:

$$
\mathbb{R}^m = \operatorname{Col}(A) \oplus \operatorname{Null}(A^T)
$$

Any target vector $\mathbf{b} \in \mathbb{R}^m$ can be uniquely decomposed into a column space projection vector $\mathbf{p}$ and a left nullspace error vector $\mathbf{e}$:

$$
\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{p} + \mathbf{e}, \quad \text{where } \mathbf{p} \in \operatorname{Col}(A), \; \mathbf{e} \in \operatorname{Null}(A^T)
$$

When real-world noisy measurements $\mathbf{b}$ fall outside the column space $\operatorname{Col}(A)$, the system $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ is inconsistent and has no exact solution.
The best we can possibly do is discard the residual error component $\mathbf{e} \in \operatorname{Null}(A^T)$ perpendicular to the column space, and solve $A\hat{\mathbf{x}} = \mathbf{p}$ for the optimal approximation $\hat{\mathbf{x}}$! Because $\mathbf{e} = \mathbf{b} - \mathbf{p} \perp \operatorname{Col}(A)$, this is the exact geometric foundation of **orthogonal projection and least squares**—setting the stage for Lectures 15 and 16!

<DualSolution
  triggerLabel="Show Full Dual Solution (Intuition + Rigorous Proof)"
  intuitionLabel="Geometric Intuition"
  rigorousLabel="Rigorous Proof / Construction"
>
<SolutionIntuition>

The two rows of $A$ are parallel, so the rank is $r = 1$.
- The input space $\mathbb{R}^3$ is partitioned orthogonally into a 1D line (the row space, pointing along $(1, 2, 1)^T$) and a 2D plane through the origin (the nullspace plane $x_1 + 2x_2 + x_3 = 0$).
- This line and plane meet at a strict $90^\circ$ angle at the origin!
- Dropping a perpendicular from $\mathbf{x} = (3, 1, 2)^T$ onto the line gives the row space component $\mathbf{x}_r$, while the remaining displacement $\mathbf{x}_n$ drops straight into the nullspace plane. These two components form the two perpendicular legs of a right triangle whose hypotenuse is $\mathbf{x}$ itself.

</SolutionIntuition>
<SolutionRigorous>

**Step 1: Basis Vectors and Orthogonality Verification**
- Because Row 2 is twice Row 1, $\operatorname{rank}(A) = 1$.
- The row space is $\operatorname{Row}(A) = \operatorname{span}(\mathbf{v}_1)$, with basis vector:
  $$
  \mathbf{v}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}
  $$
- For the nullspace, solve $x_1 + 2x_2 + x_3 = 0$ with free variables $x_2, x_3$:
  - Setting $x_2 = 1, x_3 = 0 \implies x_1 = -2$ gives the special solution $\mathbf{s}_1 = [-2, 1, 0]^T$;
  - Setting $x_2 = 0, x_3 = 1 \implies x_1 = -1$ gives the special solution $\mathbf{s}_2 = [-1, 0, 1]^T$.
- Direct inner product verification:
  $$
  \begin{aligned}
  \mathbf{v}_1^T \mathbf{s}_1 &= 1(-2) + 2(1) + 1(0) = -2 + 2 + 0 = 0 \\
  \mathbf{v}_1^T \mathbf{s}_2 &= 1(-1) + 2(0) + 1(1) = -1 + 0 + 1 = 0
  \end{aligned}
  $$
  The row space basis vector is strictly orthogonal to both nullspace basis vectors, confirming $\operatorname{Row}(A) \perp \operatorname{Null}(A)$.

**Step 2: Computing the Orthogonal Decomposition $\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{x}_r + \mathbf{x}_n$**
Because $\operatorname{Row}(A)$ is spanned by the single nonzero vector $\mathbf{v}_1$, we project $\mathbf{x}$ onto $\mathbf{v}_1$:

$$
\mathbf{x}_r = \frac{\mathbf{v}_1^T \mathbf{x}}{\mathbf{v}_1^T \mathbf{v}_1} \mathbf{v}_1
$$

Substituting the numerical values:
- $\mathbf{v}_1^T \mathbf{x} = 1(3) + 2(1) + 1(2) = 3 + 2 + 2 = 7$;
- $\mathbf{v}_1^T \mathbf{v}_1 = 1^2 + 2^2 + 1^2 = 6$;
- Therefore:
  $$
  \mathbf{x}_r = \frac{7}{6} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 7/6 \\ 14/6 \\ 7/6 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 7/6 \\ 7/3 \\ 7/6 \end{bmatrix}
  $$

The remaining nullspace component is:

$$
\mathbf{x}_n = \mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}_r = \begin{bmatrix} 3 - 7/6 \\ 1 - 14/6 \\ 2 - 7/6 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 11/6 \\ -8/6 \\ 5/6 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{6} \begin{bmatrix} 11 \\ -8 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix}
$$

Checking that $\mathbf{x}_n$ lies in the nullspace:
$1(11/6) + 2(-8/6) + 1(5/6) = \frac{11 - 16 + 5}{6} = 0$, so indeed $\mathbf{x}_n \in \operatorname{Null}(A)$!
Checking orthogonality:
$\mathbf{x}_r^T \mathbf{x}_n = \frac{7}{36} [1(11) + 2(-8) + 1(5)] = \frac{7}{36}[11 - 16 + 5] = 0$.

**Step 3: Pythagorean Norm Verification**
- Squared norm of the original vector: $\|\mathbf{x}\|_2^2 = 3^2 + 1^2 + 2^2 = 9 + 1 + 4 = 14$;
- Squared norm of the row space component: $\|\mathbf{x}_r\|_2^2 = \frac{49}{36}(1 + 4 + 1) = \frac{49 \times 6}{36} = \frac{49}{6}$;
- Squared norm of the nullspace component: $\|\mathbf{x}_n\|_2^2 = \frac{1}{36}(11^2 + (-8)^2 + 5^2) = \frac{1}{36}(121 + 64 + 25) = \frac{210}{36} = \frac{35}{6}$;
- Sum of squared norms of the two components:
  $$
  \|\mathbf{x}_r\|_2^2 + \|\mathbf{x}_n\|_2^2 = \frac{49}{6} + \frac{35}{6} = \frac{84}{6} = 14 = \|\mathbf{x}\|_2^2
  $$
  The Pythagorean theorem closes with exact precision!

"Intersecting only at zero" simply means the two subspaces share no common nonzero direction—algebraically, they form a **direct sum**.

- $V = \operatorname{span}([1, 0]^T)$ (the $x$-axis);

Take the nonzero vector $\mathbf{v} = [1, 0]^T \in V$ and the nonzero vector $\mathbf{w} = [1, 1]^T \in W$:

$$
\mathbf{v}^T \mathbf{w} = 1(1) + 0(1) = 1 \neq 0
$$

Because their inner product is nonzero (the angle between the lines is $45^\circ$), $V$ and $W$ are not orthogonal subspaces. The statement is false.

3. **Verify Double Orthogonal Complement Involution**:

>

Let $S$ be an **arbitrary nonempty subset** of $\mathbb{R}^n$ (note: $S$ could be a finite collection of scattered points, a curved arc, or a shape that does not even pass through the origin—it is NOT assumed to be a subspace!).
Define the orthogonal complement of the subset $S$ as:

S^\perp = \{ \mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n \mid \mathbf{x}^T \mathbf{s} = 0, \; \forall \mathbf{s} \in S \}
$$

Prove the following three propositions:
1. Regardless of how irregular $S$ is, $S^\perp$ is always a valid vector subspace of $\mathbb{R}^n$;
2. Prove that $S^\perp = (\operatorname{span}(S))^\perp$;
3. Prove that $(S^\perp)^\perp = \operatorname{span}(S)$.

<ProgressiveHint
  buttonPrefix="Unlock Hint"

<DualSolution
  triggerLabel="Show Full Dual Solution (Intuition + Rigorous Proof)"
  intuitionLabel="Geometric Intuition"
  rigorousLabel="Rigorous Proof / Construction"
>
<SolutionIntuition>

The orthogonal complement operator $(\cdot)^\perp$ acts as a remarkable "algebraic purifier":
Even if you feed it a completely unstructured scatter of points $S$ that fails closure under addition or scalar multiplication, finding $S^\perp$ amounts to solving a system of homogeneous linear equations $\mathbf{s}^T \mathbf{x} = 0$. And the solution set of homogeneous linear equations automatically forms a flat linear subspace through the origin!
Furthermore, if a vector is perpendicular to a collection of points, linearity forces it to be perpendicular to the entire subspace $\operatorname{span}(S)$ spanned by their linear combinations. Taking the orthogonal complement a second time automatically "straightens and completes" the irregular set $S$ into the smallest subspace containing it: $\operatorname{span}(S)$!

</SolutionIntuition>
<SolutionRigorous>

**1. Proof that $S^\perp$ is a valid subspace**
- **Contains the zero vector**: For every $\mathbf{s} \in S$, we have $\mathbf{0}^T \mathbf{s} = 0$, so $\mathbf{0} \in S^\perp$.
- **Closure under linear combinations**: Let $\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y} \in S^\perp$ and let $c, d \in \mathbb{R}$ be arbitrary scalars. For any $\mathbf{s} \in S$, bilinearity of the dot product gives:
  $$
  (c\mathbf{x} + d\mathbf{y})^T \mathbf{s} = c(\mathbf{x}^T \mathbf{s}) + d(\mathbf{y}^T \mathbf{s}) = c(0) + d(0) = 0
  $$
  Thus $c\mathbf{x} + d\mathbf{y} \in S^\perp$. Since $S^\perp$ is closed under linear combinations, it is a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^n$.

**2. Proof that $S^\perp = (\operatorname{span}(S))^\perp$**
- **Forward inclusion $(\operatorname{span}(S))^\perp \subseteq S^\perp$**:
  Because $S \subseteq \operatorname{span}(S)$, any vector $\mathbf{x} \in (\operatorname{span}(S))^\perp$ is orthogonal to all vectors in $\operatorname{span}(S)$, and in particular is orthogonal to every vector in the subset $S$. Hence $\mathbf{x} \in S^\perp$.
- **Reverse inclusion $S^\perp \subseteq (\operatorname{span}(S))^\perp$**:
  Let $\mathbf{x} \in S^\perp$. Any vector $\mathbf{v} \in \operatorname{span}(S)$ is by definition a finite linear combination of elements of $S$:
  $$
  \mathbf{v} = \sum_{i=1}^{k} c_i \mathbf{s}_i \quad (\mathbf{s}_i \in S, \; c_i \in \mathbb{R})
  $$
  Computing the inner product gives:
  $$
  \mathbf{x}^T \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{x}^T \left(\sum_{i=1}^{k} c_i \mathbf{s}_i\right) = \sum_{i=1}^{k} c_i (\mathbf{x}^T \mathbf{s}_i) = \sum_{i=1}^{k} c_i (0) = 0
  $$
  Thus $\mathbf{x}$ is orthogonal to every vector in $\operatorname{span}(S)$, meaning $\mathbf{x} \in (\operatorname{span}(S))^\perp$.
- Combining both inclusions establishes $S^\perp = (\operatorname{span}(S))^\perp$.

**3. Proof that $(S^\perp)^\perp = \operatorname{span}(S)$**
Taking the orthogonal complement of both sides of the identity from Part 2 gives:

$$
(S^\perp)^\perp = ((\operatorname{span}(S))^\perp)^\perp
$$

Notice that $V = \operatorname{span}(S)$ is already a valid vector subspace of the finite-dimensional space $\mathbb{R}^n$. By Property 5 of Theorem 14.2 (the double orthogonal complement of a subspace equals itself):

$$
(V^\perp)^\perp = V \implies ((\operatorname{span}(S))^\perp)^\perp = \operatorname{span}(S)
$$

Therefore, we immediately conclude:

$$
(S^\perp)^\perp = \operatorname{span}(S)
$$

</SolutionRigorous>

<DualSolution
  triggerLabel="Show Full Dual Solution (Intuition + Rigorous Proof)"
  intuitionLabel="Geometric Intuition"
  rigorousLabel="Rigorous Proof / Construction"
>
<SolutionIntuition>

This problem showcases the sheer power of orthogonal complement theory in practical solvability testing!
The traditional approach performs Gaussian elimination on the $3\times 3$ augmented matrix to see whether the bottom row produces a contradiction $0 = c$ with $c\neq 0$.
However, Part II of the Fundamental Theorem tells us: **whether $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ is solvable depends entirely on whether $\mathbf{b}$ is perpendicular to every constraint direction that gets zeroed out by row combinations—namely, the left nullspace!**
The left nullspace $\operatorname{Null}(A^T)$ acts as the system's "quality inspector." For this one-dimensional left nullspace, choose any nonzero basis vector $\mathbf{y}$ and take its dot product with $\mathbf{b}$—if the result is nonzero, the data fails inspection immediately and the system is guaranteed to be inconsistent!

</SolutionIntuition>
<SolutionRigorous>

**Part 1: Theoretical Proof of the Fredholm Alternative**
- By definition of matrix-vector multiplication, the linear system $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ has a solution if and only if the target vector $\mathbf{b}$ lies in the column space of $A$:
  $$
  A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b} \text{ is solvable} \iff \mathbf{b} \in \operatorname{Col}(A)
  $$
- By Part II of the Fundamental Theorem of Linear Algebra, in the output space $\mathbb{R}^m$, the column space is the orthogonal complement of the left nullspace:
  $$
  \operatorname{Col}(A) = ((\operatorname{Col}(A))^\perp)^\perp = (\operatorname{Null}(A^T))^\perp
  $$
- By definition of the orthogonal complement, a vector belongs to $(\operatorname{Null}(A^T))^\perp$ if and only if it is orthogonal to every vector in $\operatorname{Null}(A^T)$:
  $$
  \mathbf{b} \in (\operatorname{Null}(A^T))^\perp \iff \forall \mathbf{y} \in \operatorname{Null}(A^T), \; \mathbf{y}^T \mathbf{b} = 0
  $$
- Chaining these equivalences together proves the Fredholm Alternative:
  $$
  A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b} \text{ has a solution} \iff \forall \mathbf{y} \in \operatorname{Null}(A^T), \; \mathbf{y}^T \mathbf{b} = 0
  $$

**Part 2: Concrete Elimination-Free Solvability Test**
For the given matrix $A$, its transpose is:

$$
A^T = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 2 & 3 \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{2\times 3}
$$

Solve the homogeneous system $A^T \mathbf{y} = \mathbf{0}$:

$$
\begin{cases}
y_1 + y_2 + 2y_3 = 0 \\
y_1 + 2y_2 + 3y_3 = 0
\end{cases}
$$

Subtracting the first equation from the second gives $y_2 + y_3 = 0 \implies y_2 = -y_3$.
Substituting back into the first equation gives $y_1 = -y_2 - 2y_3 = y_3 - 2y_3 = -y_3$.
Setting the free variable $y_3 = 1$ yields a basis vector for the left nullspace:

$$
\mathbf{y} = \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \quad \left(\text{or its negation } \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}\right)
$$

Now take $\mathbf{y} = [1, 1, -1]^T \in \operatorname{Null}(A^T)$ and compute its dot product directly with the target vector $\mathbf{b} = [1, 2, 4]^T$:

$$
\mathbf{y}^T \mathbf{b} = 1(1) + 1(2) + (-1)(4) = 1 + 2 - 4 = -1 \neq 0
$$

The inner product is strictly nonzero!
By the necessary and sufficient criterion proved in Part 1, since $\mathbf{y}^T \mathbf{b} \neq 0$, the target vector $\mathbf{b}$ does not belong to the column space $\operatorname{Col}(A)$.
Therefore, **the linear system $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ has NO solution (it is an inconsistent system)**!

*(Comparison with Elimination)*: If we inspect the rows of $A$, Row 1 plus Row 2 equals Row 3, yet on the right-hand side $b_1 + b_2 = 1 + 2 = 3 \neq 4 = b_3$, so row elimination would produce the contradiction $0 = 1$ in the third row. The left-nullspace inner product test reaches the exact same conclusion in a single dot product, demonstrating the power of geometric duality.
