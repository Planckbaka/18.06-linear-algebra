In real-world engineering measurements and statistical regression, we almost inevitably face an **overdetermined system** $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ contaminated by noise and massive observations. Here, the matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ is typically a "tall and skinny" rectangular matrix (more equations $m$ than unknowns $n$, so $m \gt n$). When the target vector $\mathbf{b}$ does not lie inside the column space $\operatorname{Col}(A)$, the equation $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ has no exact solution at all.

Science and engineering cannot simply give up because "no exact solution exists"—within the subspace $\operatorname{Col}(A)$ spanned by the columns of $A$, we must find the best approximating vector $\mathbf{p} = A\hat{\mathbf{x}}$ that is closest to $\mathbf{b}$ in Euclidean distance. Geometrically, the closest point is precisely the foot of the perpendicular, and the perpendicular segment dropping from the target point onto the subspace is the orthogonal error (or residual). In this lecture, Professor Gilbert Strang leads us on an elegant ascent from **projection onto a 1D line (a rank-one projection matrix)** to **projection onto a column space of a full-column-rank matrix via $P = A(A^TA)^{-1}A^T$**, uncovering the algebraic soul of projection operators: symmetry, idempotence, and orthogonal complementary decomposition.

---

## 1. Dual Geometry of Projection: From a 1D Line to a Subspace

The essence of orthogonal projection is to replace an unsolvable right-hand side $\mathbf{b}$ with the optimal linear combination $\mathbf{p} = A\hat{\mathbf{x}}$ inside the column space $\operatorname{Col}(A)$, forcing the residual error vector $\mathbf{e} = \mathbf{b} - \mathbf{p}$ to be orthogonal to the entire subspace.

<LineAndSubspaceProjectionDiagram caption="Left (1D World): Projecting b onto a line spanned by a yields p = x̂a via a rank-1 outer-over-inner product matrix. Right (Subspace World): Projecting b onto the plane Col(A) yields p = Ax̂, while the perpendicular error vector e = b - Ax̂ drops directly into the left nullspace Null(A^T)." />

As shown in the diagram above:
- **Left (1D World)**: The target vector $\mathbf{b}$ drops onto a line spanned by a nonzero vector $\mathbf{a}$. The foot of the perpendicular is $\mathbf{p} = \hat{x}\mathbf{a}$, and the projection matrix is a rank-one matrix formed by the ratio of an outer product to an inner product.
- **Right (High-Dimensional World)**: The target vector $\mathbf{b}$ hovers above the subspace plane $\operatorname{Col}(A)$ spanned by multiple columns. The foot of the perpendicular in the plane is $\mathbf{p} = A\hat{\mathbf{x}}$, while the perpendicular drop $\mathbf{e} = \mathbf{b} - A\hat{\mathbf{x}}$ lands squarely inside the **left nullspace $\operatorname{Null}(A^T)$** of $A$!

## 2. Warm-Up: Projection onto a 1D Line (Rank-One Projection)

### 2.1 Orthogonal Derivation of the Scalar Coefficient $\hat{x}$

Let $\mathbf{a} \in \mathbb{R}^m \setminus \{\mathbf{0}\}$ be a direction vector, and let $L = \operatorname{span}\{\mathbf{a}\}$ denote the one-dimensional subspace (a line through the origin) that it spans. Given any target vector $\mathbf{b} \in \mathbb{R}^m$, we seek a scalar multiple $\mathbf{p} = \hat{x}\mathbf{a}$ along the line $L$ (where $\hat{x} \in \mathbb{R}$ is the unknown scalar coefficient) such that the error vector

$$
\mathbf{e} = \mathbf{b} - \mathbf{p} = \mathbf{b} - \hat{x}\mathbf{a}
$$

is strictly orthogonal (perpendicular) to the basis direction $\mathbf{a}$ of the line.

By the algebraic definition of orthogonality, the dot product of the two vectors must vanish:

$$
\mathbf{a}^T \mathbf{e} = 0 \iff \mathbf{a}^T(\mathbf{b} - \hat{x}\mathbf{a}) = 0
$$

Expanding the inner product:

$$
\mathbf{a}^T \mathbf{b} - \hat{x} \mathbf{a}^T \mathbf{a} = 0 \implies \hat{x} \mathbf{a}^T \mathbf{a} = \mathbf{a}^T \mathbf{b}
$$

Because $\mathbf{a} \neq \mathbf{0}$, its self dot product (squared Euclidean length) $\mathbf{a}^T\mathbf{a} = \|\mathbf{a}\|_2^2 \gt 0$ is strictly positive. Dividing both sides by $\mathbf{a}^T\mathbf{a}$ yields the unique optimal projection coefficient:

$$
\hat{x} = \frac{\mathbf{a}^T \mathbf{b}}{\mathbf{a}^T \mathbf{a}}
$$

### 2.2 Birth of the Rank-One Projection Matrix

Once we have the scalar coefficient $\hat{x}$, the projection vector $\mathbf{p}$ is written as:

$$
\mathbf{p} = \hat{x}\mathbf{a} = \mathbf{a} \left(\frac{\mathbf{a}^T \mathbf{b}}{\mathbf{a}^T \mathbf{a}}\right)
$$

Because the denominator $\mathbf{a}^T\mathbf{a}$ is a pure scalar, scalars can move freely in matrix multiplication. Regrouping the vector and the numerator by associativity gives:

$$
\mathbf{p} = \frac{\mathbf{a} (\mathbf{a}^T \mathbf{b})}{\mathbf{a}^T \mathbf{a}} = \left( \frac{\mathbf{a}\mathbf{a}^T}{\mathbf{a}^T \mathbf{a}} \right) \mathbf{b} = P \mathbf{b}
$$

To our delight, we discover that the mapping from any input vector $\mathbf{b}$ to its projection $\mathbf{p}$ on the line is governed entirely by a **fixed $m\times m$ square matrix**! This matrix is called the **projection matrix onto a line**:

$$
P = \frac{\mathbf{a}\mathbf{a}^T}{\mathbf{a}^T \mathbf{a}} \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
$$

Look closely at the structural contrast between denominator and numerator:
- The denominator $\mathbf{a}^T\mathbf{a}$ is "row times column"—a real **scalar (inner product)**;
- The numerator $\mathbf{a}\mathbf{a}^T$ is "column times row"—a full $m\times m$ **rank-one matrix (outer product)**!

### 2.3 Concrete Integer Example & Geometric Invariant Check

Let us verify this by hand in $\mathbb{R}^3$. Take the direction vector $\mathbf{a} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}$ and target vector $\mathbf{b} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 3 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$.

**Step 1: Compute the inner-product denominator**

$$
\mathbf{a}^T\mathbf{a} = 1^2 + 2^2 + 2^2 = 9
$$

**Step 2: Compute the outer-product numerator and projection matrix $P$**

$$
\mathbf{a}\mathbf{a}^T = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 & 4 \\ 2 & 4 & 4 \end{bmatrix}
$$

$$
P = \frac{1}{9}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 & 4 \\ 2 & 4 & 4 \end{bmatrix}
$$

**Step 3: Compute the projection vector $\mathbf{p}$ and error vector $\mathbf{e}$**

$$
\hat{x} = \frac{\mathbf{a}^T\mathbf{b}}{\mathbf{a}^T\mathbf{a}} = \frac{1(3) + 2(3) + 2(0)}{9} = \frac{9}{9} = 1
$$

$$
\mathbf{p} = \hat{x}\mathbf{a} = 1 \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}
$$

$$
\mathbf{e} = \mathbf{b} - \mathbf{p} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 3 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix}
$$

**Step 4: Verify orthogonality and the Pythagorean theorem**

$$
\mathbf{a}^T\mathbf{e} = 1(2) + 2(1) + 2(-2) = 2 + 2 - 4 = 0 \quad (\text{strictly orthogonal!})
$$

$$
\|\mathbf{b}\|_2^2 = 3^2 + 3^2 + 0^2 = 18
$$

$$
\|\mathbf{p}\|_2^2 = 1^2 + 2^2 + 2^2 = 9, \quad \|\mathbf{e}\|_2^2 = 2^2 + 1^2 + (-2)^2 = 9
$$

$$
\|\mathbf{p}\|_2^2 + \|\mathbf{e}\|_2^2 = 9 + 9 = 18 = \|\mathbf{b}\|_2^2 \quad (\text{Pythagorean theorem holds exactly!})
$$

<DefinitionCard
  label="Property 15.1"
  title="Subspace Invariants of Rank-One Projection"
>

For the rank-one projection matrix $P = \frac{\mathbf{a}\mathbf{a}^T}{\mathbf{a}^T\mathbf{a}}$ constructed from a nonzero direction $\mathbf{a} \in \mathbb{R}^m \setminus \{\mathbf{0}\}$:

1. **Column Space and Rank**: $\operatorname{Col}(P) = \operatorname{span}\{\mathbf{a}\}$, and $\operatorname{rank}(P) = 1$;
2. **Nullspace and Orthogonal Complement**: $\operatorname{Null}(P) = \mathbf{a}^\perp = \{\mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{R}^m \mid \mathbf{a}^T\mathbf{v} = 0\}$, which has dimension $m - 1$;
3. **Extreme Transformation Actions**:
   - If a vector already lies on the line ($\mathbf{b} = c\mathbf{a}$), projection leaves it unchanged: $P\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{b}$;
   - If a vector is perpendicular to the line ($\mathbf{b} \perp \mathbf{a}$), projection annihilates it to zero: $P\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{0}$.

</DefinitionCard>

## 3. Ascending to Higher Dimensions: Projection onto $\operatorname{Col}(A)$

### 3.1 Geometric Setup and Birth of the Normal Equations

Now let us lift our gaze from a 1D line to a general multi-dimensional subspace.

Suppose $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ has $n$ linearly independent columns $\mathbf{a}_1, \dots, \mathbf{a}_n$ (that is, $A$ has **full column rank**, $\operatorname{rank}(A) = n$ with $m \ge n$).

These columns of $A$ span an $n$-dimensional subspace—the column space $\operatorname{Col}(A) \subseteq \mathbb{R}^m$. Given a target vector $\mathbf{b} \in \mathbb{R}^m$, if $\mathbf{b} \notin \operatorname{Col}(A)$, then the system $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ has no hope of an exact solution.

We want to find the point $\mathbf{p}$ inside $\operatorname{Col}(A)$ that is closest to $\mathbf{b}$. Because $\mathbf{p} \in \operatorname{Col}(A)$, it must be some linear combination of the columns of $A$. Hence there exists a unique coefficient vector $\hat{\mathbf{x}} = (\hat{x}_1, \dots, \hat{x}_n)^T \in \mathbb{R}^n$ such that:

$$
\mathbf{p} = A\hat{\mathbf{x}} = \hat{x}_1 \mathbf{a}_1 + \hat{x}_2 \mathbf{a}_2 + \cdots + \hat{x}_n \mathbf{a}_n
$$

Define the error vector:

$$
\mathbf{e} = \mathbf{b} - \mathbf{p} = \mathbf{b} - A\hat{\mathbf{x}}
$$

**Core Geometric Criterion**: The foot $\mathbf{p}$ is closest to $\mathbf{b}$ if and only if the error vector $\mathbf{e}$ is perpendicular to the entire subspace $\operatorname{Col}(A)$! In other words, $\mathbf{e}$ must be simultaneously orthogonal to **every single basis column** $\mathbf{a}_1, \dots, \mathbf{a}_n$ spanning the subspace:

$$
\begin{cases}
\mathbf{a}_1^T (\mathbf{b} - A\hat{\mathbf{x}}) = 0 \\
\mathbf{a}_2^T (\mathbf{b} - A\hat{\mathbf{x}}) = 0 \\
\quad \vdots \\
\mathbf{a}_n^T (\mathbf{b} - A\hat{\mathbf{x}}) = 0
\end{cases}
$$

Stacking these $n$ scalar equations vertically places the transposed columns as rows of $A^T$ on the left:

$$
\begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{a}_1^T \\ \mathbf{a}_2^T \\ \vdots \\ \mathbf{a}_n^T \end{bmatrix} (\mathbf{b} - A\hat{\mathbf{x}}) = \mathbf{0} \iff A^T(\mathbf{b} - A\hat{\mathbf{x}}) = \mathbf{0}
$$

Expanding and rearranging brings us to the most famous system of equations in all of applied linear algebra—the **Normal Equations**:

$$
A^T A \hat{\mathbf{x}} = A^T \mathbf{b}
$$

<TheoremCard
  label="Theorem 15.1"
  title="Closest Point and the Left Nullspace"
>

Let $A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$, $\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^m$, and $\hat{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^n$. The point $\mathbf{p}=A\hat{\mathbf{x}}$ is the unique closest point to $\mathbf{b}$ in $\operatorname{Col}(A)$ if and only if its residual $\mathbf{e}=\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{p}$ satisfies

$$
\mathbf{e} \in \operatorname{Col}(A)^\perp = \operatorname{Null}(A^T)
$$

This is a direct triumph of the **Fundamental Theorem of Linear Algebra, Part II (Orthogonal Complements)** established in Lecture 14: the left nullspace $\operatorname{Null}(A^T)$ is precisely the orthogonal complement of the column space $\operatorname{Col}(A)$ inside the output space $\mathbb{R}^m$!

</TheoremCard>

<ProofCollapse title="Why the orthogonal projection is the unique closest point">

By the orthogonal direct sum from Lecture 14, there is a unique decomposition $\mathbf{b}=\mathbf{p}_*+\mathbf{e}_*$ with $\mathbf{p}_*\in\operatorname{Col}(A)$ and $\mathbf{e}_*\in\operatorname{Null}(A^T)$. For every $\mathbf{y}\in\operatorname{Col}(A)$, the difference $\mathbf{p}_*-\mathbf{y}$ is orthogonal to $\mathbf{e}_*$. Therefore

$$
\|\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{y}\|_2^2=\|\mathbf{e}_*\|_2^2+\|\mathbf{p}_*-\mathbf{y}\|_2^2.
$$

Equality with the minimum $\|\mathbf{e}_*\|_2^2$ holds exactly when $\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{p}_*$. The uniqueness of the decomposition also gives $\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{p}\in\operatorname{Null}(A^T)$ exactly when $\mathbf{p}=\mathbf{p}_*$.

</ProofCollapse>

### 3.2 Closed-Form Subspace Projection Matrix $P = A(A^TA)^{-1}A^T$

When $A$ has full column rank ($\operatorname{rank}(A) = n$), the symmetric square matrix $A^TA \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$ is guaranteed to be **invertible** (we will prove this algebraic closure in full detail in Lecture 16: if $A^TA\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$, then $\mathbf{x}^TA^TA\mathbf{x} = \|A\mathbf{x}\|_2^2 = 0 \implies A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}$; since the columns of $A$ are independent, $\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}$, so the nullspace contains only the zero vector and the square matrix $A^TA$ is invertible!).

Multiplying both sides of the normal equations on the left by $(A^TA)^{-1}$ solves for the unique coordinate vector:

$$
\hat{\mathbf{x}} = (A^T A)^{-1} A^T \mathbf{b}
$$

Substituting $\hat{\mathbf{x}}$ back into the projection formula $\mathbf{p} = A\hat{\mathbf{x}}$ gives:

$$
\mathbf{p} = A \hat{\mathbf{x}} = A (A^T A)^{-1} A^T \mathbf{b} = P \mathbf{b}
$$

Thus we have captured the **general subspace projection matrix onto $\operatorname{Col}(A)$** for any full-column-rank matrix $A$:

$$
P = A (A^T A)^{-1} A^T \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
$$

For dependent columns, the projected point $\mathbf{p}$ is still unique, while its coefficient vector is not. Use a basis of $\operatorname{Col}(A)$ in the formula above, or write $P=AA^+$ using the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse. If $A=0$, the projection sends every vector to $\mathbf{0}$.

#### Seamless Unification of the 1D and Subspace Formulas
When $n = 1$, full column rank means that $A$ consists of a single nonzero column $\mathbf{a}$. Then:
- $A^TA$ is the $1\times 1$ matrix with entry $\mathbf{a}^T\mathbf{a}$;
- Matrix inversion $(A^TA)^{-1}$ reduces to the scalar reciprocal $\frac{1}{\mathbf{a}^T\mathbf{a}}$;
- $P = A(A^TA)^{-1}A^T = \mathbf{a} \left(\frac{1}{\mathbf{a}^T\mathbf{a}}\right) \mathbf{a}^T = \frac{\mathbf{a}\mathbf{a}^T}{\mathbf{a}^T\mathbf{a}}$.

The 1D line projection formula is simply the $n = 1$ special case of the general subspace projection formula!

## 4. Algebraic Soul of Projection Matrices: Symmetry, Idempotence & Complementary Split

An orthogonal projection matrix $P$ is far from an ordinary matrix—it possesses an extraordinarily clean algebraic structure.

<ProjectionComplementarySplitDiagram caption="Orthogonal complementary split of R^m: P projects b onto p = Pb in Col(A), while the complementary projection I - P projects b onto the residual e = (I - P)b in Null(A^T) = Col(A)^⊥, satisfying ||b||^2 = ||Pb||^2 + ||(I - P)b||^2." />

<TheoremCard
  label="Theorem 15.2"
  title="Four Fundamental Algebraic Properties of Orthogonal Projections"
>

Let $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ have full column rank. Its orthogonal projection matrix $P = A(A^TA)^{-1}A^T \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times m}$ always satisfies the following four fundamental properties:

1. **Symmetry**: $P^T = P$;
2. **Idempotence**: $P^2 = P$;
3. **Complementary Projection**: The matrix $I_m - P$ is the orthogonal projection matrix onto the orthogonal complement $\operatorname{Col}(A)^\perp = \operatorname{Null}(A^T)$, satisfying $(I - P)^T = I - P$ and $(I - P)^2 = I - P$;
4. **Orthogonal Decoupling & Pythagorean Conservation**: $P(I - P) = (I - P)P = 0$. Every $\mathbf{b} \in \mathbb{R}^m$ admits the unique orthogonal decomposition $\mathbf{b} = P\mathbf{b} + (I - P)\mathbf{b}$ with norm conservation $\|\mathbf{b}\|_2^2 = \|P\mathbf{b}\|_2^2 + \|(I - P)\mathbf{b}\|_2^2$.

</TheoremCard>

<ProofCollapse title="Rigorous Proof: Complete Derivation of the Four Algebraic Properties">

**1. Proof of Symmetry**:

Using the reverse-order transpose rule $(BCD)^T = D^T C^T B^T$:

$$
P^T = \left( A (A^TA)^{-1} A^T \right)^T = (A^T)^T \left( (A^TA)^{-1} \right)^T A^T = A \left( (A^TA)^T \right)^{-1} A^T
$$

Because $(A^TA)^T = A^T(A^T)^T = A^TA$ is symmetric, its inverse is also symmetric: $\left( (A^TA)^T \right)^{-1} = (A^TA)^{-1}$.
Substituting back gives:

$$
P^T = A(A^TA)^{-1}A^T = P
$$

**2. Proof of Idempotence**:

Applying the matrix multiplication twice in succession:

$$
P^2 = \left[ A (A^TA)^{-1} A^T \right] \left[ A (A^TA)^{-1} A^T \right] = A (A^TA)^{-1} \underbrace{(A^TA)(A^TA)^{-1}}_{I_n} A^T
$$

The middle factors $(A^TA)(A^TA)^{-1} = I_n$ cancel cleanly to the $n\times n$ identity matrix, leaving:

$$
P^2 = A (A^TA)^{-1} I_n A^T = A(A^TA)^{-1} A^T = P
$$

Geometric intuition: Once you have projected a vector onto the subspace plane (landing at $\mathbf{p} = P\mathbf{b}$), projecting it a second time does nothing because it already lies inside the plane: $P\mathbf{p} = P(P\mathbf{b}) = P\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{p}$.

**3. Proof of Complementary Projection**:

Checking symmetry:

$$
(I - P)^T = I^T - P^T = I - P
$$

Checking idempotence:

$$
(I - P)^2 = I - 2P + P^2
$$

Since $P^2 = P$, this simplifies to $I - 2P + P = I - P$. By Theorem 15.1, every $(I_m-P)\mathbf{b}=\mathbf{b}-P\mathbf{b}$ lies in $\operatorname{Null}(A^T)$. Conversely, $I_m-P$ fixes every vector in $\operatorname{Null}(A^T)$ because $P$ sends those vectors to $\mathbf{0}$.

**4. Proof of Orthogonal Decoupling & Pythagorean Energy Conservation**:

Computing the product of the two projectors:

$$
P(I - P) = P - P^2 = P - P = 0, \quad (I - P)P = P - P^2 = 0
$$

The inner product of the two projected components $\mathbf{p} = P\mathbf{b}$ and $\mathbf{e} = (I - P)\mathbf{b}$ is:

$$
\mathbf{p}^T \mathbf{e} = (P\mathbf{b})^T ((I - P)\mathbf{b}) = \mathbf{b}^T P^T (I - P)\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{b}^T (P - P^2)\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{b}^T 0 \mathbf{b} = 0
$$

Because their inner product vanishes, the Pythagorean theorem follows immediately:

$$
\|\mathbf{b}\|_2^2 = \|\mathbf{p} + \mathbf{e}\|_2^2 = \|\mathbf{p}\|_2^2 + 2\mathbf{p}^T\mathbf{e} + \|\mathbf{e}\|_2^2 = \|\mathbf{p}\|_2^2 + \|\mathbf{e}\|_2^2
$$

</ProofCollapse>

<DefinitionCard
  label="Definition 15.1"
  title="Algebraic Characterization of Orthogonal Projection Operators"
>

A real square matrix $P \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times m}$ is an **orthogonal projection matrix** on Euclidean space $\mathbb{R}^m$ if and only if it is simultaneously **idempotent** and **symmetric**:

$$
P^2 = P \quad \text{and} \quad P^T = P
$$

If a square matrix satisfies idempotence ($P^2 = P$) but fails symmetry ($P^T \neq P$), it represents an **oblique projection** that casts shadows onto a subspace along a non-orthogonal angle; for some inputs, its residual fails to be perpendicular to the target subspace.

</DefinitionCard>

<ProofCollapse title="Proof of the symmetric-idempotent criterion">

For each $\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^m$, idempotence puts $(I_m-P)\mathbf{b}$ in $\operatorname{Null}(P)$, while $P\mathbf{b}\in\operatorname{Col}(P)$. If $P^T=P$, then $(P\mathbf{u})^T\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{u}^TP\mathbf{v}=0$ for every $\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^m$ and $\mathbf{v}\in\operatorname{Null}(P)$. This is therefore the orthogonal decomposition for $\operatorname{Col}(P)$. Conversely, in an orthonormal basis formed from bases of the target subspace and its orthogonal complement, the orthogonal projector has diagonal matrix $\operatorname{diag}(I_r,0)$, where $r$ is the target dimension. It stays symmetric and idempotent after the orthogonal change of basis.

</ProofCollapse>

## 5. Common Pitfalls & Transcript Errata

<PitfallAlert title="Common Pitfalls & Transcript Errata: The Fatal Algebraic Illusion of Splitting (AᵀA)⁻¹ into A⁻¹(Aᵀ)⁻¹ to Get P = I!">

**Classic Student Blunder**:
"Since $(BC)^{-1} = C^{-1}B^{-1}$, I can expand $P = A(A^TA)^{-1}A^T$ as:

$$
P = A A^{-1} (A^T)^{-1} A^T = (A A^{-1}) ((A^T)^{-1} A^T) = I \cdot I = I
$$

Therefore, every projection matrix is actually the identity matrix $I$!"

**Rigorous Correction**:
This simplification is a catastrophic algebraic blunder!

1. **$A$ Is Not Square**: Our premise is an overdetermined system $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ with $m \gt n$ (a tall, skinny rectangular matrix). **A rectangular matrix never has a two-sided inverse $A^{-1}$!**
2. **Prerequisite of the Shoe-Sock Rule**: The identity $(BC)^{-1} = C^{-1}B^{-1}$ holds if and only if **both factors $B$ and $C$ are individually invertible square matrices of the same size!** Since $A$ and $A^T$ are not even square, pulling $(A^TA)^{-1}$ apart into $A^{-1}(A^T)^{-1}$ is illegal.
3. **When Does the Single Exception Occur?**
   Only when $m = n$ and $A$ is a full-rank invertible square matrix does $A^{-1}$ actually exist. In that case, the $n$ columns of $A$ already span the entire space $\mathbb{R}^n$ ($\operatorname{Col}(A) = \mathbb{R}^n$). Every vector $\mathbf{b} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ already lies inside $\operatorname{Col}(A)$, so projecting it onto the whole space leaves it untouched! Thus, under the full-column-rank assumption, $P=I_m$ exactly when $m=n$.

</PitfallAlert>

<PitfallAlert title="Common Pitfalls & Transcript Errata: Outer vs. Inner Products & Interpreting Zero Residual">

1. **Never Swap Numerator and Denominator**: In 1D projection, $P = \frac{\mathbf{a}\mathbf{a}^T}{\mathbf{a}^T\mathbf{a}}$. The denominator is the inner-product scalar $\mathbf{a}^T\mathbf{a}$, while the numerator is the outer-product matrix $\mathbf{a}\mathbf{a}^T$. Reversing the transpose order in your head instantly breaks matrix dimensions.
2. **Zero Residual Does Not Mean Zero Vector**: Finding $(I - P)\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{0}$ never implies $\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{0}$! It simply tells you that $\mathbf{b}$ already belongs entirely to $\operatorname{Col}(A)$, so its orthogonal complement component (the error) is zero and $P\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{b}$.

</PitfallAlert>

<SolutionIntuition>

Projecting a vector onto $V_1$ and adding its projection onto $V_2$ double-counts any shared direction between $V_1$ and $V_2$! </SolutionIntuition>
<SolutionRigorous>

Let $P = P_1 + P_2$.

$$
P^T = (P_1 + P_2)^T = P_1^T + P_2^T = P_1 + P_2 = P
$$

Symmetry always holds.

Next check idempotence:

$$
P^2 = (P_1 + P_2)^2 = P_1^2 + P_1 P_2 + P_2 P_1 + P_2^2 = P_1 + P_2 + (P_1 P_2 + P_2 P_1)

$$
P_1 P_2 + P_2 P_1 = 0

More generally, if $P^2=P$, then every $\mathbf{v}\in V_1$ satisfies $\mathbf{v}^T(P_1P_2+P_2P_1)\mathbf{v}=2\mathbf{v}^TP_2\mathbf{v}=2\|P_2\mathbf{v}\|_2^2=0$. Thus $V_1\subseteq\operatorname{Null}(P_2)=V_2^\perp$. Conversely, if $V_1\perp V_2$, both cross products vanish and $P$ is the orthogonal projection onto $V_1\oplus V_2$.

<DualSolution
  triggerLabel="Show Full Dual Solution (Intuition + Rigorous Proof)"
  intuitionLabel="Geometric Intuition"
  rigorousLabel="Rigorous Proof / Construction"
>
<SolutionIntuition>

Projection flattens 3D objects onto a 2D sheet of paper (a dimensionality reduction). During this flattening, the axis perpendicular to the sheet (the orthogonal complement subspace) is crushed all the way down to the origin $\mathbf{0}$. Once nonzero vectors are crushed to $\mathbf{0}$, information is permanently lost and the operation can never be reversed—hence $P$ cannot be invertible! The only exception where no crushing occurs is the identity projection $P = I_m$ onto the whole space.

</SolutionIntuition>
<SolutionRigorous>

**Method 1 (Nullspace Argument)**:

By Theorem 15.2, $P(I_m - P) = 0$.

Because $P \neq I_m$, the matrix $I_m - P \neq 0$, which means there exists at least one nonzero vector $\mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{R}^m$ such that $\mathbf{u} = (I_m - P)\mathbf{v} \neq \mathbf{0}$.

Multiplying both sides on the left by $P$:

$$
P\mathbf{u} = P(I_m - P)\mathbf{v} = 0 \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}
$$

Since there exists a nonzero vector $\mathbf{u} \neq \mathbf{0}$ satisfying $P\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{0}$, the matrix $P$ has a nontrivial nullspace ($\dim(\operatorname{Null}(P)) \ge 1$). By the invertible matrix theorem, $\det(P) = 0$ and $P$ is singular.

**Method 2 (Eigenvalue Argument)**:

Let $(\lambda, \mathbf{x})$ be any eigenvalue and nonzero eigenvector of $P$, so $P\mathbf{x} = \lambda\mathbf{x}$.

Multiplying both sides on the left by $P$ gives $P^2\mathbf{x} = \lambda P\mathbf{x} = \lambda^2 \mathbf{x}$.

Since $P^2 = P$, we have $P\mathbf{x} = P^2\mathbf{x} \implies \lambda\mathbf{x} = \lambda^2\mathbf{x}$.

Because $\mathbf{x} \neq \mathbf{0}$, the scalar satisfies $\lambda^2 - \lambda = 0 \implies \lambda \in \{0, 1\}$.

If $P$ were invertible, all of its eigenvalues would be nonzero, forcing every eigenvalue to be $1$. By the spectral theorem for real symmetric matrices, $P$ would be orthogonally similar to $I_m$ and hence $P = I_m$, contradicting $P \neq I_m$. Therefore $0$ must be an eigenvalue, and $\det(P) = \prod \lambda_i = 0$.

<ProgressiveHint
  buttonPrefix="Unlock Hint"
  hints={[

<DualSolution
  triggerLabel="Show Full Dual Solution (Intuition + Rigorous Proof)"
  intuitionLabel="Geometric Intuition"
  rigorousLabel="Rigorous Proof / Construction"
>
<SolutionIntuition>

The trace of a matrix equals the sum of all its eigenvalues. For an orthogonal projection matrix, every nonzero vector lying inside the target subspace stays unchanged (eigenvalue $\lambda = 1$), while every nonzero vector in the orthogonal complement is sent to zero (eigenvalue $\lambda = 0$). Therefore, the number of $1$s among the eigenvalues is literally the dimension (degrees of freedom) of the subspace! Summing the diagonal entries of the $m\times m$ matrix $P$ counts exactly how many independent orthogonal directions live inside the subspace.

</SolutionIntuition>
<SolutionRigorous>

**1. Cyclic Trace Derivation**:

Let $X = A \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ and $Y = (A^TA)^{-1}A^T \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times m}$, so $P = XY \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times m}$.

Applying the cyclic trace identity $\operatorname{tr}(XY) = \operatorname{tr}(YX)$:

$$
\operatorname{tr}(P) = \operatorname{tr}(XY) = \operatorname{tr}(YX) = \operatorname{tr}\left( (A^TA)^{-1}A^T \cdot A \right)
$$

Notice that the product inside the parentheses collapses to the $n\times n$ identity matrix:

$$
(A^TA)^{-1}(A^TA) = I_n \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
$$

Therefore:

$$
\operatorname{tr}(P) = \operatorname{tr}(I_n) = \underbrace{1 + 1 + \cdots + 1}_{n \text{ ones}} = n
$$

**2. Unifying Trace, Rank, and Dimension**:

Because $P \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times m}$ is real symmetric and idempotent, it is orthogonally diagonalizable with eigenvalues restricted to $1$ and $0$:
- The multiplicity of eigenvalue $\lambda = 1$ equals the dimension of the eigenspace $E(1) = \{\mathbf{x} \mid P\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{x}\} = \operatorname{Col}(A)$, which is $n$;
- The multiplicity of eigenvalue $\lambda = 0$ equals the dimension of the eigenspace $E(0) = \{\mathbf{x} \mid P\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}\} = \operatorname{Null}(A^T)$, which is $m - n$.

Consequently:

$$
\operatorname{tr}(P) = \sum_{i=1}^{m} \lambda_i = n \times 1 + (m-n) \times 0 = n
$$

The rank equals the number of nonzero eigenvalues counted with multiplicity, so

$$
\operatorname{rank}(P) = n = \dim(\operatorname{Col}(A)) = \operatorname{tr}(P)
$$

The same argument applies to a projector onto any subspace by replacing $n$ with the dimension of that subspace.

</SolutionRigorous>
</DualSolution>

(A^TA)^{-1} = \frac{1}{3} \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}

Now compute the projection matrix $P$:

### 1. Removing Selected Probe Directions from Activations

Let $\mathbf{h}\in\mathbb{R}^d$ be a model activation. Given a set of linear probe weight directions, the complementary projector $I_d-P_C$ removes the activation's components along their span and keeps its orthogonal component.

### 2. Orthogonal Projection for a Fixed Probe Subspace

Suppose we train one or more **linear probes** on the model's activations and extract direction vectors $\mathbf{c}_1, \dots, \mathbf{c}_k \in \mathbb{R}^d$ representing a sensitive concept (typically $k \ll d$, such as $k = 1$ or $k = 4$). Stacking these concept directions as columns forms the matrix:

$$
C = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{c}_1 & \mathbf{c}_2 & \cdots & \mathbf{c}_k \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times k}
$$

Assuming the probe directions are linearly independent, $C$ has full column rank, and the subspace spanned by the sensitive concept is the column space $\operatorname{Col}(C) \subseteq \mathbb{R}^d$.

By the full-column-rank formula in Section 3.2, the orthogonal projector onto the probe-weight span is

$$
P_C = C(C^T C)^{-1} C^T \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
$$

and the complementary projection matrix is:

$$
I_d - P_C = I_d - C(C^T C)^{-1} C^T
$$

For any hidden-state vector $\mathbf{h} \in \mathbb{R}^d$, projecting it onto the orthogonal complement of the concept subspace yields the scrubbed representation:

$$
\mathbf{h}_\perp = (I_d - P_C)\mathbf{h}
$$

The projector gives the following two guarantees.

#### 1. Zero Scores Along the Specified Probe Directions
For any probe weight $\mathbf{w}=C\boldsymbol{\alpha}$ with $\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}^k$, its linear score after projection is

$$
\mathbf{w}^T \mathbf{h}_\perp = (C\boldsymbol{\alpha})^T (I_d - P_C)\mathbf{h} = \boldsymbol{\alpha}^T \left( C^T - \underbrace{C^T C(C^T C)^{-1}}_{I_k} C^T \right) \mathbf{h} = \boldsymbol{\alpha}^T (C^T - C^T)\mathbf{h} = 0
$$

An affine probe with such a weight outputs only its constant bias. This guarantee covers weights in the specified span. Other linear or nonlinear probes may still recover concept information.

#### 2. Minimum Euclidean Change to the Specified Nullspace
Among all candidate scrubbed vectors $\mathbf{z}$ that are completely orthogonal to the concept subspace ($C^T\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{0}$), $\mathbf{h}_\perp$ is the **unique closest point** minimizing the Euclidean distortion $\|\mathbf{h} - \mathbf{z}\|_2$:

$$
\mathbf{h}_\perp = \arg\min_{\mathbf{z} \in \mathbb{R}^d, \, C^T\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{0}} \|\mathbf{h} - \mathbf{z}\|_2^2
$$

**Algebraic Proof**:

For any vector $\mathbf{z}$ satisfying $C^T\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{0}$, decompose the perturbation into two pieces:

$$
\mathbf{h} - \mathbf{z} = (\mathbf{h} - \mathbf{h}_\perp) + (\mathbf{h}_\perp - \mathbf{z}) = P_C\mathbf{h} + (\mathbf{h}_\perp - \mathbf{z})
$$

Notice that the first term $P_C\mathbf{h} \in \operatorname{Col}(C)$, whereas the second term satisfies $C^T(\mathbf{h}_\perp - \mathbf{z}) = C^T\mathbf{h}_\perp - C^T\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{0} - \mathbf{0} = \mathbf{0}$, so it lies in the orthogonal complement $\operatorname{Null}(C^T)$!

By the Pythagorean theorem:

$$
\|\mathbf{h} - \mathbf{z}\|_2^2 = \|P_C\mathbf{h}\|_2^2 + \|\mathbf{h}_\perp - \mathbf{z}\|_2^2 \ge \|P_C\mathbf{h}\|_2^2
$$

Equality holds if and only if the second term vanishes, i.e., $\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{h}_\perp$!

### 3. Iterative Nullspace Projection and LEACE

**INLP (Iterative Null-space Projection)** repeatedly fits a classifier to the projected representations. Let $P_{i-1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ be the orthogonal projector onto the surviving space, starting with $P_0=I_d$. Let $W_i\in\mathbb{R}^{k_i\times d}$ be the new probe weight matrix with $k_i$ outputs. The effective weights are $B_i=W_iP_{i-1}$. Their rows lie in the surviving space, so $P_i=P_{i-1}-B_i^+B_i$ projects onto its intersection with $\operatorname{Null}(B_i)$. For a single nonzero effective weight $\mathbf{w}_i$, the removed rank-one term is $\mathbf{w}_i\mathbf{w}_i^T/\|\mathbf{w}_i\|_2^2$. A product of arbitrary orthogonal projectors need not project orthogonally onto their intersection.

**LEACE (Least-squares Concept Erasure)** constrains feature-label cross-covariance directly. Let random column vectors $\mathbf{h}\in\mathbb{R}^d$ and $\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^k$ have finite second moments. Write $\boldsymbol{\mu}=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{h}]$, $\Sigma_{hh}=\operatorname{Cov}(\mathbf{h},\mathbf{h})$, and $\Sigma_{hz}=\operatorname{Cov}(\mathbf{h},\mathbf{z})$. Put $W=(\Sigma_{hh}^{1/2})^+$, $B=W\Sigma_{hz}$, and $P_B=BB^+$, the orthogonal projector onto $\operatorname{Col}(B)$. Here $^+$ denotes the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse. LEACE applies the affine map

$$
f(\mathbf{h})=\boldsymbol{\mu}+(I_d-W^+P_BW)(\mathbf{h}-\boldsymbol{\mu}).
$$

Since $\operatorname{Col}(\Sigma_{hz})\subseteq\operatorname{Col}(\Sigma_{hh})$ and $P_BB=B$, we have $W^+P_BW\Sigma_{hz}=W^+W\Sigma_{hz}=\Sigma_{hz}$. Hence $\operatorname{Cov}(f(\mathbf{h}),\mathbf{z})=0$. For one-hot labels with positive class probabilities, this is equivalent to equality of the class-conditional means. For losses convex in the prediction, Jensen’s inequality within each class shows that any affine probe has risk at least that of its constant prediction at the common mean. The infimum of the risk is therefore the same for affine and constant predictors. Higher-order class statistics can still differ.

LEACE minimizes $\mathbb{E}\|f(\mathbf{h})-\mathbf{h}\|_2^2$ among affine maps satisfying the cross-covariance constraint. Its linear part is generally an oblique projector in the original coordinates. The pointwise closest-point problem above fixes the output subspace, whereas this constraint specifies the directions to annihilate.

import torch

def verify_concept_erasure():
    # Set hidden dimension d=256 and erased concept dimension k=3
    torch.manual_seed(42)
    d, k, batch_size = 256, 3, 10
    
    # Simulate probe weight matrix C: d x k (full column rank)
    C = torch.randn(d, k, dtype=torch.float64)
    
    # 1. Construct subspace orthogonal projection matrix P_C = C (C^T C)^{-1} C^T
    CtC = C.T @ C  # k x k symmetric square matrix
    P_C = C @ torch.linalg.solve(CtC, C.T)  # d x d
    
    # 2. Construct complementary projection matrix I - P_C
    I_d = torch.eye(d, dtype=C.dtype)
    P_perp = I_d - P_C
    
    # Verify core algebraic axioms
    # (a) Symmetry: P^T = P
    sym_error = torch.norm(P_C - P_C.T).item()
    assert sym_error < 1e-5, f"Symmetry failed: {sym_error}"
    
    # (b) Idempotence: P^2 = P
    idemp_error = torch.norm(P_C @ P_C - P_C).item()
    assert idemp_error < 1e-5, f"Idempotence failed: {idemp_error}"
    
    # (c) Orthogonal decoupling: P * (I - P) = 0
    orth_decouple_error = torch.norm(P_C @ P_perp).item()
    assert orth_decouple_error < 1e-5, f"Decoupling failed: {orth_decouple_error}"
    
    # 3. Simulate hidden-layer representations h: batch_size x d
    h = torch.randn(batch_size, d, dtype=C.dtype)
    
    # 4. Surgical concept erasure: h_perp = h @ (I - P_C)^T = h @ P_perp
    h_perp = h @ P_perp.T  # batch_size x d
    h_parallel = h @ P_C.T  # concept component
    
    # 5. Verify that the specified probe scores C.T @ h_perp.T are near zero
    # i.e., C^T @ (h_perp)^T = 0  => shape: k x batch_size
    leakage = C.T @ h_perp.T
    max_leakage = torch.max(torch.abs(leakage)).item()
    assert max_leakage < 1e-5, f"Linear leakage detected: {max_leakage}"
    
    # 6. Verify Pythagorean energy conservation: ||h||^2 = ||h_parallel||^2 + ||h_perp||^2
    norm_h_sq = torch.norm(h, dim=1) ** 2
    norm_parts_sq = torch.norm(h_parallel, dim=1) ** 2 + torch.norm(h_perp, dim=1) ** 2
    energy_diff = torch.max(torch.abs(norm_h_sq - norm_parts_sq)).item()
    assert energy_diff < 1e-4, f"Pythagorean theorem mismatch: {energy_diff}"
    
    print("Complementary projection checks passed for the specified probes.")
    print(f"  - Projection symmetry residual:   {sym_error:.2e}")
    print(f"  - Projection idempotence residual: {idemp_error:.2e}")
    print(f"  - Max absolute probe score:        {max_leakage:.2e}")
    print(f"  - Max Pythagorean energy error:    {energy_diff:.2e}")

if __name__ == "__main__":
    verify_concept_erasure()
