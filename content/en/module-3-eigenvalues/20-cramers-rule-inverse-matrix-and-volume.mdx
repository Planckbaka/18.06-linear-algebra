In the previous two lectures, we laid the grand foundation for the theory of determinants: in Lecture 18, we defined the determinant rigorously through 3 fundamental axioms and 7 derived properties; in Lecture 19, we established explicit computational paths via the $n!$-term Big Formula and Laplace cofactor expansion.

At this point, a completely natural mathematical question arises: **Now that we have worked so hard to compute this scalar $\det(A)$, what is it actually good for in linear algebra and geometric space?**

In this lecture, Prof. Gilbert Strang gives three striking and elegant answers:
1. **The ultimate closed-form algebraic formula for the inverse**: Without any elimination, we can write the inverse matrix directly via the **adjugate matrix** as $A^{-1} = \frac{1}{\det A} C^T$;
2. **A purely symbolic closed-form solution for linear systems**: **Cramer's Rule**, accompanied by a breathtaking one-line matrix-identity proof;
3. **The true geometric measure of space**: The absolute value $|\det(A)|$ equals the **unsigned volume** of an $n$-dimensional **parallelepiped**, while its sign strictly encodes the handedness (orientation) of the coordinate system.

---

## 1. The Adjugate Matrix and the Closed-Form Inverse $A^{-1} = \frac{1}{\det A} C^T$

In numerical computing, Gauss-Jordan elimination is the standard workhorse for computing matrix inverses (with $O(n^3)$ complexity). However, in linear algebra theory, symbolic physics derivations, and perturbation analysis, we need an **explicit analytical expression** for every entry of $A^{-1}$ in terms of the entries of $A$.

### 1.1 Cofactor Matrix and Adjugate Matrix

Recall from Lecture 19 that for $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$, if we delete row $i$ and column $j$ to obtain the $(n-1) \times (n-1)$ submatrix $M'_{ij}$, then the minor is $M_{ij}=\det(M'_{ij})$ and the **cofactor** at position $(i, j)$ is defined as:

$$
C_{ij} = (-1)^{i+j} \det(M'_{ij})
$$

Arranging all cofactors into an $n \times n$ matrix gives the **cofactor matrix** $C$.

<DefinitionCard label="Definition" title="Cofactor Matrix and Adjugate Matrix">
Let $A = [a_{ij}] \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ with $n \ge 1$. For $n=1$, the empty minor is $1$. Its **cofactor matrix** $C \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ has $(i, j)$ entry equal to the cofactor $C_{ij}$ of $A$:

$$
C = \begin{bmatrix}
C_{11} & C_{12} & \cdots & C_{1n} \\
C_{21} & C_{22} & \cdots & C_{2n} \\
\vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
C_{n1} & C_{n2} & \cdots & C_{nn}
\end{bmatrix}
$$

The **transpose** $C^T$ of the cofactor matrix is called the **adjugate matrix** (or classical adjoint) of $A$, denoted $\operatorname{adj}(A)$:

$$
\operatorname{adj}(A) \triangleq C^T = \begin{bmatrix}
C_{11} & C_{21} & \cdots & C_{n1} \\
C_{12} & C_{22} & \cdots & C_{n2} \\
\vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
C_{1n} & C_{2n} & \cdots & C_{nn}
\end{bmatrix}
$$

**Crucial Warning**: The $(j, i)$ entry of the adjugate matrix is the cofactor $C_{ij}$ from row $i$, column $j$ of $A$. **Never forget the transpose!**
</DefinitionCard>

### 1.2 The Adjugate Identity and the Inverse Formula

The most remarkable property of the adjugate matrix is the extraordinary symmetry it reveals when multiplied by the original matrix $A$.

<TheoremCard label="Theorem" title="Fundamental Adjugate Identity and Closed-Form Inverse">
For any square matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$, the product of $A$ and its adjugate $C^T$ is always the scalar matrix with $\det(A)$ along the main diagonal:

$$
A C^T = C^T A = \det(A) I_n
$$

By the determinant criterion, $A$ is invertible exactly when $\det(A)\ne 0$. In that case,

$$
A^{-1} = \frac{1}{\det(A)} C^T = \frac{1}{\det(A)} \operatorname{adj}(A)
$$
</TheoremCard>

<ProofCollapse title="Rigorous Proof: The Adjugate Identity A C^T = (det A) I_n">
Let us examine each entry $P_{ik}$ of the product matrix $P = A C^T \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$. By the definition of matrix multiplication:

$$
P_{ik} = \sum_{j=1}^n a_{ij} (C^T)_{jk} = \sum_{j=1}^n a_{ij} C_{kj}
$$

Here $a_{ij}$ comes from row $i$ of $A$, while $C_{kj}$ are the cofactors corresponding to row $k$ of $A$. We split into two cases depending on whether $i = k$ or $i \neq k$:

**Case 1: Diagonal entries ($i = k$)**
When $i = k$:

$$
P_{ii} = \sum_{j=1}^n a_{ij} C_{ij} = a_{i1} C_{i1} + a_{i2} C_{i2} + \cdots + a_{in} C_{in}
$$

This is precisely the **Laplace cofactor expansion of $\det(A)$ along row $i$**, proved in Lecture 19! Therefore:

$$
P_{ii} = \det(A) \quad (\forall i = 1, 2, \dots, n)
$$

**Case 2: Off-diagonal entries ($i \neq k$, "cross-row cofactor multiplication")**
When $i \neq k$, the sum becomes:

$$
P_{ik} = a_{i1} C_{k1} + a_{i2} C_{k2} + \cdots + a_{in} C_{kn}
$$

This expression has a brilliant geometric and algebraic interpretation. Let $\widetilde{A}$ be $A$ with row $k$ replaced by row $i$.

Now compute $\det(\widetilde{A})$ by Laplace expansion along its $k$-th row:
- The entries of row $k$ of $\widetilde{A}$ are $a_{i1}, a_{i2}, \dots, a_{in}$;
- Deleting row $k$ of $\widetilde{A}$ leaves the exact same $(n-1)$ rows as deleting row $k$ of $A$, so the cofactors of $\widetilde{A}$ along row $k$ are still $C_{k1}, C_{k2}, \dots, C_{kn}$!

Thus, the determinant of the auxiliary matrix is:

$$
\det(\widetilde{A}) = \sum_{j=1}^n a_{ij} C_{kj} = P_{ik}
$$

However, $\widetilde{A}$ has **two identical rows** (row $i$ and row $k$). By Property 4 of determinants from Lecture 18 (two equal rows force the determinant to be zero):

$$
\det(\widetilde{A}) = 0 \implies P_{ik} = 0 \quad (\forall i \neq k)
$$

By an identical argument using column-wise Laplace expansion and column replacement, one shows $(C^T A)_{ik} = \det(A) \delta_{ik}$, where $\delta_{ik}$ is $1$ for $i=k$ and $0$ otherwise.

Combining both cases, $A C^T$ and $C^T A$ have $\det(A)$ on the diagonal and $0$ everywhere else:

$$
A C^T = C^T A = \begin{bmatrix}
\det(A) & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\
0 & \det(A) & \cdots & 0 \\
\vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
0 & 0 & \cdots & \det(A)
\end{bmatrix} = \det(A) I_n
$$

When $\det(A) \neq 0$, dividing both sides by the scalar $\det(A)$ immediately yields $A^{-1} = \frac{1}{\det(A)} C^T$.
</ProofCollapse>

### 1.3 Worked Examples: From $2 \times 2$ to $3 \times 3$

#### Re-deriving the $2 \times 2$ Inverse Formula

Consider the $2 \times 2$ matrix:

$$
A = \begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{bmatrix}
$$

- Delete row 1, col 1: minor is $d$, sign $(-1)^{1+1} = +1 \implies C_{11} = d$;
- Delete row 1, col 2: minor is $c$, sign $(-1)^{1+2} = -1 \implies C_{12} = -c$;
- Delete row 2, col 1: minor is $b$, sign $(-1)^{2+1} = -1 \implies C_{21} = -b$;
- Delete row 2, col 2: minor is $a$, sign $(-1)^{2+2} = +1 \implies C_{22} = a$.

The cofactor matrix is:

$$
C = \begin{bmatrix} d & -c \\ -b & a \end{bmatrix}
$$

Taking its **transpose**:

$$
\operatorname{adj}(A) = C^T = \begin{bmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a \end{bmatrix}
$$

If $\det(A)=ad-bc\ne 0$, we immediately recover the familiar $2 \times 2$ inverse recipe ("swap the diagonal, negate the off-diagonal, divide by the determinant"):

$$
A^{-1} = \frac{1}{ad - bc} \begin{bmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a \end{bmatrix}
$$

#### A $3 \times 3$ Matrix Inversion in Action

Let:

$$
A = \begin{bmatrix}
1 & 0 & 2 \\
2 & -1 & 3 \\
4 & 1 & 8
\end{bmatrix}
$$

First, compute the determinant by expanding along row 1:

$$
\det(A) = 1 \cdot \det\begin{bmatrix} -1 & 3 \\ 1 & 8 \end{bmatrix} - 0 + 2 \cdot \det\begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ 4 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = 1(-8 - 3) + 2(2 - (-4)) = -11 + 12 = 1
$$

Compute all 9 cofactors:

$$
\begin{aligned}
C_{11} &= +(-8 - 3) = -11, & C_{12} &= -(16 - 12) = -4, & C_{13} &= +(2 - (-4)) = 6 \\
C_{21} &= -(0 - 2) = 2, & C_{22} &= +(8 - 8) = 0, & C_{23} &= -(1 - 0) = -1 \\
C_{31} &= +(0 - (-2)) = 2, & C_{32} &= -(3 - 4) = 1, & C_{33} &= +(-1 - 0) = -1
\end{aligned}
$$

Write down the cofactor matrix $C$ and transpose it to obtain the adjugate:

$$
C = \begin{bmatrix} -11 & -4 & 6 \\ 2 & 0 & -1 \\ 2 & 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix} \implies C^T = \begin{bmatrix} -11 & 2 & 2 \\ -4 & 0 & 1 \\ 6 & -1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}
$$

Since $\det(A) = 1$, the inverse matrix is:

$$
A^{-1} = \frac{1}{1} C^T = \begin{bmatrix} -11 & 2 & 2 \\ -4 & 0 & 1 \\ 6 & -1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}
$$

Verification:

$$
A A^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 2 \\ 2 & -1 & 3 \\ 4 & 1 & 8 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} -11 & 2 & 2 \\ -4 & 0 & 1 \\ 6 & -1 & -1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = I_3
$$

## 2. Cramer's Rule and Strang's One-Line Proof

Given a nonsingular linear system $A \mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$, how can we write down each unknown component $x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n$ directly in terms of the entries of $A$ and $\mathbf{b}$, without performing any elimination?

Swiss mathematician Gabriel Cramer (1750) gave the famous ratio rule. In MIT 18.06, Prof. Gilbert Strang presents a **brilliant one-line proof** that bypasses index-by-index summation entirely and relies solely on the multiplicative property of determinants.

<TheoremCard label="Theorem" title="Cramer's Rule">
Let $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ be nonsingular ($\det(A) \neq 0$), and let $\mathbf{b} \in \mathbb{R}^n$. Define $B_j \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ as the matrix obtained by replacing column $j$ of $A$ with the right-hand side vector $\mathbf{b}$:

$$
B_j = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{a}_1 & \cdots & \mathbf{a}_{j-1} & \mathbf{b} & \mathbf{a}_{j+1} & \cdots & \mathbf{a}_n \end{bmatrix}
$$

Then the unique solution $\mathbf{x} = [x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n]^T$ to $A \mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ has components given by:

$$
x_j = \frac{\det(B_j)}{\det(A)} \quad (j = 1, 2, \dots, n)
$$
</TheoremCard>

### 2.1 Proof Path 1: Algebraic Derivation via the Adjugate Inverse Formula

We know $\mathbf{x} = A^{-1} \mathbf{b}$. Substituting $A^{-1} = \frac{1}{\det A} C^T$ gives:

$$
\mathbf{x} = \frac{1}{\det(A)} C^T \mathbf{b}
$$

Looking at the $j$-th component $x_j$:

$$
x_j = \frac{1}{\det(A)} \sum_{i=1}^n (C^T)_{ji} b_i = \frac{1}{\det(A)} \sum_{i=1}^n b_i C_{ij}
$$

Now examine $B_j$. Because $B_j$ differs from $A$ only in column $j$ (which is $\mathbf{b}$), if we expand $\det(B_j)$ **along column $j$**:
- The entries in column $j$ are $b_1, b_2, \dots, b_n$;
- Deleting column $j$ leaves the exact same submatrices as deleting column $j$ of $A$, so the cofactors are still $C_{1j}, C_{2j}, \dots, C_{nj}$!

Therefore, the determinant of $B_j$ is precisely:

$$
\det(B_j) = b_1 C_{1j} + b_2 C_{2j} + \cdots + b_n C_{nj} = \sum_{i=1}^n b_i C_{ij}
$$

Substituting this into the expression for $x_j$ gives:

$$
x_j = \frac{\det(B_j)}{\det(A)}
$$

### 2.2 Proof Path 2: Gilbert Strang's "One-Line Proof"

Prof. Strang's classroom proof is widely celebrated as a gem of linear algebra exposition.

Construct a special matrix $X_j$ by replacing column $j$ of the identity matrix $I_n$ with the unknown solution vector $\mathbf{x}$:

$$
X_j = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{e}_1 & \cdots & \mathbf{e}_{j-1} & \mathbf{x} & \mathbf{e}_{j+1} & \cdots & \mathbf{e}_n \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}
1 & & x_1 & & \\
& \ddots & \vdots & & \\
& & x_j & & \\
& & \vdots & \ddots & \\
& & x_n & & 1
\end{bmatrix}
$$

What is $\det(X_j)$? Row $j$ has only one possibly nonzero entry, $x_j$. Its $(j,j)$-minor is the determinant of $I_{n-1}$, so expanding along row $j$ gives

$$
\det(X_j) = x_j
$$

Now multiply $A$ on the right by $X_j$, using the column picture of matrix multiplication:

$$
A X_j = A \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{e}_1 & \cdots & \mathbf{e}_{j-1} & \mathbf{x} & \mathbf{e}_{j+1} & \cdots & \mathbf{e}_n \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} A \mathbf{e}_1 & \cdots & A \mathbf{x} & \cdots & A \mathbf{e}_n \end{bmatrix}
$$

Since $A \mathbf{e}_k = \mathbf{a}_k$ (column $k$ of $A$) and $A \mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$:

$$
A X_j = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{a}_1 & \cdots & \mathbf{b} & \cdots & \mathbf{a}_n \end{bmatrix} = B_j
$$

Take determinants of both sides! By Property 9 ($\det(AB) = \det(A)\det(B)$):

$$
\det(A X_j) = \det(B_j) \implies \det(A) \cdot \det(X_j) = \det(B_j)
$$

Substitute $\det(X_j) = x_j$:

$$
\det(A) \cdot x_j = \det(B_j) \implies x_j = \frac{\det(B_j)}{\det(A)}
$$

The entire proof takes a single matrix multiplication—no messy index sums, just pure structural insight!

### 2.3 Algorithmic Reality Check: Cramer's Rule vs. Gaussian Elimination

Cramer's Rule is taught in every linear algebra course, yet in large-scale numerical problems such as finite element simulations, **computing each Cramer determinant separately is usually more expensive than solving the system by elimination**. How do the costs compare?

Let us count the floating-point operations (FLOPs):
- Solving an $n \times n$ system with Cramer's Rule requires computing **$n + 1$ determinants** ($\det(A)$ and $\det(B_1), \dots, \det(B_n)$).
- If computed via cofactor expansion, each determinant takes $O(n!)$ operations, for a total of $O((n+1)!)$. Even for $n = 50$, $51! \approx 1.55 \times 10^{66}$.
- Even if each determinant is computed via Gaussian elimination ($O(n^3)$ per determinant), $n + 1$ determinants require:

$$
(n+1) \cdot \frac{2}{3} n^3 \approx \frac{2}{3} n^4 \text{ FLOPs}
$$

- By contrast, $PA = LU$ factorization solves the entire system with **one** elimination pass costing $\frac{2}{3}n^3$ FLOPs, followed by forward/back substitution in $O(n^2)$ operations—giving a total cost of $\frac{2}{3}n^3+O(n^2)$ FLOPs.

| Method | Operation Count (FLOPs) | Cost at $n = 10$ | Estimated Cost at $n = 1000$ | Best Use Case |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Cofactor Cramer's Rule** | $O((n+1)!)$ | $\approx 4 \times 10^7$ terms | On the order of $10^{2570}$ terms | Theoretical proof of concept |
| **Elimination Cramer's Rule** | $O(n^4)$ | $\approx 7.3 \times 10^3$ ops | $\approx 6.7 \times 10^{11}$ ops | Symbolic algebraic derivation |
| **$LU$ Elimination** | $O(n^3)$ | $\approx 6.7 \times 10^2$ ops | $\approx 6.7 \times 10^8$ ops | **Modern engineering standard** |

*These estimates use $(n+1)!$ to count the permutation terms across the $n+1$ determinants. The LU estimates use its leading FLOP count.*

**Conclusion**: The true value of Cramer's Rule lies in **purely symbolic and analytical work** (for instance, when a matrix depends on parameters $k, t$, or when we need closed-form sensitivities $\frac{\partial x_j}{\partial b_i}$), not large-scale numerical computation.

## 3. Volume $|\det(A)|$ and Orientation and the $A = QR$ Altitude Decomposition

Now we arrive at the visual climax of this lecture: **What is the geometric essence of the determinant?**

In 2D, two column vectors $\mathbf{a}_1, \mathbf{a}_2$ span a parallelogram; in 3D, three column vectors $\mathbf{a}_1, \mathbf{a}_2, \mathbf{a}_3$ span a parallelepiped; in $\mathbb{R}^n$, $n$ column vectors span an $n$-dimensional **parallelepiped**:

$$
P = \left\{ \sum_{i=1}^n c_i \mathbf{a}_i \;\middle|\; 0 \le c_i \le 1 \right\}
$$

<TheoremCard label="Theorem" title="Oriented Volume Theorem">
Let $A = [\mathbf{a}_1, \mathbf{a}_2, \dots, \mathbf{a}_n] \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$. The $n$-dimensional volume of the possibly degenerate parallelepiped $P$ spanned by its column vectors (or row vectors) equals the absolute value of the determinant:

$$
\operatorname{Vol}(P) = |\det(A)|
$$

Moreover, the signed volume is $\det(A)$. For independent columns, its sign gives their orientation relative to the standard ordered basis:
- $\det(A) \gt 0$: The ordered basis has the same orientation;
- $\det(A) \lt 0$: The ordered basis has the opposite orientation;
- $\det(A) = 0$: The parallelepiped collapses into a lower-dimensional subspace with zero volume (linearly dependent columns).
</TheoremCard>

<ProofCollapse title="Rigorous Proof: Path 1 — Gram-Schmidt Orthogonalization and A = QR">
If $A$ is singular, its columns lie in a proper subspace, so both the $n$-dimensional volume and $|\det(A)|$ are zero. For invertible $A$, Lecture 17 gives the unique QR factorization. In this case the matrix $A$ factors uniquely as $A = QR$, where:
- $Q \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ is an orthogonal matrix ($Q^T Q = I_n$);
- $R \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ is upper triangular with positive diagonal entries ($r_{ii} \gt 0$).

By the multiplicative property of determinants:

$$
|\det(A)| = |\det(Q)| \cdot |\det(R)|
$$

1. Since $Q^T Q = I_n$, we have $\det(Q^T Q) = (\det Q)^2 = \det(I_n) = 1$, so $|\det(Q)| = 1$ (orthogonal maps preserve lengths and volumes).
2. Since $R$ is upper triangular, Property 7 from Lecture 18 tells us its determinant is the product of its diagonal entries:

$$
\det(R) = \prod_{i=1}^n r_{ii} = r_{11} r_{22} \cdots r_{nn}
$$

Hence:

$$
|\det(A)| = 1 \cdot \prod_{i=1}^n r_{ii} = r_{11} r_{22} \cdots r_{nn}
$$

Now recall the geometry of Gram-Schmidt from Lecture 17:
- Step 1: $\mathbf{a}_1 = r_{11} \mathbf{q}_1$. Because $\|\mathbf{q}_1\|_2 = 1$, $r_{11} = \|\mathbf{a}_1\|_2$ is the **length of the first base edge**;
- Step 2: $\mathbf{a}_2 = r_{12} \mathbf{q}_1 + r_{22} \mathbf{q}_2$. Here $r_{12} \mathbf{q}_1$ is the projection of $\mathbf{a}_2$ along the first edge (a parallel shear component), while $r_{22} \mathbf{q}_2$ is the component perpendicular to $\mathbf{q}_1$! Thus $r_{22}$ is the **perpendicular altitude** of $\mathbf{a}_2$ relative to the base, giving 2D area:

$$
\text{Area}_{2D} = r_{11} \cdot r_{22}
$$

- Step 3: $\mathbf{a}_3 = r_{13} \mathbf{q}_1 + r_{23} \mathbf{q}_2 + r_{33} \mathbf{q}_3$. The first two terms lie in the base plane $\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf{q}_1, \mathbf{q}_2\}$, and only $r_{33} \mathbf{q}_3$ points normal to that plane! Thus $r_{33}$ is the **orthogonal 3D height**, giving:

$$
\text{Vol}_{3D} = (\text{base area } r_{11} r_{22}) \times (\text{orthogonal height } r_{33}) = r_{11} r_{22} r_{33}
$$

By mathematical induction, $r_{ii}$ is geometrically the **perpendicular altitude** of $\mathbf{a}_i$ relative to the subspace spanned by $\mathbf{a}_1, \dots, \mathbf{a}_{i-1}$. Therefore, $\prod_{i=1}^n r_{ii}$ is the direct $n$-dimensional generalization of "base $\times$ height"!
</ProofCollapse>

<ProofCollapse title="Geometric Proof via Signed Heights">

Fix all but one column. If the fixed columns are linearly dependent, every resulting parallelepiped has zero $n$-volume. Otherwise, they span an $(n-1)$-dimensional subspace. Choose its unit normal $\mathbf{n}$ so that the ordered columns have positive orientation when the missing column is $\mathbf{n}$. For a variable column $\mathbf{v}$, the signed volume is then the fixed base volume times the signed height $\mathbf{n}^T\mathbf{v}$. This is linear in $\mathbf{v}$, proving both scaling and additivity.

Exchanging two columns preserves unsigned volume and reverses orientation when the columns form a basis. The standard ordered basis gives signed volume $1$. Thus signed volume satisfies the column versions of the three determinant axioms, so it equals $\det(A)$.

</ProofCollapse>

### 3.1 Classic Geometric Applications: Triangle Area and the Jacobian

#### Area of Any Triangle in the Plane

Suppose a triangle in the $xy$-plane has vertices $(x_1, y_1)$, $(x_2, y_2)$, and $(x_3, y_3)$. How do we compute its area with a single determinant? We embed the plane at height $z = 1$ in $\mathbb{R}^3$:

$$
\operatorname{Area} = \frac{1}{2} \left| \det \begin{bmatrix}
x_1 & y_1 & 1 \\
x_2 & y_2 & 1 \\
x_3 & y_3 & 1
\end{bmatrix} \right|
$$

**Why append a column of $1$'s?**
Using row operations, subtract row 1 from row 2 and row 3:

$$
\det \begin{bmatrix}
x_1 & y_1 & 1 \\
x_2 - x_1 & y_2 - y_1 & 0 \\
x_3 - x_1 & y_3 - y_1 & 0
\end{bmatrix} = 1 \cdot \det \begin{bmatrix}
x_2 - x_1 & y_2 - y_1 \\
x_3 - x_1 & y_3 - y_1
\end{bmatrix}
$$

This is precisely the determinant of the parallelogram spanned by the edge vectors $\mathbf{v}_{12}$ and $\mathbf{v}_{13}$ when vertex 1 is shifted to the origin! Because a triangle is half of that parallelogram, the factor $\frac{1}{2}$ appears naturally.

#### The Jacobian Determinant in Multivariable Calculus

When changing variables in a multiple integral from Cartesian coordinates $(x, y, z)$ to curvilinear coordinates $(u, v, w)$ via $\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{g}(u, v, w)$, assume $\mathbf{g}$ is a $C^1$ diffeomorphism between the integration domains. The volume element transforms by a local scaling factor:

$$
dx \, dy \, dz = |\det(J)| \, du \, dv \, dw, \quad J = \frac{\partial (x, y, z)}{\partial (u, v, w)} = \begin{bmatrix}
\frac{\partial x}{\partial u} & \frac{\partial x}{\partial v} & \frac{\partial x}{\partial w} \\
\frac{\partial y}{\partial u} & \frac{\partial y}{\partial v} & \frac{\partial y}{\partial w} \\
\frac{\partial z}{\partial u} & \frac{\partial z}{\partial v} & \frac{\partial z}{\partial w}
\end{bmatrix}
$$

**The linear-algebraic essence**: On an infinitesimal neighborhood, any smooth nonlinear map is locally linearized by its first-order Taylor approximation—the **Jacobian matrix** $J$. An infinitesimal cube $du \times dv \times dw$ is mapped by $J$ into an infinitesimal parallelepiped whose volume scaling ratio, by the Oriented Volume Theorem, is exactly $|\det(J)|$!

## 4. Common Pitfalls & Conceptual Traps

<PitfallAlert title="Common Pitfalls & Transcript Errata">
1. **Forgetting the transpose in the adjugate ($C^T$ vs. $C$)**:
   The adjugate is $C^T$. For an invertible $A$, the identity $C^T=\det(A)A^{-1}$ shows that $C=C^T$ exactly when $A=A^T$. For singular matrices, $C$ can be symmetric even if $A$ is not, for example when every cofactor is zero.
2. **Treating $\det(A) \lt 0$ as an error or meaningless**:
   The absolute value gives geometric volume. A negative determinant indicates that an invertible real linear map reverses orientation, for example by reversing the winding of a transformed mesh.
3. **Applying Cramer's Rule when $\det(A) = 0$**:
   Cramer's Rule divides by $\det(A)$, so it requires $A$ to be nonsingular. When $\det(A) = 0$, the system has either no solution or infinitely many solutions. Even if $\det(B_j) = 0$ as well, $\frac{0}{0}$ is indeterminate—you cannot conclude anything from Cramer's Rule and must return to echelon elimination (Lectures 7–9).
</PitfallAlert>

\det(A) \in \mathbb{Z} \quad \text{and} \quad \det(A^{-1}) \in \mathbb{Z}

A^{-1} = \frac{1}{\det(A)} C^T = \pm C^T \in \mathbb{Z}^{n \times n}

<ExerciseCard id="l20-ex4" tier="L3" tierLabel="L3 · Zero-Calculation Subspace Reasoning" title="Determinant and Rank Trichotomy of the Adjugate Matrix">
Let $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ ($n \ge 2$), and let $\operatorname{adj}(A) = C^T$ be its adjugate matrix.
1. Prove that $\det(\operatorname{adj}(A)) = (\det A)^{n-1}$.
2. Prove the **Rank Trichotomy** of the adjugate matrix:

$$
\operatorname{rank}(\operatorname{adj}(A)) = \begin{cases}
n, & \text{if } \operatorname{rank}(A) = n \\
1, & \text{if } \operatorname{rank}(A) = n - 1 \\
0, & \text{if } \operatorname{rank}(A) \le n - 2
\end{cases}
$$

<ProgressiveHint buttonPrefix="Unlock Hint" hints={[
  "For Part 1, start from $A\\operatorname{adj}(A) = \\det(A) I_n$ and take determinants of both sides.",
  "Be careful when $\\det(A)=0$: you cannot divide by 0, so handle the singular case separately using rank.",
  "When $\\operatorname{rank}(A)=n-1$, a nonzero minor gives $\\operatorname{adj}(A)\\ne 0$. Use $A\\operatorname{adj}(A)=0$ to bound its rank."

<DualSolution triggerLabel="Show Full Dual Solution (Intuition + Rigorous Proof)" intuitionLabel="Geometric Intuition" rigorousLabel="Rigorous Proof / Construction">
<SolutionIntuition>
The adjugate is built from $(n-1) \times (n-1)$ minors. When $A$ has full rank, $\det(\operatorname{adj}(A)) = (\det A)^{n-1}$; when $A$ drops by 1 rank, all $n \times n$ determinants vanish but a nonzero $(n-1) \times (n-1)$ minor survives, forcing all columns of $\operatorname{adj}(A)$ into the 1D nullspace of $A$ (rank 1); when $A$ drops by 2 or more ranks, every $(n-1) \times (n-1)$ minor collapses to zero.
</SolutionIntuition>

<SolutionRigorous>
**Proof of Part 1:**
Start from the fundamental adjugate identity:

$$
A \operatorname{adj}(A) = \det(A) I_n
$$

Take the determinant of both sides:

$$
\det(A \operatorname{adj}(A)) = \det((\det A) I_n)
$$

On the left, $\det(A \operatorname{adj}(A)) = \det(A) \det(\operatorname{adj}(A))$. On the right, factoring out the scalar $\det(A)$ from all $n$ rows of $I_n$ raises it to the $n$-th power:

$$
\det((\det A) I_n) = (\det A)^n \det(I_n) = (\det A)^n
$$

Thus:

$$
\det(A) \cdot \det(\operatorname{adj}(A)) = (\det A)^n
$$

- If $\det(A) \neq 0$, dividing both sides by $\det(A)$ gives $\det(\operatorname{adj}(A)) = (\det A)^{n-1}$.
- If $\det(A) = 0$, then by Part 2 below, $\operatorname{rank}(\operatorname{adj}(A)) \le 1 \lt n$ (since $n \ge 2$), so $\det(\operatorname{adj}(A)) = 0 = 0^{n-1}$. The identity holds in all cases.

**Proof of Part 2:**
- **Case 1: $\operatorname{rank}(A) = n$ (full rank)**
  Here $\det(A) \neq 0$, so $\det(\operatorname{adj}(A)) = (\det A)^{n-1} \neq 0$. Thus $\operatorname{adj}(A)$ is invertible and $\operatorname{rank}(\operatorname{adj}(A)) = n$.
- **Case 2: $\operatorname{rank}(A) = n - 1$**
  Since $\operatorname{rank}(A) = n - 1$, $A$ has at least one nonzero $(n-1) \times (n-1)$ minor, meaning at least one cofactor $C_{ij} \neq 0$. Therefore:

$$
\operatorname{adj}(A) \neq O \implies \operatorname{rank}(\operatorname{adj}(A)) \ge 1
$$

  On the other hand, $\det(A) = 0$ implies:

$$
A \operatorname{adj}(A) = O
$$

  Hence every column $\mathbf{c}_k$ of $\operatorname{adj}(A)$ satisfies $A \mathbf{c}_k = \mathbf{0}$, so $\operatorname{Col}(\operatorname{adj}(A)) \subseteq \operatorname{Null}(A)$. By the Rank-Nullity Theorem:

$$
\dim(\operatorname{Null}(A)) = n - \operatorname{rank}(A) = n - (n - 1) = 1
$$

  Therefore:

$$
\operatorname{rank}(\operatorname{adj}(A)) = \dim(\operatorname{Col}(\operatorname{adj}(A))) \le \dim(\operatorname{Null}(A)) = 1
$$

  Combining $\ge 1$ and $\le 1$ forces $\operatorname{rank}(\operatorname{adj}(A)) = 1$.
- **Case 3: $\operatorname{rank}(A) \le n - 2$**
  Every $(n-1) \times (n-1)$ minor of $A$ must be zero. Since each entry of $\operatorname{adj}(A)$ is $\pm$ an $(n-1) \times (n-1)$ minor of $A$, we have $\operatorname{adj}(A) = O$, and hence $\operatorname{rank}(\operatorname{adj}(A)) = 0$.
</SolutionRigorous>
</DualSolution>

<ExerciseCard id="l20-ex5" tier="L4" tierLabel="L4 · MIT 18.06 Exam Synthesis" title="Surface Area of Embedded Parallelograms and the Gram Determinant det(A^T A)">
In physics and engineering, we frequently encounter **rectangular matrices**—for example $A \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 2}$, whose two columns $\mathbf{a}_1, \mathbf{a}_2 \in \mathbb{R}^3$ span a tilted 2D parallelogram embedded in 3D space. Because $A$ is not square, $\det(A)$ is undefined.
1. Prove that the area of the parallelogram spanned by $\mathbf{a}_1, \mathbf{a}_2$ in $\mathbb{R}^3$ is:

$$
\operatorname{Area} = \sqrt{\det(A^T A)} = \|\mathbf{a}_1 \times \mathbf{a}_2\|_2
$$

2. Re-prove this formula using the **thin $QR$ factorization** $A = Q_1 R$, where $Q_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 2}$ has orthonormal columns ($Q_1^T Q_1 = I_2$) and $R \in \mathbb{R}^{2 \times 2}$ is upper triangular.

<ProgressiveHint buttonPrefix="Unlock Hint" hints={[
  "The $(i,j)$ entry of $A^T A$ is $\\mathbf{a}_i^T\\mathbf{a}_j$. Use the $2\\times 2$ determinant formula.",
  "If a column is zero, all three quantities vanish. For two nonzero columns, use $\\mathbf{a}_1\\cdot\\mathbf{a}_2 = \\|\\mathbf{a}_1\\|_2\\|\\mathbf{a}_2\\|_2\\cos\\theta$ and the cross-product formula.",

<DualSolution triggerLabel="Show Full Dual Solution (Intuition + Rigorous Proof)" intuitionLabel="Geometric Intuition" rigorousLabel="Rigorous Proof / Construction">
<SolutionIntuition>
The Gram matrix $A^T A$ records the inner products between the columns of $A$. Taking $\sqrt{\det(A^T A)}$ generalizes $|\det(A)|$ to $k$-dimensional surfaces in $\mathbb{R}^m$—and in differential geometry, it is the Riemannian area element $\sqrt{\det g}$.
</SolutionIntuition>

<SolutionRigorous>
**Proof of Part 1 (Vector Algebra):**
Write $A = [\mathbf{a}_1, \mathbf{a}_2]$ and compute $A^T A$:

$$
A^T A = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{a}_1^T \\ \mathbf{a}_2^T \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{a}_1 & \mathbf{a}_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \|\mathbf{a}_1\|^2 & \mathbf{a}_1 \cdot \mathbf{a}_2 \\ \mathbf{a}_1 \cdot \mathbf{a}_2 & \|\mathbf{a}_2\|^2 \end{bmatrix}
$$

Its determinant (the Gram determinant) is:

$$
\det(A^T A) = \|\mathbf{a}_1\|^2 \|\mathbf{a}_2\|^2 - (\mathbf{a}_1 \cdot \mathbf{a}_2)^2
$$

If either column is zero, the area, Gram determinant, and cross product are all zero. Otherwise let $\theta\in[0,\pi]$ be the angle between $\mathbf{a}_1$ and $\mathbf{a}_2$, so $\mathbf{a}_1 \cdot \mathbf{a}_2 = \|\mathbf{a}_1\| \|\mathbf{a}_2\| \cos\theta$:

$$
\det(A^T A) = \|\mathbf{a}_1\|^2 \|\mathbf{a}_2\|^2 (1 - \cos^2\theta) = \|\mathbf{a}_1\|^2 \|\mathbf{a}_2\|^2 \sin^2\theta
$$

Taking the square root gives:

$$
\sqrt{\det(A^T A)} = \|\mathbf{a}_1\| \|\mathbf{a}_2\| \sin\theta
$$

This is base $\|\mathbf{a}_1\|$ times perpendicular height $\|\mathbf{a}_2\|\sin\theta$—the exact area of the parallelogram, and also the magnitude of the cross product $\|\mathbf{a}_1 \times \mathbf{a}_2\|_2$.

**Proof of Part 2 (Thin $QR$ Factorization):**
If the columns are dependent, the area is zero and $A^T A$ is singular because $\operatorname{Null}(A^T A)=\operatorname{Null}(A)$. For independent columns, apply Gram-Schmidt to $A \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 2}$ to obtain the thin $QR$ factorization:

$$
A = Q_1 R = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{q}_1 & \mathbf{q}_2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} r_{11} & r_{12} \\ 0 & r_{22} \end{bmatrix}
$$

where $Q_1^T Q_1 = I_2$. Then:

$$
A^T A = (Q_1 R)^T (Q_1 R) = R^T (Q_1^T Q_1) R = R^T I_2 R = R^T R
$$

Now $R \in \mathbb{R}^{2 \times 2}$ is **square**! Taking determinants:

$$
\det(A^T A) = \det(R^T R) = \det(R^T) \det(R) = (\det R)^2
$$

Since $R$ is upper triangular with $r_{11}, r_{22} \gt 0$, $\det(R) = r_{11} r_{22}$, so:

$$
\sqrt{\det(A^T A)} = r_{11} r_{22}
$$

In Gram-Schmidt, $r_{11} = \|\mathbf{a}_1\|_2$ is the base length and $r_{22} = \|\mathbf{a}_2 - \operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf{q}_1}\mathbf{a}_2\|_2$ is the perpendicular altitude! This generalizes directly to the $k$-dimensional volume element of a regular parametrization with Jacobian $J\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times k}$ of full column rank, $dV = \sqrt{\det(J^T J)} \, du_1 \cdots du_k$ in $\mathbb{R}^m$.
</SolutionRigorous>

## 8. Frontier AI Reading: Volume Scaling in Normalizing Flows & Flow Matching

<FrontierAIReading
  id="AI-08"
  title="Normalizing Flows and Flow Matching: Jacobian Determinant Volume Scaling in Change-of-Variables"
  paperRef="Dinh et al. (ICLR 2017) RealNVP · Lipman et al. (ICLR 2023) Flow Matching for Generative Modeling"
>

In modern generative AI—from image synthesis and molecular generation to state-of-the-art voice cloning—the central task is to **model and sample from an unknown high-dimensional probability density $p_X(\mathbf{x})$**.

The classical geometric theorem from Lecture 20—that the determinant measures local volume scaling—is the mathematical soul of two landmark families of deep generative models: **Normalizing Flows** and **Flow Matching**.

---

### 1. Conservation of Probability and Change of Variables

Let $\mathbf{z}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},I_d)$ have density $p_Z$. Let $g_\theta:\mathbb{R}^d\to\mathbb{R}^d$ be a $C^1$ diffeomorphism with inverse $f_\theta$. For $\mathbf{x}=g_\theta(\mathbf{z})$, conservation of probability mass gives the change-of-variables formula

$$
p_X(\mathbf{x}) |d\mathbf{x}| = p_Z(\mathbf{z}) |d\mathbf{z}|
$$

Using the Jacobian volume-scaling rule from Section 3, $|d\mathbf{z}| = \left|\det\left(\frac{\partial f_\theta(\mathbf{x})}{\partial \mathbf{x}}\right)\right| |d\mathbf{x}|$. Canceling $|d\mathbf{x}|$ yields the **Change of Variables Formula** for probability densities:

$$
p_X(\mathbf{x}) = p_Z(f_\theta(\mathbf{x})) \cdot \left| \det J_{f_\theta}(\mathbf{x}) \right|
$$

For positive densities the per-sample log-likelihood is
$$
\log p_X(\mathbf{x}) = \log p_Z(f_\theta(\mathbf{x})) + \log \left| \det J_{f_\theta}(\mathbf{x}) \right|.
$$
Maximum likelihood maximizes the sum over observed samples. If $g_\theta$ compresses volume at $\mathbf{z}$, so $|\det J_{g_\theta}(\mathbf{z})|\lt 1$, the inverse factor is its reciprocal, giving $p_X(g_\theta(\mathbf{z}))\gt p_Z(\mathbf{z})$ when $p_Z(\mathbf{z})\gt 0$.

---

### 2. The Curse of Dimensionality and RealNVP's Block-Triangular Jacobian

For a $512 \times 512$ RGB image, the dimension is $d = 512 \times 512 \times 3 = 786{,}432$.
If $f_\theta$ were an unconstrained fully connected network, its Jacobian $J \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ would contain nearly $6 \times 10^{11}$ entries. Computing its determinant by Gaussian elimination would cost:

$$
\frac{2}{3}d^3 \approx 3.2 \times 10^{17} \text{ FLOPs}
$$

Running an $O(d^3)$ determinant inside every backpropagation step is computationally impossible.

**The AI Breakthrough (RealNVP Affine Coupling Layer)**:
Dinh et al. exploited the **block-triangular determinant property** from Lecture 18, Exercise 5 to design the **Affine Coupling Layer**. Split the input vector into two halves $\mathbf{x}=(\mathbf{x}_1^T,\mathbf{x}_2^T)^T$ with both blocks in $\mathbb{R}^{d/2}$ and $d$ even:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{z}_1 &= \mathbf{x}_1 \\
\mathbf{z}_2 &= \mathbf{x}_2 \odot \exp(s_\theta(\mathbf{x}_1)) + t_\theta(\mathbf{x}_1)
\end{aligned}
$$

Take $s_\theta,t_\theta:\mathbb{R}^{d/2}\to\mathbb{R}^{d/2}$ to be $C^1$. The exponential and $\odot$ act elementwise. The inverse recovers $\mathbf{x}_1=\mathbf{z}_1$ and then $\mathbf{x}_2=(\mathbf{z}_2-t_\theta(\mathbf{z}_1))\odot\exp(-s_\theta(\mathbf{z}_1))$. The data-to-latent Jacobian is

$$
J = \frac{\partial (\mathbf{z}_1, \mathbf{z}_2)}{\partial (\mathbf{x}_1, \mathbf{x}_2)} = \begin{bmatrix}
I_{d/2} & 0_{d/2\times d/2} \\
\frac{\partial \mathbf{z}_2}{\partial \mathbf{x}_1} & \operatorname{diag}(\exp(s_\theta(\mathbf{x}_1)))
\end{bmatrix}
$$

It is **block lower triangular**! By the block-triangular formula, the determinant of a block-triangular matrix is simply the product of the determinants of its diagonal blocks:

$$
\det(J) = \det(I_{d/2}) \cdot \det(\operatorname{diag}(\exp(s_\theta(\mathbf{x}_1)))) = 1 \cdot \prod_{i=1}^{d/2} \exp(s_{\theta, i}(\mathbf{x}_1))
$$

Taking the logarithm:

$$
\log |\det(J)| = \sum_{i=1}^{d/2} s_{\theta, i}(\mathbf{x}_1)
$$

Once the scale outputs are available, their sum costs $O(d)$.

---

### 3. Continuous Normalizing Flows and the Instantaneous Volume Rate

Flow Matching trains the velocity field of a continuous normalizing flow by regression. The flow itself is defined by the ODE
$$
\dot{\mathbf{x}}_t=\mathbf{v}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t,t), \qquad \mathbf{x}_t=\phi_t(\mathbf{x}_0), \qquad \phi_0(\mathbf{x}_0)=\mathbf{x}_0.
$$
Assume the velocity and its spatial derivative are continuous and the ODE has a unique forward and backward solution on the time interval in question. The flow maps have differentiable inverses. Their spatial Jacobian $J_t=D_{\mathbf{x}_0}\phi_t(\mathbf{x}_0)$ satisfies
$$
\dot J_t=D_{\mathbf{x}}\mathbf{v}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t,t)J_t,\qquad J_0=I_d.
$$

For a differentiable square matrix $M(t)$, Jacobi's formula gives $\frac{d}{dt}\det(M)=\operatorname{tr}(\operatorname{adj}(M)\dot M)$. When $M(t)$ is invertible, $\frac{d}{dt}\log|\det(M)|=\operatorname{tr}(M^{-1}\dot M)$. Substitute $\dot J_t$ and use cyclicity of the trace:
$$
\frac{d}{dt}\log|\det(J_t)|
=\operatorname{tr}\left(J_t^{-1}D_{\mathbf{x}}\mathbf{v}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t,t)J_t\right)
=\operatorname{div}\mathbf{v}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t,t).
$$

The divergence is the fractional rate of change of local volume. Probability conservation gives $p_t(\mathbf{x}_t)|\det(J_t)|=p_0(\mathbf{x}_0)$. Along a trajectory with positive density, differentiating gives
$$
\frac{d}{dt}\log p_t(\mathbf{x}_t)=-\operatorname{div}\mathbf{v}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t,t).
$$
Positive divergence expands volume and decreases density along the trajectory.

### 4. Hands-On Experiment: Numerical Verification of Triangular Jacobians and Trace-Divergence

The PyTorch script below verifies two key results:
1. The $O(d)$ analytical $\log |\det J|$ of a RealNVP coupling layer matches the full autograd Jacobian's `torch.linalg.slogdet` within floating-point tolerance;
2. A forward Euler map approximates the initial log-volume rate $\operatorname{tr}(D_{\mathbf{x}}\mathbf{v})$ with a first-order finite difference.

<CollapsibleCode title="Numerical Verification of RealNVP Block-Triangular Jacobian and Flow Matching Trace-Divergence" language="python">
```python
import torch
import torch.nn as nn

torch.manual_seed(42)
torch.set_default_dtype(torch.float64)

# =====================================================================
# Experiment 1: RealNVP Affine Coupling Block-Triangular Jacobian Check
# =====================================================================
class AffineCouplingLayer(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, dim):
        super().__init__()
        self.d = dim // 2
        # The scale-and-shift network can be non-invertible
        self.net = nn.Sequential(
            nn.Linear(self.d, 64),
            nn.Tanh(),
            nn.Linear(64, self.d * 2)
        )

    def forward(self, x):
        x1 = x[:self.d]
        x2 = x[self.d:]
        st = self.net(x1)
        s, t = st[:self.d], st[self.d:]
        # Coupling transformation
        z1 = x1
        z2 = x2 * torch.exp(s) + t
        z = torch.cat([z1, z2], dim=0)
        # Analytical log-det from the triangular diagonal: O(d) complexity
        log_det_analytic = torch.sum(s)
        return z, log_det_analytic

dim = 6
coupling = AffineCouplingLayer(dim)
x = torch.randn(dim)

# 1. Fast analytical computation
z, log_det_analytic = coupling(x)

# 2. Build the full Jacobian with autograd
def forward_fn(inp):
    out, _ = coupling(inp)
    return out

J = torch.autograd.functional.jacobian(forward_fn, x)
assert torch.count_nonzero(J[:dim // 2, dim // 2:]).item() == 0

# Print Jacobian structure: upper-right block must be all zeros
print("=== RealNVP Jacobian Structure J (6x6) ===")
print(torch.round(J, decimals=3))

# 3. Verify against slogdet
sign, log_det_full = torch.linalg.slogdet(J)
assert sign.item() == 1
diff = torch.abs(log_det_analytic - log_det_full).item()

print(f"\nAnalytic log|det(J)| = {log_det_analytic.item():.6f}")
print(f"Full slogdet(J)      = {log_det_full.item():.6f}")
print(f"Absolute error: {diff:.2e}")
assert diff < 1e-12, "Analytical and numerical log-det do not match!"

# =====================================================================
# Experiment 2: Jacobi's Formula & Velocity Divergence Trace (Continuous Flows)
# =====================================================================
print("\n=== Flow Matching: Time Derivative of log|det(J)| vs. Divergence Trace ===")

# Construct a 3D continuous velocity field v(x)
def velocity_field(x):
    return torch.stack([
        torch.sin(x[1]) + 0.5 * x[0],
        x[0] * x[2] - 0.2 * x[1],
        torch.cos(x[0]) + 0.8 * x[2]
    ])

x0 = torch.tensor([1.0, -0.5, 0.8])
dt = 1e-4

# One small forward Euler step: x + dt * v(x)
def flow_map(x):
    return x + dt * velocity_field(x)

# Jacobian of the Euler map at x0
J_dt = torch.autograd.functional.jacobian(flow_map, x0)
_, log_det_dt = torch.linalg.slogdet(J_dt)

# Numerical derivative: d/dt log|det(J)| at t=0
d_log_det_dt_numerical = (log_det_dt - 0.0) / dt

# Theoretical prediction: tr(nabla v) = div(v)
grad_v = torch.autograd.functional.jacobian(velocity_field, x0)
div_v_theory = torch.trace(grad_v)

print(f"Numerical d/dt log|det(J)|: {d_log_det_dt_numerical.item():.5f}")
print(f"Theoretical trace tr(∇v):   {div_v_theory.item():.5f}")
diff_flow = torch.abs(d_log_det_dt_numerical - div_v_theory).item()
print(f"Finite-difference error: {diff_flow:.2e}")
assert diff_flow < 2e-4
```
