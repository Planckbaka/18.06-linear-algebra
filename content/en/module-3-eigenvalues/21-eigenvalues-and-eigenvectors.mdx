# Lecture 21: Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors

## Introduction: The Leap from Static Equations to Dynamical Systems

Throughout the first two modules of this course, our attention has been fixed on **static linear systems**: solving $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$, Gaussian elimination $PA = LU$, least-squares projection $A^T A \hat{\mathbf{x}} = A^T \mathbf{b}$, and orthogonal factorization $A = QR$. In all of those problems, the input vector $\mathbf{x}$ and the output vector $\mathbf{b}$ often live in different spaces, or we simply care about solving once and for all for a static equilibrium state that satisfies the constraints.

Yet the essence of natural science and modern engineering is **dynamics**—how a system evolves. As we turn to Module 3, the central questions become:

1. **Discrete Dynamical Systems**: A matrix acts repeatedly, step after step, and we study the asymptotic behavior of the state sequence $\mathbf{u}_k = A^k \mathbf{u}_0$ as $k \to \infty$ (for example, Markov chain transitions, population growth models, and Fibonacci numbers).
2. **Continuous Dynamical Systems**: A first-order system of linear ordinary differential equations $\frac{d\mathbf{u}}{dt} = A\mathbf{u}(t)$, where the state evolves continuously in time (such as mechanical vibrations, electrical circuits, heat conduction, and wave equations).

Left multiplication by an invertible elementary matrix preserves $\operatorname{Null}(A)$. It can change the action of $A$ on other vectors and therefore change its eigenvalues.

An **eigenvector** of a square matrix is a nonzero vector whose span is invariant under $A$. The scalar by which $A$ acts on that line is the **eigenvalue**. For real vectors a negative eigenvalue reverses the arrow, and a zero eigenvalue sends it to $\mathbf{0}$.

---

## Geometric and Algebraic Foundations: Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors

When an $n \times n$ matrix $A$ multiplies an ordinary vector $\mathbf{v}$, the output $A\mathbf{v}$ usually changes both length and direction. But for certain very special nonzero vectors $\mathbf{x}$, matrix multiplication acts just like **pure scalar multiplication**!

<DefinitionCard label="Definition 21.1" title="Eigenvalues, Eigenvectors, and Eigenspaces">

Let $A \in \mathbb{C}^{n \times n}$ be an $n \times n$ square matrix. If there exists a scalar $\lambda \in \mathbb{C}$ and a **nonzero vector** $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{C}^n$ ($\mathbf{x} \neq \mathbf{0}$) such that

$$
A\mathbf{x} = \lambda \mathbf{x}
$$

then $\lambda$ is called an **eigenvalue** of $A$, and the nonzero vector $\mathbf{x}$ is called an **eigenvector** of $A$ corresponding to $\lambda$.

**Important Note on Number Fields**: Even when a real square matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ has entirely real entries, its eigenvalues $\lambda$ and eigenvectors $\mathbf{x}$ can still belong to the complex field $\mathbb{C}$.

The set of all eigenvectors corresponding to an eigenvalue $\lambda$, together with the zero vector $\mathbf{0}$, forms a subspace of $\mathbb{C}^n$ called the **eigenspace**, denoted $E_\lambda$:

$$
E_\lambda = \operatorname{Null}(A - \lambda I_n) = \{\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{C}^n : (A - \lambda I_n)\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}\}
$$

The dimension of this subspace is the **geometric multiplicity** of $\lambda$:

$$
g_\lambda = \dim(E_\lambda) = n - \operatorname{rank}(A - \lambda I_n) \ge 1
$$

</DefinitionCard>

<EigenvalueInvariantDirectionsDiagram caption="For the illustrated matrix, $A\mathbf{v}$ leaves the line through $\mathbf{v}$ and the origin, while each eigenvector maps to a scalar multiple of itself." />

### The Algebraic Core: Why Must the Determinant Be Zero?

We can move everything to one side of $A\mathbf{x} = \lambda \mathbf{x}$ to rewrite it as a homogeneous linear system:

$$
(A - \lambda I_n)\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}
$$

Here $I_n$ is the $n \times n$ identity matrix. This step is the critical bridge to the algebra of eigenvalues:
- If the matrix $(A - \lambda I_n)$ were **invertible**, multiplying both sides on the left by its inverse would force the unique solution $\mathbf{x} = (A - \lambda I_n)^{-1}\mathbf{0} = \mathbf{0}$.
- By definition, however, an eigenvector must be **nonzero** ($\mathbf{x} \neq \mathbf{0}$)!
- Therefore, for the homogeneous system to admit a nonzero solution, the matrix $(A - \lambda I_n)$ **must be singular** (non-invertible)!

A square matrix is singular if and only if its determinant is zero. That gives us the fundamental algebraic equation for eigenvalues:

$$
\det(A - \lambda I_n) = 0
$$

Conversely, singularity gives a nonzero solution, so $\lambda$ is an eigenvalue exactly when the determinant is zero. This degree-$n$ polynomial equation in $\lambda$ is called the **characteristic equation**, and the polynomial on the left, $p(\lambda) = \det(A - \lambda I_n)$, is the **characteristic polynomial** of $A$.

The **algebraic multiplicity** of $\lambda$ is its multiplicity as a root of $p$. If $g_\lambda=k$, extend a basis of $E_\lambda$ to a basis of $\mathbb{C}^n$. In that basis, $A$ is block upper triangular with leading block $\lambda I_k$, so $p(t)$ has a factor $(\lambda-t)^k$. Here a change of basis leaves the determinant unchanged by the product rule. Thus geometric multiplicity is at most algebraic multiplicity.

## Mental Eigenvalues by Geometry: Five Classic Operators Without Polynomials

In MIT 18.06, Prof. Gilbert Strang emphasizes a vital habit: before rushing to expand a messy determinant, strong geometric intuition should let you "see right through" a linear transformation and read off its eigenvalues immediately.

<GeometricEigenSpectraDiagram caption="Eigenvalues of projection onto a line ($1,0$), reflection across a line ($1,-1$), and a $90^\circ$ rotation ($\pm i$)." />

### 1. Orthogonal Projection Matrix $P$

Consider an orthogonal projection matrix $P = P^T = P^2$ onto a plane or a line in 3D space:
- Suppose a vector $\mathbf{x}$ already lies in that subspace ($\mathbf{x} \in \operatorname{Col}(P)$). Its projection is itself: $P\mathbf{x} = 1 \cdot \mathbf{x}$. Thus **$\lambda_1 = 1$ is an eigenvalue**, and every nonzero vector in the subspace is an eigenvector!
- Suppose a vector $\mathbf{x}$ is perpendicular to that subspace ($\mathbf{x} \in \operatorname{Null}(P) = \operatorname{Col}(P)^\perp$). Projection collapses it to the origin: $P\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0} = 0 \cdot \mathbf{x}$. Thus **$\lambda_2 = 0$ is an eigenvalue**, and every nonzero vector orthogonal to the subspace is an eigenvector for $\lambda = 0$!
- **Algebraic consistency**: Because $P^2 = P$, if $P\mathbf{x} = \lambda \mathbf{x}$ with $\mathbf{x}\ne\mathbf{0}$, then $P^2\mathbf{x} = \lambda^2 \mathbf{x} = \lambda \mathbf{x}$, so $\lambda^2 - \lambda = 0 \implies \lambda \in \{0, 1\}$. A projection matrix can only have eigenvalues $1$ or $0$.

### 2. Mirror Reflection Matrix $R = 2P - I$

Consider a reflection across a mirror plane:
- Every nonzero vector $\mathbf{x}$ in the mirror plane is untouched: $R\mathbf{x} = 1 \cdot \mathbf{x} \implies \lambda_1 = 1$.
- Every nonzero vector $\mathbf{x}$ perpendicular to the mirror plane is negated: $R\mathbf{x} = -1 \cdot \mathbf{x} \implies \lambda_2 = -1$.
- **Algebraic consistency**: Reflecting twice returns every vector to its starting point, so $R^2 = I \implies \lambda^2 = 1 \implies \lambda \in \{1, -1\}$.

### 3. Permutation Matrix $P_{12}$

Consider the $2 \times 2$ permutation matrix that swaps the two components of a vector in $\mathbb{R}^2$:

$$
P_{12} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad P_{12} \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} x_2 \\ x_1 \end{bmatrix}
$$

- When the two components are equal, swapping leaves the vector unchanged, giving $\lambda_1 = 1$:

$$
\mathbf{x}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad P_{12}\mathbf{x}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = 1 \cdot \mathbf{x}_1
$$

- When the two components are opposites, swapping negates the vector, giving $\lambda_2 = -1$:

$$
\mathbf{x}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad P_{12}\mathbf{x}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = -1 \cdot \mathbf{x}_2
$$

### 4. $90^\circ$ Pure Rotation Matrix $Q$

Consider the $2 \times 2$ matrix that rotates every vector in $\mathbb{R}^2$ counterclockwise by $90^\circ$:

$$
Q = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}
$$

- Geometrically, no real line in the plane can stay collinear with itself after a $90^\circ$ rotation! Therefore, **no real eigenvectors exist**.
- Algebraically, we solve the characteristic equation:

$$
\det(Q - \lambda I) = \det\begin{bmatrix} -\lambda & -1 \\ 1 & -\lambda \end{bmatrix} = \lambda^2 + 1 = 0 \implies \lambda = \pm i
$$

- The eigenvalues are purely imaginary: $\pm i$! Substituting $\lambda_1 = i$ into $(Q - iI)\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$ yields the complex eigenvector

$$
\mathbf{x}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -i \end{bmatrix}
$$

Complex conjugation gives $\mathbf{x}_2=(1,i)^T$ for $\lambda_2=-i$.

### 5. The Shift Theorem: $A + cI$

Let $A\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}$ and $c\in\mathbb{C}$. Suppose $A$ has eigenvalue $\lambda$ and eigenvector $\mathbf{x}$. What happens if we add a constant $c$ to every diagonal entry to form $A + cI$?

$$
(A + cI)\mathbf{x} = A\mathbf{x} + c\mathbf{x} = \lambda \mathbf{x} + c\mathbf{x} = (\lambda + c)\mathbf{x}
$$

**Conclusion**: The eigenvector $\mathbf{x}$ stays completely unchanged, while every eigenvalue shifts by $c$. In fact $(A+cI_n)-(\lambda+c)I_n=A-\lambda I_n$, so the corresponding eigenspaces are equal and the characteristic polynomial shifts by the same amount.

## Two Fundamental Invariants: Trace and Determinant

Expanding the characteristic polynomial reveals the two most important algebraic invariants of any square matrix.

<TheoremCard label="Theorem 21.1" title="Trace and Determinant Invariants">

Let $A \in \mathbb{C}^{n \times n}$ be an $n \times n$ matrix with complex eigenvalues $\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \dots, \lambda_n$ (counted with algebraic multiplicity). Then:

1. **The trace equals the sum of the eigenvalues**:
   $$
   \operatorname{tr}(A) = \sum_{i=1}^n a_{ii} = \sum_{i=1}^n \lambda_i
   $$
2. **The determinant equals the product of the eigenvalues**:
   $$
   \det(A) = \prod_{i=1}^n \lambda_i
   $$

</TheoremCard>

<ProofCollapse title="Rigorous Proof: Relating Roots and Coefficients of the Characteristic Polynomial">

By the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra, the degree-$n$ characteristic polynomial $p(\lambda) = \det(A - \lambda I_n)$ has exactly $n$ roots $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n$ in $\mathbb{C}$ counted with algebraic multiplicity, so it factors completely as

$$
p(\lambda) = \det(A - \lambda I_n) = (-1)^n \prod_{i=1}^n (\lambda - \lambda_i) = \prod_{i=1}^n (\lambda_i - \lambda)
$$

Expanding this factored product in powers of $-\lambda$ gives

$$
\prod_{i=1}^n (\lambda_i - \lambda) = (-\lambda)^n + (-\lambda)^{n-1} \left( \sum_{i=1}^n \lambda_i \right) + \text{terms of degree at most } n-2
$$

On the other hand, we can expand $\det(A - \lambda I_n)$ directly using the Leibniz permutation formula $\sum_{\sigma} \operatorname{sgn}(\sigma) \prod_{j=1}^n (A - \lambda I_n)_{j,\sigma(j)}$. Only the main-diagonal term ($\sigma = \text{id}$) contains all $n$ factors of $(a_{ii} - \lambda)$:

$$
\prod_{i=1}^n (a_{ii} - \lambda) = (-\lambda)^n + (-\lambda)^{n-1} \left( \sum_{i=1}^n a_{ii} \right) + \dots
$$

Every other permutation $\sigma \neq \text{id}$ must pick at least two off-diagonal entries (which contain no $\lambda$), so its degree in $\lambda$ is at most $n - 2$ and it contributes nothing to the $\lambda^n$ or $\lambda^{n-1}$ terms.

Comparing the coefficients of $(-\lambda)^{n-1}$ in both expressions immediately gives

$$
\sum_{i=1}^n \lambda_i = \sum_{i=1}^n a_{ii} = \operatorname{tr}(A)
$$

For the constant term, simply evaluate $p(\lambda)$ at $\lambda = 0$:

$$
p(0) = \det(A - 0 \cdot I_n) = \det(A)
$$

Substituting $\lambda = 0$ into the factored form gives

$$
p(0) = \prod_{i=1}^n (\lambda_i - 0) = \prod_{i=1}^n \lambda_i
$$

Equating the two proves $\det(A) = \prod_{i=1}^n \lambda_i$. $\blacksquare$

</ProofCollapse>

### Fast Quadratic Formula for $2 \times 2$ Matrices

For any $2 \times 2$ matrix

$$
A = \begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{bmatrix}
$$

we can write the characteristic polynomial directly in terms of its trace and determinant:

$$
\det\begin{bmatrix} a - \lambda & b \\ c & d - \lambda \end{bmatrix} = \lambda^2 - (a+d)\lambda + (ad-bc) = \lambda^2 - \operatorname{tr}(A)\lambda + \det(A)
$$

Set this polynomial equal to zero and apply the quadratic formula over $\mathbb{C}$:

$$
\lambda_{1, 2} = \frac{\operatorname{tr}(A) \pm \sqrt{(\operatorname{tr}(A))^2 - 4\det(A)}}{2}
$$

**Worked Example 1**: Consider the symmetric matrix

$$
A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}
$$

- $\operatorname{tr}(A) = 2 + 2 = 4$
- $\det(A) = 2 \times 2 - 1 \times 1 = 3$
- Characteristic equation: $\lambda^2 - 4\lambda + 3 = (\lambda - 3)(\lambda - 1) = 0 \implies \lambda_1 = 3,\; \lambda_2 = 1$.
- Quick check: $3 + 1 = 4 = \operatorname{tr}(A)$, and $3 \times 1 = 3 = \det(A)$!
- Finding the eigenvectors:
  - For $\lambda_1 = 3$:

$$
(A - 3I)\mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0} \implies \mathbf{x}=t\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\quad (t\in\mathbb{C})
$$

  - For $\lambda_2 = 1$:

$$
(A - I)\mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0} \implies \mathbf{x}=t\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}\quad (t\in\mathbb{C})
$$

  - Choosing $t=1$ in each case gives real eigenvectors $\mathbf{x}_1$ and $\mathbf{x}_2$ with $\mathbf{x}_1^T\mathbf{x}_2 = 1(1) + 1(-1) = 0$: the two eigenvectors are perpendicular! (Distinct eigenspaces of a real symmetric matrix are orthogonal, as we will prove in Lecture 26.)

**Worked Example 2 (Rank-One Matrix)**. Let $\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^n$ be nonzero and set $A=\mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^T$. Its nullspace is given by $\mathbf{v}^T\mathbf{x}=0$ and has dimension $n-1$. For $n\ge 2$, this accounts for at least $n-1$ zero roots of the characteristic polynomial. The trace determines the remaining root:
$$
\operatorname{tr}(A)=\sum_{i=1}^n u_i v_i=\mathbf{v}^T\mathbf{u},\qquad A\mathbf{u}=(\mathbf{v}^T\mathbf{u})\mathbf{u}.
$$
If $\mathbf{v}^T\mathbf{u}\ne 0$, it is a simple eigenvalue and any remaining roots are zero. If $\mathbf{v}^T\mathbf{u}=0$, then $A^2=0$ and all $n$ roots are zero, with geometric multiplicity $n-1$. For $n=1$, the sole eigenvalue is the nonzero scalar $\mathbf{v}^T\mathbf{u}$.

## Classic Conceptual Traps and Clarifications

<PitfallAlert title="Common Pitfalls & Transcript Errata">

### 1. Fatal Error: Thinking Row Elimination Preserves Eigenvalues
Gaussian row elimination is our primary tool for solving $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$; multiplying on the left by elementary matrices $E$ preserves the nullspace $\operatorname{Null}(A)$ and the rank. However, **elementary row operations can change eigenvalues and eigenspaces!**

$$
A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \xrightarrow{R_1 \leftrightarrow R_2} U = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}
$$

The original matrix $A$ has eigenvalues $\lambda = \pm 1$ and trace $0$. After a single row swap, the echelon matrix $U$ has eigenvalues $1, 1$ and trace $2$!
- The diagonal entries of a triangular matrix are indeed its own eigenvalues;
- But the **pivots** produced by eliminating a general matrix $A$ are **not in general** the eigenvalues of $A$. With row additions and $k$ row swaps, $\det(A)=(-1)^k\det(U)$.

### 2. The Scalar $0$ Can Be an Eigenvalue, but the Zero Vector $\mathbf{0}$ Can Never Be an Eigenvector
- $\lambda = 0$ is a valid eigenvalue $\iff A\mathbf{x} = 0\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$ has a nonzero solution $\mathbf{x} \neq \mathbf{0} \iff A$ is singular (non-invertible) $\iff \det(A) = 0$.
- By contrast, the zero vector $\mathbf{0}$ can **never** be an eigenvector! Because $A\mathbf{0} = \lambda \mathbf{0}$ holds trivially for every scalar $\lambda$, allowing $\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$ would make every number an eigenvalue of every matrix, emptying the definition of all meaning.

### 3. Eigenvalues of Sums and Products: Why pairwise rules fail
Intuition often tempts students into thinking that adding or multiplying two matrices adds or multiplies their eigenvalues. That is dead wrong!
Take two strictly triangular matrices:

$$
A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad B = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}
$$

Both $A$ and $B$ have eigenvalues $\{0, 0\}$. Yet their sum is

$$
A + B = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}
$$

whose eigenvalues are $\{1, -1\}$! Clearly $0 + 0 \neq 1$ and $0 + 0 \neq -1$. Likewise, their product is

$$
AB = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}
$$

which has eigenvalues $\{1, 0\}$, even though $0 \times 0 \neq 1$!
If $\mathbf{x}\ne\mathbf{0}$ is an eigenvector of both matrices, with eigenvalues $\alpha$ for $A$ and $\beta$ for $B$, then it gives $\alpha+\beta$ for $A+B$ and $\alpha\beta$ for $AB$. A common eigenbasis gives a consistent pairing for the full spectra. Commuting matrices that are both diagonalizable over the chosen field have a common eigenbasis. Indeed, $B$ preserves each eigenspace of $A$, and its restriction to each is diagonalizable because its minimal polynomial divides that of $B$. Commutativity alone does not guarantee an eigenbasis, as the nonzero nilpotent matrix $A$ above commutes with $I_2$.


### 4. Real Matrices Need Not Have Real Eigenvalues or Eigenvectors
A real matrix is not guaranteed to have invariant directions inside the real plane $\mathbb{R}^2$—the $90^\circ$ rotation $Q$ is the classic example. We must extend our field to $\mathbb{C}$, where the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra guarantees $n$ roots of the characteristic polynomial. Whenever a real matrix has nonreal eigenvalues, they must appear in **complex conjugate pairs** $a \pm bi$.

</PitfallAlert>

<DualSolution
  triggerLabel="Show Full Dual Solution (Intuition + Rigorous Proof)"
  intuitionLabel="Geometric Intuition"
  rigorousLabel="Rigorous Proof / Construction"
>
<SolutionIntuition>

For $A=\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}^T$, the image is the line through $\mathbf{u}$. The plane perpendicular to $\mathbf{u}$ collapses to the zero vector, so there must be two eigenvalues equal to $\lambda = 0$. The one direction that is not flattened is $\mathbf{u}$ itself, and along that line the scaling factor is simply its squared length $\mathbf{u}^T \mathbf{u} = 1^2 + 2^2 + 2^2 = 9$.

</SolutionIntuition>
<SolutionRigorous>

**Part 1**:
Multiply $A$ by $\mathbf{u}$:
$$
A\mathbf{u} = (\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}^T)\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{u}(\mathbf{u}^T \mathbf{u})
$$
Since $\mathbf{u}^T \mathbf{u} = 1^2 + 2^2 + 2^2 = 9$, we get $A\mathbf{u} = 9\mathbf{u}$. Thus $\lambda_1 = 9$ is an eigenvalue with eigenvector $\mathbf{u}$.

Because $\operatorname{rank}(A) = 1$, the Rank-Nullity Theorem gives
$$
\dim(\operatorname{Null}(A)) = 3 - 1 = 2
$$
For any vector $\mathbf{x} \in \operatorname{Null}(A)$ (meaning $\mathbf{u}^T \mathbf{x} = 0$), we have $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{u}(\mathbf{u}^T \mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{0} = 0 \cdot \mathbf{x}$.
Here $0$ has geometric multiplicity $2$, so at least two characteristic roots are zero. The remaining root is $9$ by the trace, giving algebraic multiplicity $2$ for $0$.
Trace check: $\operatorname{tr}(A) = \sum u_i^2 = 9 = 9 + 0 + 0$.

**Part 2**:
The projection matrix is $P=\frac{1}{9}A=\frac{1}{9}\begin{bmatrix}1&2&2\\2&4&4\\2&4&4\end{bmatrix}$. Its eigenspaces follow by scaling the eigenvalues of $A$.
- $P\mathbf{u} = \frac{1}{9}(9\mathbf{u}) = 1 \cdot \mathbf{u} \implies \lambda_1 = 1$, with eigenspace

$$
E_1 = \operatorname{span}\left\{\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}\right\}
$$

- For every $\mathbf{x} \perp \mathbf{u}$, $P\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0} = 0 \cdot \mathbf{x} \implies \lambda_2 = \lambda_3 = 0$. The eigenspace is $E_0 = \operatorname{Null}(\mathbf{u}^T)$; solving $x_1 + 2x_2 + 2x_3 = 0$ gives the basis

$$
\mathbf{x}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{x}_3 = \begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}
$$

</SolutionRigorous>
</DualSolution>

<SolutionIntuition>

Gaussian elimination multiplies on the left by elementary matrices $E$. That preserves the solutions to $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$ (the nullspace), but it twists the rotations and stretches of $A$ in space. Even a single row swap can change the spectrum.

</SolutionIntuition>
<SolutionRigorous>

Counterexample: Take the $2 \times 2$ permutation matrix
$$
A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}
$$
Its characteristic equation is $\det(A - \lambda I) = \lambda^2 - 1 = 0 \implies \lambda_1 = 1,\; \lambda_2 = -1$, with trace $\operatorname{tr}(A) = 0 + 0 = 0$.

Eliminating $A$ requires a single row exchange $R_1 \leftrightarrow R_2$, producing the upper-triangular matrix
$$
U = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = I_2
$$
The pivots of $U$ are $d_1 = 1,\; d_2 = 1$, and the eigenvalues of $U$ are $\lambda_1 = 1,\; \lambda_2 = 1$ with trace $\operatorname{tr}(U) = 2$.

Clearly $A$ and $U$ have different spectra. The statement is false.

</SolutionRigorous>
</DualSolution>

True or false? Let $A,B\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}$. If $\lambda_A$ is an eigenvalue of $A$ and $\lambda_B$ is an eigenvalue of $B$, then $\lambda_A + \lambda_B$ must be an eigenvalue of $A + B$, and $\lambda_A \lambda_B$ must be an eigenvalue of $AB$.

<TrueFalseInteractive
  id="l21-tf2"
  correct={false}
  trueLabel="True"
  falseLabel="False (Counterexample Exists)"
  quickFeedback={"False. A common eigenvector gives compatible eigenvalues for addition and multiplication. Arbitrarily chosen eigenvalues need not have that pairing."}
/>

<ProgressiveHint
  buttonPrefix="Unlock Hint"
  hints={[
    "A common eigenvector gives a compatible pair of eigenvalues. Does the statement guarantee one for the chosen pair?",
    "Try two strictly triangular matrices: $A = \\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\\\ 0 & 0 \\end{bmatrix}$ and $B = \\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 \\\\ 1 & 0 \\end{bmatrix}$."
  ]}
/>

<DualSolution
  triggerLabel="Show Full Dual Solution (Intuition + Rigorous Proof)"
  intuitionLabel="Geometric Intuition"
  rigorousLabel="Rigorous Proof / Construction"
>
<SolutionIntuition>

A common eigenvector stays on the same line under both operators. On that line the operators act as scalars, so their sum and product give the expected eigenvalues. With arbitrarily selected eigenvalues, that common line may be absent.

</SolutionIntuition>
<SolutionRigorous>

Construct an explicit counterexample by taking
$$
A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad B = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}
$$
$A$ is upper triangular with eigenvalues $\lambda(A) = \{0, 0\}$, and its eigenvectors satisfy $x_2 = 0$, spanning

$$
\operatorname{span}\left\{\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\right\}
$$

$B$ is lower triangular with eigenvalues $\lambda(B) = \{0, 0\}$, and its eigenvectors satisfy $x_1 = 0$, spanning

$$
\operatorname{span}\left\{\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\right\}
$$

Adding the two matrices gives
$$
A + B = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}
$$
whose characteristic equation is $\lambda^2 - 1 = 0 \implies \lambda(A + B) = \{1, -1\}$, whereas $\lambda_A + \lambda_B = 0 + 0 = 0 \neq \pm 1$.

Multiplying them gives
$$
AB = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}
$$
The predicted product $0$ is still an eigenvalue of $AB$, although the full list of eigenvalues differs from the pairwise products. To disprove the product claim itself, shift both factors: let $A'=I_2+A$ and $B'=I_2+B$. Each has only the eigenvalue $1$, but
$$
A'B'=\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&1\end{bmatrix},\qquad \det(A'B'-I_2)=-1.
$$
Thus $1$ is not an eigenvalue of the product. Both claims fail in general.
</SolutionRigorous>
</DualSolution>

  title="Spectral Theorems for Transposes, Powers, Inverses, and Shifts"
>

Suppose $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ has eigenvalue $\lambda\in\mathbb{C}$ with corresponding eigenvector $\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{C}^n\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}$.

1. Prove that $A$ and $A^T$ have the exact same characteristic polynomial—and therefore the exact same eigenvalues—and give an example where their eigenspaces differ.
2. If $A$ is invertible, find the eigenvalue of $A^{-1}$ corresponding to $\mathbf{x}$.
3. For any positive integer $k$, find the eigenvalue of $A^k$ corresponding to $\mathbf{x}$.
4. For any scalar $c \in \mathbb{R}$, find the eigenvalue of $A+cI_n$ corresponding to $\mathbf{x}$.

<ProgressiveHint
  buttonPrefix="Unlock Hint"
  hints={[
    "Use the fundamental determinant identity $\\det(M^T) = \\det(M)$ with $M = A - \\lambda I_n$.",
    "Multiply $A\\mathbf{x} = \\lambda\\mathbf{x}$ on the left by $A^{-1}$, noting that an invertible matrix must have $\\lambda \\neq 0$."
  ]}
/>

<DualSolution
  triggerLabel="Show Full Dual Solution (Intuition + Rigorous Proof)"
  intuitionLabel="Geometric Intuition"
  rigorousLabel="Rigorous Proof / Construction"
>
<SolutionIntuition>
Transposition preserves the determinant of $A-\lambda I_n$. The eigenvector equations use the right and left nullspaces of that shifted matrix, which can differ.
</SolutionIntuition>
<SolutionRigorous>

**Part 1**:
Let $p_A(\lambda) = \det(A - \lambda I_n)$. Since transposing preserves the determinant of any square matrix,
$$
p_{A^T}(\lambda) = \det(A^T - \lambda I_n) = \det((A - \lambda I_n)^T) = \det(A - \lambda I_n) = p_A(\lambda)
$$
Their characteristic polynomials are identical, so their eigenvalues and algebraic multiplicities match completely.

Let $M=A-\lambda I_n$. The eigenspaces of $A$ and $A^T$ for $\lambda$ are $\operatorname{Null}(M)$ and $\operatorname{Null}(M^T)$. The second consists of vectors satisfying $\mathbf{y}^TM=\mathbf{0}^T$. For real $\lambda$ and real vectors, it is $\operatorname{Col}(M)^\perp$. Over $\mathbb{C}$ with the Hermitian inner product it is $\operatorname{Col}(\overline M)^\perp$, since $M^T=(\overline M)^H$. The two eigenspaces can differ. For example $A=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{bmatrix}$ has $E_0=\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf{e}_1\}$, whereas $A^T$ has $E_0=\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf{e}_2\}$.

**Part 2**:
If $A$ is invertible, then $\det(A) = \prod \lambda_i \neq 0$, so every eigenvalue satisfies $\lambda \neq 0$.
Multiplying $A\mathbf{x} = \lambda \mathbf{x}$ on the left by $A^{-1}$ gives
$$
A^{-1}(A\mathbf{x}) = A^{-1}(\lambda \mathbf{x}) \implies \mathbf{x} = \lambda A^{-1}\mathbf{x} \implies A^{-1}\mathbf{x} = \frac{1}{\lambda}\mathbf{x}
$$
Thus $A^{-1}$ has eigenvalue $\frac{1}{\lambda}$ with the same eigenvector $\mathbf{x}$.

**Part 3**:
By mathematical induction on $k$:
The base case $k = 1$ holds by assumption. If $A^k \mathbf{x} = \lambda^k \mathbf{x}$, then
$$
A^{k+1}\mathbf{x} = A(A^k \mathbf{x}) = A(\lambda^k \mathbf{x}) = \lambda^k (A\mathbf{x}) = \lambda^k (\lambda \mathbf{x}) = \lambda^{k+1}\mathbf{x}
$$
Thus $A^k$ has eigenvalue $\lambda^k$ with the same eigenvector $\mathbf{x}$.

**Part 4**:
By linearity of matrix addition:
$$
(A + cI_n)\mathbf{x} = A\mathbf{x} + cI_n \mathbf{x} = \lambda \mathbf{x} + c\mathbf{x} = (\lambda + c)\mathbf{x}
$$
Thus $A + cI_n$ has eigenvalue $\lambda + c$ with the same eigenvector $\mathbf{x}$.

</SolutionRigorous>
</DualSolution>

<ExerciseCard
  id="l21-ex5"
  tier="L4"
  tierLabel="L4 · MIT 18.06 Exam Synthesis"
  title="MIT 18.06 Classic: Shifted All-Ones Matrix and Complete Graph Spectrum"
>

For $n\ge 2$, consider the $n \times n$ all-ones matrix $J_n = \mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^T$, where

$$
\mathbf{1} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & \dots & 1 \end{bmatrix}^T
$$

and the adjacency matrix of the complete graph $K_n = J_n - I_n$ (zeros on the main diagonal, ones everywhere else).

1. Using only nullspace dimension and the trace (no determinant expansion!), find all eigenvalues of $J_n$ along with their algebraic and geometric multiplicities.
2. Apply the Shift Theorem to write down all eigenvalues and eigenvectors of $K_n$.
3. Compute $\det(K_n)$, and verify your formula for $n = 3$ and $n = 4$.

<ProgressiveHint
  buttonPrefix="Unlock Hint"
  hints={[
    "$\\operatorname{rank}(J_n) = 1$. What is the dimension of its nullspace, and what happens when $J_n$ multiplies any vector whose entries sum to zero?",
    "Since $K_n = J_n - I_n$, apply the Shift Theorem directly: $\\lambda(K_n) = \\lambda(J_n) - 1$."
  ]}
/>

<DualSolution
  triggerLabel="Show Full Dual Solution (Intuition + Rigorous Proof)"
  intuitionLabel="Geometric Intuition"
  rigorousLabel="Rigorous Proof / Construction"
>
<SolutionIntuition>

The all-ones matrix is the purest rank-1 symmetric matrix. Every row sums the entries of the all-ones vector $\mathbf{1}$, scaling it by $n$. Meanwhile, every vector perpendicular to $\mathbf{1}$ (whose entries sum to zero, such as $[1, -1, 0, \dots]^T$) is annihilated by every row—and there is an entire $(n-1)$-dimensional hyperplane of such vectors! Subtracting $I_n$ simply shifts the entire spectrum down by $1$.

</SolutionIntuition>
<SolutionRigorous>

**Part 1**:
Test the all-ones vector $\mathbf{1}$ on $J_n = \mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^T$:
$$
J_n \mathbf{1} = (\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^T)\mathbf{1} = \mathbf{1}(\mathbf{1}^T \mathbf{1}) = n\mathbf{1}
$$
Thus $\lambda_1 = n$ is an eigenvalue with eigenvector $\mathbf{1}$.

Since $\operatorname{rank}(J_n) = 1$, the Rank-Nullity Theorem gives
$$
\dim(\operatorname{Null}(J_n)) = n - \operatorname{rank}(J_n) = n - 1
$$
For every nonzero vector $\mathbf{x} \in \operatorname{Null}(J_n) = \{\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n : \mathbf{1}^T \mathbf{x} = \sum x_i = 0\}$, we have
$$
J_n \mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0} = 0 \cdot \mathbf{x}
$$
Hence $0$ has geometric multiplicity $n-1$ and at least that algebraic multiplicity. The trace gives the remaining eigenvalue $n$, which has both multiplicities equal to $1$.
The spectrum of $J_n$ is
$$
\operatorname{spec}(J_n) = \{n, \underbrace{0, 0, \dots, 0}_{n-1 \text{ times}}\}
$$
Trace check: $\operatorname{tr}(J_n) = \sum_{i=1}^n 1 = n = n + 0 + \dots + 0$.

**Part 2**:
For $K_n = J_n - I_n$, the Shift Theorem says that if $J_n \mathbf{x} = \lambda \mathbf{x}$, then $K_n \mathbf{x} = (J_n - I_n)\mathbf{x} = (\lambda - 1)\mathbf{x}$.
Therefore, the eigenvalues of $K_n$ are
$$
\lambda_1(K_n) = n - 1
$$
$$
\lambda_2(K_n) = \dots = \lambda_n(K_n) = 0 - 1 = -1 \quad (\text{algebraic multiplicity } n-1)
$$
The eigenspaces are unchanged. For $\lambda=n-1$, all nonzero multiples of $\mathbf{1}$ are eigenvectors. For $\lambda=-1$, the eigenvectors are all nonzero vectors satisfying $\sum_i x_i=0$.

**Part 3**:
The determinant equals the product of all eigenvalues:
$$
\det(K_n) = \prod_{i=1}^n \lambda_i(K_n) = (n - 1) \cdot (-1)^{n-1}
$$
Checking small values:
- For $n = 3$:

$$
K_3 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \det(K_3) = (3 - 1)(-1)^2 = 2 \times 1 = 2
$$

- For $n = 4$: $\det(K_4) = (4 - 1)(-1)^3 = 3 \times (-1) = -3$.

For an independent check, add rows $2$ through $n$ of $K_n$ to row $1$ and factor out $n-1$ from that row. Row $1$ is now all ones. Subtract it from each other row to get an upper-triangular matrix with diagonal $1,-1,\dots,-1$. Thus elimination also gives $2$ when $n=3$ and $-3$ when $n=4$.

</SolutionRigorous>
</DualSolution>
