If the entire MIT 18.06 Linear Algebra course is a climb toward the summit of matrix structure, then the **Singular Value Decomposition (SVD)** is the moment we stand at the peak and look out over the whole landscape.

Let us look back at the road of matrix factorizations we have traveled from Lecture 22 to Lecture 29:
- Eigenvalue diagonalization $A = S\Lambda S^{-1}$ for a general square matrix (Lecture 22) is remarkably powerful, yet it hits two immovable walls: first, it **only applies to square matrices** ($m = n$), leaving us helpless before the rectangular matrices $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ ($m \ne n$) that dominate engineering and data science; second, even for a square matrix, once it becomes defective (fewer than $n$ linearly independent eigenvectors, Lecture 29), $S$ is not invertible and diagonalization breaks down completely. Moreover, the eigenvector matrix $S$ is generally **not orthogonal**.
- The Spectral Theorem for real symmetric matrices $A = Q\Lambda Q^T$ (Lecture 26) is perfection itself: any real symmetric matrix always possesses an **orthonormal eigenbasis** $Q$ that diagonalizes it. Yet it demands the stringent symmetry condition $A = A^T$.

Now, Prof. Gilbert Strang poses the ultimate question: **For any matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ on Earth—with no symmetry whatsoever, perhaps tall or wide, or even rank-deficient—can we still diagonalize it completely using only "orthogonal rotations/reflections" and "pure diagonal stretching"?**

The answer is a resounding **YES**! The only price we pay is that we stop insisting that the input space $\mathbb{R}^n$ and the output space $\mathbb{R}^m$ share the same basis; instead, **we allow the input space to have its own orthonormal basis $V$, and the output space to have its own orthonormal basis $U$**. That is the Singular Value Decomposition, $A = U\Sigma V^T$!

---

## 1. Geometric Intuition: Why Does Every Matrix Map the Unit Circle to an Ellipse?

The deepest entry point into $A = U\Sigma V^T$ is not memorizing a computational recipe, but staring at the geometry.

Consider any linear transformation in the plane, $A \in \mathbb{R}^{2 \times 2}$, acting on the unit circle $\|\mathbf{x}\|_2 = 1$ in the input space $\mathbb{R}^2$:
- Every point $\mathbf{x}$ on the unit circle is mapped to $A\mathbf{x}$, and all these image points together trace out an **ellipse** in the output space (degenerating to a line segment if $\operatorname{rank}(A) = 1$, or collapsing to the origin if $A = O$).
- Ask yourself: on that output ellipse, there is a pair of perpendicular principal semi-axes (major semi-axis direction $\mathbf{u}_1$ and minor semi-axis direction $\mathbf{u}_2$, of lengths $\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge 0$). Which two directions on the input unit circle did they come from?
- The breathtaking geometric fact is: **their preimages $\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2$ on the input unit circle are also strictly perpendicular** ($\mathbf{v}_1 \perp \mathbf{v}_2$)!

In other words, even though the matrix $A$ itself may combine shearing, scaling, and rotation (so it generally **does not preserve angles**), **there always exists a pair of orthogonal unit directions $(\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2)$ in the input space whose images $(A\mathbf{v}_1, A\mathbf{v}_2) = (\sigma_1 \mathbf{u}_1, \sigma_2 \mathbf{u}_2)$ have zero inner product. When $\operatorname{rank}(A)\lt 2$, at least one image is zero and has no direction**!

This immediately decomposes any linear map $A$ into a geometrically pure three-step pipeline:
1. **Right orthogonal matrix $V^T$ (input space alignment)**: Orthogonal transformations strictly preserve lengths and angles (Lecture 17). Left-multiplying by $V^T$ rotates (or reflects) the input space's natural orthogonal principal axes $\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2$ to align with the standard coordinate axes $\mathbf{e}_1, \mathbf{e}_2$;
2. **Diagonal singular value matrix $\Sigma$ (pure axial scaling & dimensional embedding)**: Along the aligned coordinate axes, stretch or compress by factors $\sigma_1, \sigma_2$ (crushing any nullspace dimensions to $0$, and padding with zeros into higher dimensions if $m \gt n$), turning the unit circle into an axis-aligned ellipse;
3. **Left orthogonal matrix $U$ (output space orientation)**: Finally, apply an orthogonal rotation (or reflection) in the output space to place the axis-aligned semi-axes $\sigma_1\mathbf{e}_1, \sigma_2\mathbf{e}_2$ into their final target directions $\sigma_1\mathbf{u}_1, \sigma_2\mathbf{u}_2$.

<SvdCircleToEllipsePipelineDiagram caption="Three-step geometric pipeline of the SVD: Vᵀ aligns the orthogonal input axes to the standard axes, Σ stretches them into an axis-aligned ellipse, and U rotates the ellipse to its final output orientation." />

To summarize this geometric picture in one line:
- In standard eigendecomposition $A\mathbf{x} = \lambda \mathbf{x}$ for a square matrix, we demand that the output direction stay **parallel** to the input direction (using one single basis);
- In the Singular Value Decomposition, we seek **a matched pair of orthonormal bases** such that each input orthogonal direction $\mathbf{v}_i$ maps precisely to a scaled output orthogonal direction $\mathbf{u}_i$:

$$
A \mathbf{v}_i = \sigma_i \mathbf{u}_i \quad (1 \le i \le r), \qquad A \mathbf{v}_i = \mathbf{0} \quad (r+1 \le i \le n)
$$

Stacking all $n$ column vector equations side by side into a matrix product immediately gives $A V = U \Sigma$. Since the square orthogonal matrix $V$ satisfies $V^{-1} = V^T$, right-multiplying by $V^T$ yields the golden formula of the Singular Value Decomposition: $A = U \Sigma V^T$!

## 2. Rigorous Definition and Existence Theorem

Guided by this geometric intuition, let us now establish the exact matrix shapes, rank constraints, compact form, and constructive existence theorem for the SVD.

<DefinitionCard label="Definition" title="Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) & Singular Values">

Let $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ be any real matrix of rank $r = \operatorname{rank}(A)$ (clearly $0 \le r \le \min(m, n)$).

1. **Full SVD**: Factor $A$ as the product of three matrices:
   $$
   A = U \Sigma V^T
   $$
   where:
   - $U = [\mathbf{u}_1 \;\; \mathbf{u}_2 \;\; \cdots \;\; \mathbf{u}_m] \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times m}$ is an **orthogonal matrix** ($U^T U = U U^T = I_m$), whose columns $\mathbf{u}_i \in \mathbb{R}^m$ are called the **left singular vectors** of $A$;
   - $V = [\mathbf{v}_1 \;\; \mathbf{v}_2 \;\; \cdots \;\; \mathbf{v}_n] \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ is an **orthogonal matrix** ($V^T V = V V^T = I_n$), whose columns $\mathbf{v}_j \in \mathbb{R}^n$ are called the **right singular vectors** of $A$;
   - $\Sigma \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ is a rectangular diagonal matrix of the same shape as $A$, whose top-left $r \times r$ block is a strictly positive diagonal matrix $\Sigma_r = \operatorname{diag}(\sigma_1, \sigma_2, \dots, \sigma_r)$ and whose remaining entries are all $0$:
     $$
     \Sigma = \begin{bmatrix} \Sigma_r & O_{r \times (n-r)} \\ O_{(m-r) \times r} & O_{(m-r) \times (n-r)} \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \dots \ge \sigma_r \gt 0
     $$
     Here $\sigma_1, \dots, \sigma_r$ are called the **nonzero singular values** of $A$ (by convention, if we pad out to $\min(m,n)$ singular values, then $\sigma_{r+1} = \dots = \sigma_{\min(m,n)} = 0$).

2. **Compact (Rank-$r$) SVD**: The term “thin” here means retaining only the positive singular values. An economy SVD can also retain all $\min(m,n)$ singular values, including zeros. Because rows and columns of $\Sigma$ beyond index $r$ are all zero, dropping the corresponding zero-block vectors $\mathbf{u}_{r+1},\dots,\mathbf{u}_m$ and $\mathbf{v}_{r+1},\dots,\mathbf{v}_n$ leaves the product unchanged:
   $$
   A = U_r \Sigma_r V_r^T = \sum_{i=1}^r \sigma_i \mathbf{u}_i \mathbf{v}_i^T
   $$
   where $U_r = [\mathbf{u}_1 \;\; \cdots \;\; \mathbf{u}_r] \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times r}$ (with $U_r^T U_r = I_r$) and $V_r = [\mathbf{v}_1 \;\; \cdots \;\; \mathbf{v}_r] \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times r}$ (with $V_r^T V_r = I_r$). On the right, $\sum_{i=1}^r \sigma_i \mathbf{u}_i \mathbf{v}_i^T$ writes $A$ as a sum of $r$ mutually orthogonal **rank-one outer-product matrices**!

*(Note: If $A \in \mathbb{C}^{m \times n}$ is a complex matrix, we simply replace orthogonal matrices with unitary matrices $U^H U = I_m, V^H V = I_n$, giving $A = U\Sigma V^H$, while the singular values $\sigma_i \ge 0$ remain **nonnegative real numbers**.)*

</DefinitionCard>

Why does such a decomposition exist for **every** real matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$—no matter how lopsided, singular, or defective? The answer lies hidden in two real symmetric matrices built directly from $A$: $A^T A$ and $A A^T$!

Observe first that if $A = U\Sigma V^T$ already holds, then using orthogonality $U^T U = I_m$ and $V^T V = I_n$, we immediately obtain:

$$
A^T A = (V \Sigma^T U^T)(U \Sigma V^T) = V (\Sigma^T \Sigma) V^T
$$

$$
A A^T = (U \Sigma V^T)(V \Sigma^T U^T) = U (\Sigma \Sigma^T) U^T
$$

This reveals a striking connection:
- The columns of $V$ must be orthonormal eigenvectors of the real symmetric positive semidefinite matrix $A^T A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$;
- The columns of $U$ must be orthonormal eigenvectors of the real symmetric positive semidefinite matrix $A A^T \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times m}$;
- The squared nonzero singular values $\sigma_1^2, \dots, \sigma_r^2$ are precisely the shared nonzero eigenvalues of both $A^T A$ and $A A^T$!

**Yet here is a life-or-death detail**: if you diagonalize $A^T A$ and $A A^T$ independently to assemble $V$ and $U$, you will almost certainly suffer sign mismatches (or even subspace rotation mismatches for repeated singular values)! The rigorous existence theorem tells us: **the left singular vectors $\mathbf{u}_i$ must be locked directly to the right singular vectors $\mathbf{v}_i$ via $A\mathbf{v}_i$**.

<TheoremCard label="Theorem" title="Existence & Constructive Characterization of the SVD">

For every real matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ with $\operatorname{rank}(A) = r$, a Singular Value Decomposition $A = U\Sigma V^T$ always exists. Specifically:

1. **Uniqueness of Singular Values**: The nonzero singular values $\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \dots \ge \sigma_r \gt 0$ are uniquely determined by $A$; they are the positive square roots ($\sigma_i = \sqrt{\lambda_i}$) of all $r$ positive eigenvalues of the real symmetric positive semidefinite matrix $A^T A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ (and equally of $A A^T \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times m}$).
2. **Right Singular Vectors from an Eigenbasis of $A^T A$**: Choose an orthonormal eigenbasis $\{\mathbf{v}_1, \dots, \mathbf{v}_r, \mathbf{v}_{r+1}, \dots, \mathbf{v}_n\}$ of $A^T A$ ordered by decreasing eigenvalues, satisfying:
   $$
   A^T A \mathbf{v}_i = \sigma_i^2 \mathbf{v}_i \;\; (1 \le i \le r), \qquad A^T A \mathbf{v}_i = \mathbf{0} \iff A\mathbf{v}_i = \mathbf{0} \;\; (r+1 \le i \le n)
   $$
3. **Left Singular Vectors Strictly Locked by $A\mathbf{v}_i$**: For $1 \le i \le r$, define:
   $$
   \mathbf{u}_i := \frac{1}{\sigma_i} A \mathbf{v}_i
   $$
   Then the set $\{\mathbf{u}_1, \dots, \mathbf{u}_r\}$ **automatically** forms an orthonormal basis for $\operatorname{Col}(A)$, and automatically satisfies $AA^T \mathbf{u}_i = \sigma_i^2 \mathbf{u}_i$ as well as $A\mathbf{v}_i = \sigma_i \mathbf{u}_i$.
4. **Completing $U$ with the Left Nullspace**: Choose any orthonormal basis $\{\mathbf{u}_{r+1}, \dots, \mathbf{u}_m\}$ for $\operatorname{Null}(A^T) = \operatorname{Col}(A)^\perp \subseteq \mathbb{R}^m$ (of dimension $m - r$) to complete $U$ to an $m \times m$ orthogonal matrix. Then $A = U\Sigma V^T$ holds exactly.

</TheoremCard>

<ProofCollapse title="Rigorous Proof: Existence of the SVD and Automatic Orthonormality">

Let us verify step by step why the construction above fits together algebraically with zero hidden gaps.

**Step 1: Symmetry, positive semidefiniteness, and spectral decomposition of $A^T A$**
Consider $A^T A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$:
- Symmetry: $(A^T A)^T = A^T (A^T)^T = A^T A$;
- Positive semidefiniteness: for any $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n$, we have $\mathbf{x}^T (A^T A) \mathbf{x} = (A\mathbf{x})^T (A\mathbf{x}) = \|A\mathbf{x}\|_2^2 \ge 0$.

By the Spectral Theorem for real symmetric matrices (Lecture 26), $A^T A$ possesses $n$ real eigenvalues $\lambda_1 \ge \lambda_2 \ge \dots \ge \lambda_n \ge 0$ and a corresponding orthonormal eigenbasis $\{\mathbf{v}_1, \dots, \mathbf{v}_n\} \subset \mathbb{R}^n$ (meaning $\mathbf{v}_i^T \mathbf{v}_j = \delta_{ij}$).

**Step 2: Pinning down the number of nonzero singular values as $r = \operatorname{rank}(A)$**
Recall the key lemma from Lecture 16: $\operatorname{Null}(A^T A) = \operatorname{Null}(A)$.
(Proof: $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0} \implies A^T A \mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$; conversely, $A^T A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0} \implies \mathbf{x}^T A^T A \mathbf{x} = \|A\mathbf{x}\|_2^2 = 0 \implies A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$.)

By the Rank-Nullity Theorem, $\operatorname{rank}(A^T A) = n - \dim\operatorname{Null}(A^T A) = n - \dim\operatorname{Null}(A) = \operatorname{rank}(A) = r$.
Since the rank of a real symmetric matrix equals the number of its nonzero eigenvalues (Lecture 26), $A^T A$ has **exactly $r$ strictly positive eigenvalues**:

$$
\lambda_1 \ge \lambda_2 \ge \dots \ge \lambda_r \gt 0, \qquad \lambda_{r+1} = \dots = \lambda_n = 0
$$

Set $\sigma_i = \sqrt{\lambda_i} \gt 0$ for $1 \le i \le r$. Meanwhile, for the last $n-r$ vectors, $A^T A \mathbf{v}_i = \mathbf{0} \implies \|A\mathbf{v}_i\|_2^2 = 0 \implies A\mathbf{v}_i = \mathbf{0}$, so $\{\mathbf{v}_{r+1}, \dots, \mathbf{v}_n\}$ is an orthonormal basis for $\operatorname{Null}(A)$, and consequently its orthogonal complement $\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf{v}_1, \dots, \mathbf{v}_r\} = \operatorname{Null}(A)^\perp = \operatorname{Row}(A)$.

**Step 3: Proving that $\mathbf{u}_i = \frac{1}{\sigma_i} A\mathbf{v}_i$ are automatically orthonormal**
For any $1 \le i, j \le r$, compute the inner product $\mathbf{u}_i^T \mathbf{u}_j$ directly:

$$
\mathbf{u}_i^T \mathbf{u}_j = \left(\frac{1}{\sigma_i} A\mathbf{v}_i\right)^T \left(\frac{1}{\sigma_j} A\mathbf{v}_j\right) = \frac{1}{\sigma_i \sigma_j} \mathbf{v}_i^T (A^T A \mathbf{v}_j) = \frac{1}{\sigma_i \sigma_j} \mathbf{v}_i^T (\sigma_j^2 \mathbf{v}_j) = \frac{\sigma_j}{\sigma_i} (\mathbf{v}_i^T \mathbf{v}_j) = \delta_{ij}
$$

How remarkable is that! Even though $A$ is neither orthogonal nor square, whenever $\mathbf{v}_i, \mathbf{v}_j$ are orthonormal eigenvectors of $A^T A$, their nonzero images under $A$ are orthogonal. Dividing them by $\sigma_i,\sigma_j$ gives the unit vectors $\mathbf{u}_i,\mathbf{u}_j$. Moreover, checking $AA^T \mathbf{u}_i$:

$$
AA^T \mathbf{u}_i = A A^T \left(\frac{1}{\sigma_i} A\mathbf{v}_i\right) = \frac{1}{\sigma_i} A (A^T A \mathbf{v}_i) = \frac{\sigma_i^2}{\sigma_i} A \mathbf{v}_i = \sigma_i^2 \mathbf{u}_i
$$

And because $\mathbf{u}_i = A(\frac{1}{\sigma_i}\mathbf{v}_i) \in \operatorname{Col}(A)$, these $r$ orthonormal vectors span the entire column space of dimension $\dim \operatorname{Col}(A) = r$!

**Step 4: Completing the basis of $\operatorname{Null}(A^T)$ and verifying $U^T A V = \Sigma$**
Since $\operatorname{Null}(A^T) = \operatorname{Col}(A)^\perp \subseteq \mathbb{R}^m$ has dimension $m - r$, use Gram-Schmidt (Lecture 17) to choose an orthonormal basis $\{\mathbf{u}_{r+1}, \dots, \mathbf{u}_m\}$ for it. Then $U = [\mathbf{u}_1 \;\; \cdots \;\; \mathbf{u}_m] \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times m}$ is orthogonal and $V = [\mathbf{v}_1 \;\; \cdots \;\; \mathbf{v}_n] \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ is orthogonal.

Finally, check the $(i, j)$ entry $\mathbf{u}_i^T A \mathbf{v}_j$ of the matrix product $U^T A V$:
- If $j \gt r$: since $\mathbf{v}_j \in \operatorname{Null}(A)$, we have $A\mathbf{v}_j = \mathbf{0}$, so $\mathbf{u}_i^T A \mathbf{v}_j = 0$;
- If $1 \le j \le r$: by definition $A\mathbf{v}_j = \sigma_j \mathbf{u}_j$, so $\mathbf{u}_i^T A \mathbf{v}_j = \sigma_j (\mathbf{u}_i^T \mathbf{u}_j) = \sigma_j \delta_{ij}$.

Thus $U^T A V$ has $\sigma_i$ in the top-left $(i, i)$ entries ($1 \le i \le r$) and $0$ everywhere else, i.e.,

$$
U^T A V = \Sigma
$$

Left-multiplying by $U$ and right-multiplying by $V^T$ (using $U U^T = I_m, V V^T = I_n$) gives $A = U \Sigma V^T$! As for the uniqueness of $\sigma_i$, the multiset of eigenvalues of $A^T A$ is uniquely determined by the characteristic polynomial $\det(\lambda I_n - A^T A)$, so their positive square roots in decreasing order are unique.

</ProofCollapse>

**Dual Shortcut (when $m \lt n$, fewer rows than columns)**: If $A$ is a "short, wide" matrix (say $2 \times 100$), computing the $100 \times 100$ matrix $A^T A$ is needlessly heavy when $A A^T$ is only $2 \times 2$! In that case, we symmetrically **diagonalize the smaller matrix $A A^T$ first** to find $\sigma_1,\dots,\sigma_r$ and $\{\mathbf{u}_1, \dots, \mathbf{u}_r\}$, and then **lock in the right singular vectors in reverse** via the transpose formula:

$$
A^T \mathbf{u}_i = A^T \left(\frac{1}{\sigma_i} A \mathbf{v}_i\right) = \frac{1}{\sigma_i} (A^T A \mathbf{v}_i) = \sigma_i \mathbf{v}_i \implies \mathbf{v}_i = \frac{1}{\sigma_i} A^T \mathbf{u}_i \quad (1 \le i \le r)
$$

Then simply complete $\mathbf{v}_{r+1}, \dots, \mathbf{v}_n$ in $\operatorname{Null}(A)$! Both paths are completely symmetric mathematically: always start by diagonalizing whichever of $A^T A$ and $A A^T$ is **smaller**, and never solve both independently!

## 3. Worked Examples by Hand: Two Classic Cases

### Example 1: Hand-Computing a $2 \times 2$ Invertible Matrix and Exposing the Sign Trap

Compute the SVD $A = U\Sigma V^T$ of the matrix:

$$
A = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 4 \\ -3 & 3 \end{bmatrix}
$$

*(Note: $A$ is not even symmetric.)*

**Step 1: Form the symmetric matrix $A^T A$ and find its eigenvalues and right singular vectors**

$$
A^T A = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & -3 \\ 4 & 3 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 4 \\ -3 & 3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 25 & 7 \\ 7 & 25 \end{bmatrix}
$$

Compute the characteristic polynomial:

$$
\det(A^T A - \lambda I_2) = (25 - \lambda)^2 - 7^2 = (\lambda - 32)(\lambda - 18) = 0
$$

Order the eigenvalues in decreasing order: $\lambda_1 = 32$, $\lambda_2 = 18$. Thus the singular values are:

$$
\sigma_1 = \sqrt{32} = 4\sqrt{2}, \qquad \sigma_2 = \sqrt{18} = 3\sqrt{2}
$$

*(Quick sanity check: $\sigma_1 \sigma_2 = 4\sqrt{2} \cdot 3\sqrt{2} = 24 = |\det(A)|$; and $\sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2 = 32 + 18 = 50 = 4^2 + 4^2 + (-3)^2 + 3^2 = \|A\|_F^2$!)*

Find the corresponding unit orthogonal eigenvectors $\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2$:
- For $\lambda_1 = 32$:

$$
(A^T A - 32 I_2)\mathbf{v}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} -7 & 7 \\ 7 & -7 \end{bmatrix}\mathbf{v}_1 = \mathbf{0} \implies \mathbf{v}_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}
$$

- For $\lambda_2 = 18$:

$$
(A^T A - 18 I_2)\mathbf{v}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 7 & 7 \\ 7 & 7 \end{bmatrix}\mathbf{v}_2 = \mathbf{0} \implies \mathbf{v}_2 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}
$$

**Step 2: Strictly lock the left singular vectors using $\mathbf{u}_i = \frac{1}{\sigma_i} A \mathbf{v}_i$**

$$
\mathbf{u}_1 = \frac{1}{\sigma_1} A \mathbf{v}_1 = \frac{1}{4\sqrt{2}} \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 4 \\ -3 & 3 \end{bmatrix} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{8} \begin{bmatrix} 8 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}
$$

$$
\mathbf{u}_2 = \frac{1}{\sigma_2} A \mathbf{v}_2 = \frac{1}{3\sqrt{2}} \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 4 \\ -3 & 3 \end{bmatrix} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{6} \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ -6 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}
$$

Notice the **minus sign** in $\mathbf{u}_2 = [0, -1]^T$! It is completely locked in by our sign choice for $\mathbf{v}_2$.

**Step 3: Assemble the factorization and verify by multiplying back**

$$
A = U \Sigma V^T = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 4\sqrt{2} & 0 \\ 0 & 3\sqrt{2} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1/\sqrt{2} & 1/\sqrt{2} \\ 1/\sqrt{2} & -1/\sqrt{2} \end{bmatrix}
$$

Let us check both the matrix product and the sum of rank-one outer products:

$$
U \Sigma V^T = \begin{bmatrix} 4\sqrt{2} & 0 \\ 0 & -3\sqrt{2} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1/\sqrt{2} & 1/\sqrt{2} \\ 1/\sqrt{2} & -1/\sqrt{2} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 4 \\ -3 & 3 \end{bmatrix} = A
$$

$$
\sigma_1 \mathbf{u}_1 \mathbf{v}_1^T + \sigma_2 \mathbf{u}_2 \mathbf{v}_2^T = 4\sqrt{2}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} + 3\sqrt{2}\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 4 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ -3 & 3 \end{bmatrix} = A
$$

Imagine what happens if someone gets lazy and computes $A A^T = \operatorname{diag}(32, 18)$ separately: seeing a diagonal matrix, they instinctively pick the standard basis $\mathbf{u}_1 = [1, 0]^T, \mathbf{u}_2 = [0, 1]^T$. Multiplying back gives rows $[4, 4]$ and $[3, -3]$, flipping the sign of the entire second row so the product is not $A$! That is why this course insists you must never solve $A^T A$ and $AA^T$ independently!

### Example 2: Thin SVD vs. Full SVD for a Rank-Deficient Rectangular Matrix

Consider a $3 \times 2$ matrix of rank $1$:

$$
A = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 4 \\ 6 & 8 \\ 6 & 8 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix}
$$

- Here the rank is $r = 1$. Without even forming $A^T A$, we can see by inspection that the row direction is $[3, 4]^T$ (length $\sqrt{3^2+4^2} = 5$) and the column direction is $[1, 2, 2]^T$ (length $\sqrt{1^2+2^2+2^2} = 3$)!
- Normalizing gives the first pair of singular vectors:
  $$
  \mathbf{v}_1 = \frac{1}{5} \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{u}_1 = \frac{1}{3} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}
  $$
  Checking $A \mathbf{v}_1 = [1, 2, 2]^T (5) = 15 \mathbf{u}_1$, the sole nonzero singular value is $\sigma_1 = 15$!
- **Thin SVD ($r = 1$)**: Keep only the active part to write a compact product immediately:
  $$
  A = U_1 \Sigma_1 V_1^T = \left(\frac{1}{3}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}\right) [15] \left(\frac{1}{5}\begin{bmatrix} 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix}\right)
  $$
- **Full SVD ($3 \times 3, 3 \times 2, 2 \times 2$)**: We must complete orthonormal bases for both nullspaces!
  - In $\operatorname{Null}(A) \subseteq \mathbb{R}^2$ ($\dim = 2 - 1 = 1$), find a unit vector orthogonal to $\mathbf{v}_1$: $\mathbf{v}_2 = \frac{1}{5}[-4, 3]^T$;
  - In $\operatorname{Null}(A^T) \subseteq \mathbb{R}^3$ ($\dim = 3 - 1 = 2$), find two mutually orthogonal unit vectors that are also orthogonal to $\mathbf{u}_1$:
    Notice that $\mathbf{u}_2 = \frac{1}{3}[2, 1, -2]^T$ and $\mathbf{u}_3 = \frac{1}{3}[2, -2, 1]^T$ satisfy $\mathbf{u}_1^T \mathbf{u}_2 = \mathbf{u}_1^T \mathbf{u}_3 = \mathbf{u}_2^T \mathbf{u}_3 = 0$, and both have unit length!

Thus we obtain a remarkably clean Full SVD with exact fractions:

$$
A = \underbrace{\frac{1}{3}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 & -2 \\ 2 & -2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{U \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 3}} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 15 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}}_{\Sigma \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 2}} \underbrace{\frac{1}{5}\begin{bmatrix} 3 & 4 \\ -4 & 3 \end{bmatrix}}_{V^T \in \mathbb{R}^{2 \times 2}}
$$

This factorization not only reconstructs $A$, it hands us **orthonormal bases** for all four fundamental subspaces in one single package!

## 4. The Ultimate Upgrade of the Four Fundamental Subspaces, Matrix Norms, and Polar Decomposition

### 1. The Peak Form of the Fundamental Theorem of Linear Algebra

In Module 1 (Lecture 10) and Module 2 (Lecture 14), we worked hard with elimination $R = \operatorname{rref}(A)$ to find bases for the four fundamental subspaces, and proved that the row space is orthogonal to the nullspace while the column space is orthogonal to the left nullspace. Yet the basis vectors produced by RREF are **not orthogonal**, and their lengths vary wildly.

The SVD equips all four fundamental subspaces with their **ultimate orthonormal bases** simultaneously:

| Fundamental Subspace | Ambient Space | Dimension | SVD Orthonormal Basis | Block Matrix Notation |
| :--- | :---: | :---: | :--- | :---: |
| **Row Space $\operatorname{Row}(A)$** | $\mathbb{R}^n$ | $r$ | $\{\mathbf{v}_1, \dots, \mathbf{v}_r\}$ | $V_r$ |
| **Nullspace $\operatorname{Null}(A)$** | $\mathbb{R}^n$ | $n - r$ | $\{\mathbf{v}_{r+1}, \dots, \mathbf{v}_n\}$ | $V_{n-r}$ |
| **Column Space $\operatorname{Col}(A)$** | $\mathbb{R}^m$ | $r$ | $\{\mathbf{u}_1, \dots, \mathbf{u}_r\}$ | $U_r$ |
| **Left Nullspace $\operatorname{Null}(A^T)$** | $\mathbb{R}^m$ | $m - r$ | $\{\mathbf{u}_{r+1}, \dots, \mathbf{u}_m\}$ | $U_{m-r}$ |

In the "diagonal coordinate system" of the SVD, the action of $A$ on the entire universe becomes completely transparent:
- Any vector in the input space $\mathbb{R}^n = \operatorname{Row}(A) \oplus \operatorname{Null}(A)$ decomposes as $\mathbf{x} = \sum_{i=1}^n (\mathbf{v}_i^T \mathbf{x}) \mathbf{v}_i$;
- After $A$ acts, every nullspace component ($i \gt r$) vanishes completely, while each row-space component ($i \le r$) is mapped bijectively into $\operatorname{Col}(A)$ and scaled by $\sigma_i$: $A\mathbf{x} = \sum_{i=1}^r \sigma_i (\mathbf{v}_i^T \mathbf{x}) \mathbf{u}_i$!

### 2. Spectral Norm, Frobenius Norm, and Condition Number

Because orthogonal matrices $U, V$ preserve the Euclidean $\ell_2$-norm of every vector, the singular values directly govern all the core metric invariants of a matrix:
- **Spectral Norm (Induced $\ell_2$-Norm)**: The maximum stretching factor of $A$ over all unit vectors equals the largest singular value:
  $$
  \|A\|_2 := \max_{\|\mathbf{x}\|_2 = 1} \|A\mathbf{x}\|_2 = \sigma_1
  $$
  The maximizers are the unit vectors in the top eigenspace of $A^T A$, including $\pm\mathbf{v}_1$. When $r = n \le m$, the minimum stretching factor is $\min_{\|\mathbf{x}\|_2=1}\|A\mathbf{x}\|_2 = \sigma_n$ (achieved at $\mathbf{v}_n$).
- **Frobenius Norm**: The square root of the sum of squares of all matrix entries equals the Euclidean length of the vector of singular values:
  $$
  \|A\|_F := \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^m \sum_{j=1}^n a_{ij}^2} = \sqrt{\operatorname{tr}(A^T A)} = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^r \sigma_i^2}
  $$
- **$\ell_2$ condition number**: For a full-rank matrix, $\kappa_2(A)=\sigma_1/\sigma_{\min(m,n)}$. For a rank-deficient matrix the usual condition number is infinite. If $r>0$, the ratio $\sigma_1/\sigma_r$ instead measures conditioning of the bijection $\operatorname{Row}(A)\to\operatorname{Col}(A)$. For square invertible $A$, it equals $\|A\|_2\|A^{-1}\|_2$.

### 3. One-Line Derivation of the Polar Decomposition

As a natural corollary of the SVD, for any square matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$, insert $I_n = V^T V$ cleverly into the middle of $A = U\Sigma V^T$:

$$
A = U \Sigma V^T = \underbrace{(U V^T)}_{Q} \underbrace{(V \Sigma V^T)}_{S} = Q S
$$

Look at the properties of these two factors:
- $Q = U V^T$ is a product of two orthogonal matrices, so $Q$ is itself an **orthogonal matrix** (a pure rigid rotation/reflection);
- $S = V \Sigma V^T$ is a real symmetric positive semidefinite matrix (in fact, $S = (A^T A)^{1/2}$, a pure stretch by $\sigma_i$ along the orthogonal principal axes $\mathbf{v}_i$); when $A$ is invertible, $S$ is strictly positive definite and both $Q$ and $S$ are unique.

This is the exact higher-dimensional matrix generalization of the polar form of a complex number, $z = e^{i\theta} \cdot r$! And in Lecture 34, we will see how the SVD immediately gives the ultimate generalized inverse for any rectangular or singular matrix—the **Moore-Penrose Pseudoinverse** $A^+ = V \Sigma^+ U^T$.

<PitfallAlert title="Common Pitfalls & Transcript Errata">

### 1. Fatal Error #1: Computing Eigenvectors of $A^T A$ and $AA^T$ Independently to Assemble $V$ and $U$!
Many informal online notes claim: "To find the SVD, compute the eigenvectors of $A^T A$ for $V$, and compute the eigenvectors of $AA^T$ for $U$." **This is the single most dangerous mistake in the entire course!**
- **Sign-flip mismatch**: Multiplying an eigenvector by $-1$ still gives an eigenvector. As we demonstrated in Example 1 on $A$ with rows $[4, 4]$ and $[-3, 3]$, solving $AA^T = \operatorname{diag}(32, 18)$ independently naturally picks $\mathbf{u}_2 = [0, 1]^T$, producing the wrong matrix with rows $[4, 4]$ and $[3, -3]$ instead of $A$!
- **Repeated-singular-value catastrophe**: When a singular value is repeated (e.g., $\sigma_1 = \sigma_2 = 5$), the corresponding eigenspace has dimension $\ge 2$, so **any** orthonormal basis in that subspace is a valid eigenbasis! Solving $U$ and $V$ independently can leave them differing by an arbitrary rotation angle $\theta$ inside that 2D eigenspace, so $U\Sigma V^T$ will not equal $A$ at all!
- **The Iron Rule**: Once you choose an orthonormal eigenbasis $\mathbf{v}_1,\dots,\mathbf{v}_r$ of $A^T A$, you **must lock in** the first $r$ left singular vectors via $\mathbf{u}_i = \frac{1}{\sigma_i} A \mathbf{v}_i$ (or, when $m \lt n$, find $\mathbf{u}_1,\dots,\mathbf{u}_r$ from $AA^T$ and lock in the first $r$ right singular vectors via $\mathbf{v}_i = \frac{1}{\sigma_i} A^T \mathbf{u}_i$). Only the zero-eigenvalue nullspaces $\operatorname{Null}(A)$ and $\operatorname{Null}(A^T)$ may be completed independently!

### 2. Confusing Singular Values $\sigma_i$ with Absolute Values of Eigenvalues $|\lambda_i|$
Many beginners mistakenly assume that the singular values of a square matrix are just the absolute values of its eigenvalues. **False!**
Consider the simplest nilpotent shear matrix:

$$
A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}
$$

Both of its eigenvalues are zero ($\lambda_1 = \lambda_2 = 0$), yet computing $A^T A = \operatorname{diag}(0, 4)$ immediately gives singular values $\sigma_1 = 2, \sigma_2 = 0$!
- **Root cause**: Eigenvalues measure stretching along special invariant directions, whereas singular values measure the maximum geometric stretching over the entire unit sphere. Non-normal matrices ($A^T A \ne A A^T$) have non-orthogonal eigenvectors (here, only one eigenvector exists), allowing shearing to produce geometric amplification far exceeding the spectral radius.
- **When are they equal?** Only when a square matrix is a **normal matrix** (meaning $A^T A = A A^T$, or $A^H A = A A^H$ in the complex case—which includes symmetric, skew-symmetric, and orthogonal matrices) does $\sigma_i = |\lambda_i|$ hold; and for a general symmetric matrix with negative eigenvalues, the singular values are the **absolute values** $|\lambda_i|$, while the left singular vectors absorb the sign via $\mathbf{u}_i = \operatorname{sgn}(\lambda_i)\mathbf{q}_i$ ($\lambda_i\ne 0$) so that $\sigma_i \ge 0$.

### 3. Singular Values Are Unique, but the Matrices $U$ and $V$ Are Not
For any given matrix $A$:
- The sequence of singular values $\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \dots \ge \sigma_r \gt 0$ is **absolutely unique**;
- When the singular values are distinct ($\sigma_1 \gt \sigma_2 \gt \dots \gt \sigma_r \gt 0$), the singular directions are still free up to **paired sign flips** (replacing $\mathbf{u}_i, \mathbf{v}_i$ simultaneously by $-\mathbf{u}_i, -\mathbf{v}_i$ leaves the outer product $(-\mathbf{u}_i)(-\mathbf{v}_i)^T = \mathbf{u}_i \mathbf{v}_i^T$ unchanged);
- For a repeated positive singular value, apply the same orthogonal basis change to the paired left and right bases. The bases of the two nullspaces may be chosen independently.

</PitfallAlert>

<SolutionIntuition>

For a $2 \times 3$ matrix, $A^T A$ is $3 \times 3$ while $A A^T$ is only $2 \times 2$, so starting from the dual side $AA^T$ cuts the workload by more than half. Because the symmetric matrix $AA^T$ has equal diagonal entries $17$ and off-diagonal entries $8$, its eigenvectors naturally point along $45^\circ$ and $-45^\circ$, and its eigenvalues turn out to be perfect squares $25$ and $9$ (giving integer singular values $\sigma_1 = 5, \sigma_2 = 3$). Once $\mathbf{v}_i = \frac{1}{\sigma_i} A^T \mathbf{u}_i$ locks in $\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2$, the nullspace has dimension $3 - 2 = 1$, so we only need one unit vector perpendicular to both rows (or to $\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2$) to complete the $3 \times 3$ orthogonal matrix $V$!

</SolutionIntuition>
<SolutionRigorous>

**Step 1: Eigendecomposition of $A A^T$**

$$
A A^T = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 2 & 2 \\ 2 & 3 & -2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 2 \\ 2 & 3 \\ 2 & -2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 17 & 8 \\ 8 & 17 \end{bmatrix}
$$

The characteristic equation is $(17 - \lambda)^2 - 64 = 0$, giving decreasing eigenvalues $\lambda_1 = 25, \lambda_2 = 9$. Thus the nonzero singular values are:

$$
\sigma_1 = \sqrt{25} = 5, \qquad \sigma_2 = \sqrt{9} = 3
$$

Find the corresponding orthonormal left singular vectors:
- $\lambda_1 = 25$:

$$
(A A^T - 25 I_2)\mathbf{u}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} -8 & 8 \\ 8 & -8 \end{bmatrix}\mathbf{u}_1 = \mathbf{0} \implies \mathbf{u}_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}
$$

- $\lambda_2 = 9$:

$$
(A A^T - 9 I_2)\mathbf{u}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 8 & 8 \\ 8 & 8 \end{bmatrix}\mathbf{u}_2 = \mathbf{0} \implies \mathbf{u}_2 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}
$$

**Step 2: Lock in the right singular vectors and complete the nullspace basis**
Using $\mathbf{v}_i = \frac{1}{\sigma_i} A^T \mathbf{u}_i$:

$$
\mathbf{v}_1 = \frac{1}{5} \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 2 \\ 2 & 3 \\ 2 & -2 \end{bmatrix} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{5\sqrt{2}} \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 5 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}
$$

$$
\mathbf{v}_2 = \frac{1}{3} \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 2 \\ 2 & 3 \\ 2 & -2 \end{bmatrix} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{3\sqrt{2}} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}
$$

*(Check: $\|\mathbf{v}_2\|_2^2 = \frac{1+1+16}{18} = 1$, and $\mathbf{v}_1^T \mathbf{v}_2 = \frac{1-1+0}{6} = 0$ — automatically orthonormal!)*

For $\mathbf{v}_3 \in \operatorname{Null}(A)$: $\mathbf{v}_3 \perp \mathbf{v}_1$ implies it has the form $[a, -a, b]^T$. Substituting into the first row gives $3a - 2a + 2b = 0 \implies a = -2b$. Choosing $b = -1$ gives $[2, -2, -1]^T$ of length $\sqrt{4+4+1} = 3$, so normalizing yields:

$$
\mathbf{v}_3 = \frac{1}{3} \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ -2 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}
$$

*(Check: $A\mathbf{v}_3 = \frac{1}{3}[6-4-2, \; 4-6+2]^T = \mathbf{0}$, and $\mathbf{v}_2^T \mathbf{v}_3 = \frac{2+2-4}{9\sqrt{2}} = 0$.)*

**Step 3: Write Thin SVD, Full SVD, and Rank-1 Expansion**
- **Thin SVD**:
  $$
  A = \underbrace{\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}}_{U_2 \in \mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 5 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix}}_{\Sigma_2 \in \mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1/\sqrt{2} & 1/\sqrt{2} & 0 \\ 1/(3\sqrt{2}) & -1/(3\sqrt{2}) & 4/(3\sqrt{2}) \end{bmatrix}}_{V_2^T \in \mathbb{R}^{2\times 3}}
  $$
- **Full SVD**:
  $$
  A = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1/\sqrt{2} & 1/\sqrt{2} & 0 \\ 1/(3\sqrt{2}) & -1/(3\sqrt{2}) & 4/(3\sqrt{2}) \\ 2/3 & -2/3 & -1/3 \end{bmatrix}
  $$
- **Rank-1 Expansion & Verification**:
  $$
  5 \mathbf{u}_1 \mathbf{v}_1^T + 3 \mathbf{u}_2 \mathbf{v}_2^T = \frac{5}{2}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} + \frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 & 4 \\ -1 & 1 & -4 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 2 & 2 \\ 2 & 3 & -2 \end{bmatrix} = A
  $$

<SolutionRigorous>

The statement is **False**.

**Counterexample**: Take the strictly upper triangular nonzero (nilpotent) matrix:

$$
A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}
$$

2. Singular values:
   $$
   A^T A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 2 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 4 \end{bmatrix}
   $$
   Its eigenvalues are $4$ and $0$, so $\sigma_1 = 2 \ne 0 = |\lambda_1|$ and $\sigma_2 = 0$.

**Deeper Algebraic Connection (Schur's Inequality & Normal Matrices)**:
In fact, for an arbitrary square matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$, only two global relations always hold:
1. Determinant product conservation: $\prod_{i=1}^n \sigma_i = \sqrt{\det(A^T A)} = |\det(A)| = \prod_{i=1}^n |\lambda_i|$;
2. Energy bound (Schur's inequality): $\sum_{i=1}^n \sigma_i^2 = \|A\|_F^2 \ge \sum_{i=1}^n |\lambda_i|^2$.

By Schur's triangularization theorem, equality holds above (and $\{\sigma_i\} = \{|\lambda_i|\}$) **if and only if** $A$ is a **normal matrix** ($A^T A = A A^T$, which includes real symmetric, skew-symmetric, and orthogonal matrices). For non-normal matrices, coupling between non-orthogonal eigenvectors inflates the singular values so that $\sigma_1 \ge \max_i |\lambda_i|$!

**Part 1 Proof (Spectral norm and minimum singular value):**
For any unit vector $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ ($\|\mathbf{x}\|_2 = 1$), let $\mathbf{y} = V^T \mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n$.
Since $V$ is orthogonal, length preservation gives $\|\mathbf{y}\|_2 = \|V^T \mathbf{x}\|_2 = \|\mathbf{x}\|_2 = 1$ (so $\sum_{i=1}^n y_i^2 = 1$).
Because $U$ is also orthogonal, it preserves the $\ell_2$-norm of every vector:

$$
\|A\mathbf{x}\|_2^2 = \|U (\Sigma V^T \mathbf{x})\|_2^2 = \|\Sigma \mathbf{y}\|_2^2 = \sum_{i=1}^r \sigma_i^2 y_i^2

$$

Equality holds at $\mathbf{x} = \pm \mathbf{v}_n$, so $\min_{\|\mathbf{x}\|_2 = 1} \|A\mathbf{x}\|_2 = \sigma_n$.

Taking square roots gives $\|A\|_F = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^r \sigma_i^2}$.

$$
\tilde{U}^T \tilde{U} = U^T Q_1^T Q_1 U = U^T I_m U = I_m
$$

$$
\tilde{V}^T \tilde{V} = V^T Q_2 Q_2^T V = V^T I_n V = I_n
$$

Thus $B = \tilde{U} \Sigma \tilde{V}^T$ is a valid SVD of $B$! By uniqueness of singular values, $B$ and $A$ share the exact same singular value matrix $\Sigma$. Combining with Parts 1 and 2 immediately yields $\|Q_1 A Q_2\|_2 = \|A\|_2$ and $\|Q_1 A Q_2\|_F = \|A\|_F$.

$$
\operatorname{tr}(A^T W) = \operatorname{tr}(V \Sigma U^T W) = \operatorname{tr}\!\left(\Sigma (U^T W V)\right)

$$
\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma Z) = \sum_{i=1}^n \sigma_i z_{ii} \le \sum_{i=1}^n \sigma_i \cdot 1 = \sum_{i=1}^n \sigma_i

At equality, $z_{ii}=1$ for each $\sigma_i\gt 0$. ### 1. The Eckart-Young-Mirsky Theorem: Why SVD Truncation Is an Unbeatable Compression Limit

In Sections 1 and 2, we saw that the SVD writes any weight matrix $W \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ as a sum of rank-1 building blocks ordered from highest energy to lowest:

$$
W = \sum_{i=1}^r \sigma_i \mathbf{u}_i \mathbf{v}_i^T, \qquad \sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \dots \ge \sigma_r \gt 0
$$

If our budget allows only a matrix $B$ of rank at most $k$ ($k \lt r$) to approximate $W$ (for example, to factor a high-dimensional weight matrix into two thin factors $B = L R$ with $L \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times k}, R \in \mathbb{R}^{k\times n}$), we simply retain the top $k$ singular components:

$$
W_k := \sum_{i=1}^k \sigma_i \mathbf{u}_i \mathbf{v}_i^T = U_k \Sigma_k V_k^T
$$

The **Eckart-Young-Mirsky Theorem (1936 / 1960)** provides a remarkable optimality guarantee: in **both the spectral norm and the Frobenius norm**, no matrix of rank $\le k$ can get closer to $W$ than $W_k$!

$$
\min_{\operatorname{rank}(B) \le k} \|W - B\|_2 = \|W - W_k\|_2 = \sigma_{k+1}
$$

$$
\min_{\operatorname{rank}(B) \le k} \|W - B\|_F = \|W - W_k\|_F = \sqrt{\sum_{i=k+1}^r \sigma_i^2}
$$

**Five-Step Dimension-Intersection Proof of Spectral Norm Optimality (using only Lecture 10 nullspace dimensions!):**
Let $B \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ be **any** matrix with $\operatorname{rank}(B) \le k$.
1. By the Rank-Nullity Theorem, $\dim \operatorname{Null}(B) = n - \operatorname{rank}(B) \ge n - k$;
2. Consider the $(k+1)$-dimensional subspace spanned by the first $k+1$ right singular vectors, $\mathcal{V}_{k+1} := \operatorname{span}\{\mathbf{v}_1, \dots, \mathbf{v}_{k+1}\} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$;
3. Since the sum of dimensions $(n - k) + (k + 1) = n + 1 \gt n$ exceeds the ambient dimension $n$, these two subspaces **must intersect in at least a nonzero line**! Thus there exists a unit vector $\mathbf{z} \in \operatorname{Null}(B) \cap \mathcal{V}_{k+1}$ ($\|\mathbf{z}\|_2 = 1$);
4. Expand in orthonormal coordinates $\mathbf{z} = \sum_{i=1}^{k+1} c_i \mathbf{v}_i$ ($\sum_{i=1}^{k+1} c_i^2 = 1$), with $B\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{0}$;
5. By the definition of the spectral norm and the Pythagorean theorem:
   $$
   \|W - B\|_2^2 \ge \|(W - B)\mathbf{z}\|_2^2 = \|W\mathbf{z}\|_2^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{k+1} \sigma_i^2 c_i^2 \ge \sigma_{k+1}^2 \sum_{i=1}^{k+1} c_i^2 = \sigma_{k+1}^2
   $$
   Taking square roots gives $\|W - B\|_2 \ge \sigma_{k+1} = \|W - W_k\|_2$! Q.E.D.

### 2. The Bilinear Low-Rank Bottleneck of Transformer Multi-Head Attention $W_{QK}^h$ (Elhage et al., 2021)

In Anthropic's landmark paper *A Mathematical Framework for Transformer Circuits* (Elhage et al., 2021), the authors dissected the structural bottleneck of Transformer self-attention directly through matrix rank and singular value spectra.

Let the model hidden dimension be $d_{\text{model}}$ (e.g., $d_{\text{model}} = 4096$ in Llama-3 8B), with $H$ attention heads and per-head dimension $d_{\text{head}} = d_{\text{model}} / H$ (e.g., $H = 32, d_{\text{head}} = 128$).
For head $h$, let $\mathbf{x}_t,\mathbf{x}_s\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{model}}}$ be the token hidden states. For a bias-free head with no position-dependent transformation, take $W_Q^h,W_K^h\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{model}}\times d_{\mathrm{head}}}$. Use the column vectors $\mathbf{q}_t^h=(W_Q^h)^T\mathbf{x}_t$ and $\mathbf{k}_s^h=(W_K^h)^T\mathbf{x}_s$. Then the unmasked score is

$$
\text{score}_h(t, s) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{d_{\text{head}}}} \mathbf{x}_t^T \underbrace{\left( W_Q^h (W_K^h)^T \right)}_{W_{QK}^h \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}} \times d_{\text{model}}}} \mathbf{x}_s
$$

*(Note: Under column-vector projection conventions $\tilde{W}_Q^h, \tilde{W}_K^h \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{head}} \times d_{\text{model}}}$, i.e., $\mathbf{q}_t^h = \tilde{W}_Q^h \mathbf{x}_t, \mathbf{k}_s^h = \tilde{W}_K^h \mathbf{x}_s$, the $d_{\text{model}} \times d_{\text{model}}$ bilinear scoring matrix is written $W_{QK}^h = (\tilde{W}_Q^h)^T \tilde{W}_K^h$.)*

Regardless of transpose convention, examine the **rank** of this massive $d_{\text{model}} \times d_{\text{model}}$ matrix:

$$
\operatorname{rank}(W_{QK}^h) \le \min\!\left(\operatorname{rank}(W_Q^h), \, \operatorname{rank}(W_K^h)\right) \le d_{\text{head}} \ll d_{\text{model}}
$$

From the SVD viewpoint, $W_{QK}^h$ can have at most $d_{\text{head}}$ (say $128$) nonzero singular values, while at least $d_{\text{model}} - d_{\text{head}} = 3968$ singular values are strictly zero!
Writing $r_h=\operatorname{rank}(W_{QK}^h)$, the first $r_h$ left and right singular vectors span the query-side and key-side directions that affect the score. For a token matrix $X\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times d_{\mathrm{model}}}$ with one hidden state per row, the raw logit matrix $XW_{QK}^hX^T/\sqrt{d_{\mathrm{head}}}$ also has rank at most $d_{\mathrm{head}}$. This bound applies before masking and row-wise softmax. Each head applies its own softmax and aggregates its own values. The head outputs are then concatenated and projected.

A related use of low-rank factorization appears in Multi-Head Latent Attention (MLA). If the content keys and values are reconstructed from a shared latent vector, their stacked linear projection factors through the latent dimension. That factorization explains why the shared latent can replace the separate content-key and value caches.

### 3. From Activation-Aware Compression (SVD-LLM) to Orthogonal Gradient Optimization (Muon)

Finally, the SVD sits at the forefront of modern LLM compression and efficient training:
- **Why does naive weight SVD fail while SVD-LLM (Wang et al., 2024) succeeds?**
  Weight truncation minimizes $\|W-B\|_F$ among matrices of rank at most $k$. For calibration activations $X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times N}$ stored as columns, the output error is $\|(W-B)X\|_F$. When $X$ has full row rank, factor its uncentered Gram matrix as $XX^T=SS^T$ with $S$ an invertible Cholesky factor. Then $\|(W-B)X\|_F=\|(W-B)S\|_F$ by the trace identity. Since right multiplication by $S$ preserves rank, a minimizer is $B=(WS)_k S^{-1}$.
- **The Muon Optimizer (Jordan et al., 2024) and Polar Orthogonalization**:
  When training 2D linear-layer weights $W \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$, the gradient/momentum matrix $G = U\Sigma V^T$ is often severely ill-conditioned ($\sigma_1 \gg \sigma_r$), causing optimization steps to be dominated by a handful of top singular directions. The Muon optimizer uses fast Newton-Schulz matrix iterations to approximate the **polar partial isometry**:
  $$
  G = U\Sigma V^T \;\longmapsto\; \operatorname{Polar}(G) = U_r V_r^T
  $$
  The ideal partial isometry has singular value $1$ on each nonzero singular direction and vanishes on the right nullspace. The iteration approximates this transformation of the update matrix.
