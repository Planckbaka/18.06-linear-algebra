In Lecture 30, we reached the algebraic summit of linear algebra—the Singular Value Decomposition (SVD), $A = U\Sigma V^T$. It revealed that even a completely non-symmetric, rectangular, or rank-deficient matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ is fundamentally nothing more than a pure axial stretching $\Sigma$ between an orthonormal basis $V$ of the input space $\mathbb{R}^n$ and an orthonormal basis $U$ of the output space $\mathbb{R}^m$.

Yet in engineering and mathematics, the central task is rarely "given input $\mathbf{x}$, compute output $\mathbf{b} = A\mathbf{x}$." Far more often, we work **in reverse**: given an observed output $\mathbf{b}$ and a linear system $A$, we want to invert the system to recover the best input $\mathbf{x}$.
- When $A$ is an invertible square matrix, the answer is crisp and clean: $\mathbf{x} = A^{-1}\mathbf{b}$.
- In the real world, however, most matrices $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ are **not square** ($m \neq n$) and frequently **not full rank** ($r \lt \min(m, n)$). In that setting, the two-sided inverse $A^{-1}$ simply does not exist!

In this lecture (Lecture 34 / Textbook §7.3–7.4), Prof. Gilbert Strang poses an overarching question: **When the standard inverse $A^{-1}$ no longer exists, what is the "most perfect inverse operation" we can assign to a matrix?** Following the complete classification of matrices by rank $r$ and dimensions $(m, n)$ into four cases, we will start from one-sided **Left Inverses** and **Right Inverses**, strip away the obstruction of the nullspaces, uncover the hidden strict **bijection** between the row space and the column space, and finally forge the universal **Moore-Penrose Pseudoinverse ($A^+$)** that works for every matrix.

---

## 1. The Four Rank Cases and the Family of One-Sided Inverses

Let $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ be any real matrix of rank $r = \operatorname{rank}(A)$. Recall the dimension-counting laws for the Four Fundamental Subspaces (Lecture 10):
- Input space $\mathbb{R}^n = \operatorname{Row}(A) \oplus \operatorname{Null}(A)$, where $\dim \operatorname{Row}(A) = r$ and $\dim \operatorname{Null}(A) = n - r$;
- Output space $\mathbb{R}^m = \operatorname{Col}(A) \oplus \operatorname{Null}(A^T)$, where $\dim \operatorname{Col}(A) = r$ and $\dim \operatorname{Null}(A^T) = m - r$.

Depending on whether $r$ reaches the number of rows $m$ and/or the number of columns $n$, every matrix in linear algebra falls cleanly into one of **Four Rank Cases**:

| Matrix Case | Rank Condition | Nullspace Dimensions | Number of Solutions to $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ | What "Inverse" Exists? | Canonical Formula |
| :--- | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: |
| **1. Square Full Rank** | $r = m = n$ | $\dim \operatorname{Null}(A) = 0$<br/>$\dim \operatorname{Null}(A^T) = 0$ | Always a **unique solution** | Unique **two-sided inverse** | $A^{-1}$ |
| **2. Full Column Rank (Tall & Thin)** | $r = n \lt m$ | $\dim \operatorname{Null}(A) = 0$<br/>$\dim \operatorname{Null}(A^T) = m - n \ge 1$ | $0$ or $1$ solution<br/>(overdetermined least squares) | **Infinitely many left inverses**<br/>(no right inverse) | $(A^T A)^{-1} A^T$ |
| **3. Full Row Rank (Short & Wide)** | $r = m \lt n$ | $\dim \operatorname{Null}(A) = n - m \ge 1$<br/>$\dim \operatorname{Null}(A^T) = 0$ | Always **infinitely many solutions**<br/>(underdetermined free variables) | **Infinitely many right inverses**<br/>(no left inverse) | $A^T (A A^T)^{-1}$ |
| **4. General Rank-Deficient** | $r \lt m$ and $r \lt n$ | $\dim \operatorname{Null}(A) \ge 1$<br/>$\dim \operatorname{Null}(A^T) \ge 1$ | $0$ or infinitely many solutions | **Neither left nor right inverse**<br/>(only the **pseudoinverse**) | $A^+ = V \Sigma^+ U^T$ |

Let us dissect the algebraic mechanism and geometric intuition of each case—especially why left and right inverses exist, why they are not unique, and what makes the "canonical" left and right inverses stand above the rest.

### 1.1 Case 2: Full Column Rank ($r = n \lt m$) and Left Inverses

When $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ has **full column rank**, all its columns are linearly independent, so its nullspace shrinks to the origin alone: $\operatorname{Null}(A) = \{\mathbf{0}\}$. Recall the key lemma from Lecture 15 (Least Squares): the Gram matrix $A^T A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ is **symmetric positive definite (SPD)**, and therefore strictly invertible!

<DefinitionCard label="Definition" title="Left Inverse and Canonical Left Inverse">

Let $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ have **full column rank** ($r = \operatorname{rank}(A) = n \le m$).

1. **Left Inverse**: A matrix $B \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times m}$ is called a **left inverse** of $A$ if multiplying $A$ on the left by $B$ yields the $n \times n$ identity matrix:
   $$
   B A = I_n
   $$
2. **Canonical Left Inverse**: The particular left inverse constructed from the normal-equations matrix $A^T A$:
   $$
   A_{\text{left}}^{-1} := (A^T A)^{-1} A^T \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times m}
   $$
   Multiplying on the left immediately gives the identity:
   $$
   A_{\text{left}}^{-1} A = (A^T A)^{-1}(A^T A) = I_n
   $$

</DefinitionCard>

#### What Happens If We Multiply in the Reverse Order?
Be careful: **putting $A_{\text{left}}^{-1}$ on the right of $A$ definitely does NOT produce $I_m$!** In fact:

$$
A A_{\text{left}}^{-1} = A (A^T A)^{-1} A^T = P_{\operatorname{Col}(A)} \neq I_m
$$

Its rank is only $n \lt m$, so it can never equal the $m \times m$ identity matrix $I_m$. Instead, it is precisely the orthogonal **projection matrix** $P_{\operatorname{Col}(A)}$ onto the column space $\operatorname{Col}(A)$ from Lecture 15!

#### Why Is the Left Inverse Not Unique? A Minimal $3 \times 2$ Numerical Example

When $n \lt m$, the left nullspace $\operatorname{Null}(A^T)$ has dimension $m - n \ge 1$—it is nontrivial! If we add any matrix $D \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times m}$ to the canonical left inverse such that **the transpose of every row of $D$ lies in $\operatorname{Null}(A^T)$** (equivalently, $D A = O_{n \times n}$), then:

$$
(A_{\text{left}}^{-1} + D) A = A_{\text{left}}^{-1} A + D A = I_n + O_{n \times n} = I_n
$$

Every left inverse has this form, since subtracting $A_{\text{left}}^{-1}$ from any left inverse gives a matrix $D$ with $DA=O$. A nonzero such $D$ gives infinitely many choices by scaling.

**Concrete Numerical Check**: Take the $3 \times 2$ full-column-rank matrix:

$$
A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 2} \quad (r = 2 = n \lt m = 3)
$$

First compute the canonical left inverse $A_{\text{left}}^{-1} = (A^T A)^{-1} A^T$:

$$
A^T A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad (A^T A)^{-1} = \frac{1}{3} \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}
$$

$$
A_{\text{left}}^{-1} = \frac{1}{3} \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{3} \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 & 1 \\ -1 & 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}
$$

Verification:
- Left product:

$$
A_{\text{left}}^{-1} A = \frac{1}{3} \begin{bmatrix} 2+0+1 & 0-1+1 \\ -1+0+1 & 0+2+1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = I_2
$$

- Reverse-order right product:

$$
A A_{\text{left}}^{-1} = \frac{1}{3} \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 & 1 \\ -1 & 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix} = P_{\operatorname{Col}(A)}
$$

which is symmetric, idempotent, and has trace $2$.

Now construct **another valid left inverse**—just notice that the first two rows of $A$ already form $I_2$, so we can simply throw away the third row:

$$
L_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \implies L_1 A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = I_2
$$

Taking the difference gives:

$$
L_1 - A_{\text{left}}^{-1} = \frac{1}{3} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & -1 \\ 1 & 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}
$$

whose rows are both multiples of the left-nullspace basis vector $[1, 1, -1]$ (since $A^T [1, 1, -1]^T = \mathbf{0}$)!

So why is $(A^T A)^{-1} A^T$ the **preeminent canonical left inverse**?
1. For any error/noise vector $\mathbf{y} \in \operatorname{Null}(A^T)$ in the left nullspace, $A_{\text{left}}^{-1} \mathbf{y} = (A^T A)^{-1}(A^T \mathbf{y}) = \mathbf{0}$—it **cleanly annihilates the left nullspace**. By contrast, $L_1 [1, 1, -1]^T = [1, 1]^T \neq \mathbf{0}$, so $L_1$ contaminates the solution with orthogonal error.
2. The reverse product

$$
A L_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}
$$

is an **oblique projection** (not symmetric), whereas only $A A_{\text{left}}^{-1}$ is the symmetric **orthogonal projection** $P_{\operatorname{Col}(A)}$, ensuring that $\hat{\mathbf{x}} = A_{\text{left}}^{-1} \mathbf{b}$ is the unique least-squares solution!

### 1.2 Case 3: Full Row Rank ($r = m \lt n$) and Right Inverses

Transpose our perspective on $A$, and we step immediately into a mirror-image world: when $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ has **full row rank** ($r = m \lt n$), the column space fills the entire output space ($\operatorname{Col}(A) = \mathbb{R}^m$), and the left nullspace shrinks to the origin $\operatorname{Null}(A^T) = \{\mathbf{0}\}$. Therefore, $A A^T \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times m}$ is symmetric positive definite and invertible!

<DefinitionCard label="Definition" title="Right Inverse and Canonical Right Inverse">

Let $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ have **full row rank** ($r = \operatorname{rank}(A) = m \le n$).

1. **Right Inverse**: A matrix $C \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times m}$ is called a **right inverse** of $A$ if multiplying $A$ on the right by $C$ yields the $m \times m$ identity matrix:
   $$
   A C = I_m
   $$
2. **Canonical Right Inverse**: The particular right inverse constructed from $A A^T$:
   $$
   A_{\text{right}}^{-1} := A^T (A A^T)^{-1} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times m}
   $$
   Multiplying on the right immediately gives the identity:
   $$
   A A_{\text{right}}^{-1} = (A A^T)(A A^T)^{-1} = I_m
   $$

</DefinitionCard>

#### Reverse-Order Product and Non-Uniqueness
Similarly, if we place $A_{\text{right}}^{-1}$ on the left of $A$, we do not get $I_n$; instead, we get the **orthogonal projection matrix** onto the row space $\operatorname{Row}(A) = \operatorname{Col}(A^T)$ in the input space:

$$
A_{\text{right}}^{-1} A = A^T (A A^T)^{-1} A = P_{\operatorname{Row}(A)} \neq I_n
$$

Here, because $\dim \operatorname{Null}(A) = n - m \ge 1$, taking any matrix $E \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times m}$ whose columns lie in $\operatorname{Null}(A)$ (so $AE = O_{m \times m}$) gives $A(A_{\text{right}}^{-1} + E) = I_m$. Conversely, every right inverse differs from $A_{\text{right}}^{-1}$ by a matrix $E$ with $AE=O$. Scaling a nonzero $E$ gives infinitely many choices.

#### Why Is $A^T (AA^T)^{-1}$ the Canonical Right Inverse? — Minimum Euclidean Norm Exact Solution!
When $r = m \lt n$, the equation $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ has infinitely many exact solutions for **every** $\mathbf{b} \in \mathbb{R}^m$, all of the form $\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{x}_{\text{particular}} + \mathbf{x}_n$ (where $\mathbf{x}_n \in \operatorname{Null}(A)$).
Now examine the solution returned by the canonical right inverse:

$$
\mathbf{x}^+ = A_{\text{right}}^{-1} \mathbf{b} = A^T \left[(AA^T)^{-1} \mathbf{b}\right] \in \operatorname{Col}(A^T) = \operatorname{Row}(A)
$$

Because $\mathbf{x}^+$ is a linear combination of the columns of $A^T$, it **lies completely inside the row space $\operatorname{Row}(A)$ and contains zero $\operatorname{Null}(A)$ component**! By the Pythagorean theorem using $\operatorname{Row}(A) \perp \operatorname{Null}(A)$, among all infinitely many exact solutions, $\mathbf{x}^+$ has the **globally minimum $\ell_2$ length (Minimum $\ell_2$-Norm)**!

**Quick Numerical Comparison**: Take the transpose of our previous example,

$$
A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{2 \times 3}, \qquad \mathbf{b} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}
$$

- The naive right inverse

$$
R_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}
$$

satisfies $AR_1 = I_2$ and gives the exact solution $\mathbf{x}_1 = R_1\mathbf{b} = [1, 1, 0]^T$, whose squared length is $\|\mathbf{x}_1\|_2^2 = 1^2 + 1^2 + 0^2 = 2$.
- The canonical right inverse

$$
A_{\text{right}}^{-1} = \frac{1}{3} \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}
$$

gives the exact solution

$$
\mathbf{x}^+ = A_{\text{right}}^{-1}\mathbf{b} = \frac{1}{3} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}
$$

Checking:

$$
A\mathbf{x}^+ = \begin{bmatrix} 1/3 + 2/3 \\ 1/3 + 2/3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}
$$

while its squared length is only:

$$
\|\mathbf{x}^+\|_2^2 = \left(\frac{1}{3}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{1}{3}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^2 = \frac{6}{9} = \frac{2}{3} \lt 2
$$

This is precisely the result of orthogonally projecting $\mathbf{x}_1$ onto $\operatorname{Row}(A)$ and completely stripping away the extraneous nullspace component $\mathbf{x}_n = \frac{1}{3}[2, 2, -2]^T \in \operatorname{Null}(A)$!

## 2. The General Rank-Deficient Case ($r \lt m, r \lt n$) and the Row-to-Column Space Bijection

Now we face the hardest case, **Case 4**: $r \lt m$ and $r \lt n$. Here:
- $\dim \operatorname{Null}(A) = n - r \ge 1 \implies A^T A$ is **singular and non-invertible**, so $(A^TA)^{-1}A^T$ breaks down completely;
- $\dim \operatorname{Null}(A^T) = m - r \ge 1 \implies A A^T$ is **singular and non-invertible**, so $A^T(AA^T)^{-1}$ breaks down as well!
- More generally, by the rank inequalities $\operatorname{rank}(BA) \le \operatorname{rank}(A) = r \lt n = \operatorname{rank}(I_n)$ and $\operatorname{rank}(AC) \le r \lt m = \operatorname{rank}(I_m)$, **neither a left inverse nor a right inverse can possibly exist**!

Since the full matrix $A : \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$ is neither injective (one-to-one) nor surjective (onto), where can we possibly find an inverse?

Prof. Gilbert Strang offers one of the deepest and most beautiful insights in all of linear algebra: **The only reason $A$ fails to be invertible is that it loses information on $\operatorname{Null}(A)$ and fails to reach $\operatorname{Null}(A^T)$. If we discard both nullspaces and look strictly at the mapping between the row space $\operatorname{Row}(A)$ and the column space $\operatorname{Col}(A)$, then $A$ is always a one-to-one, onto bijection between them!**

<TheoremCard label="Theorem" title="Bijection Theorem from Row(A) to Col(A)">

Let $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ be any real matrix with $\operatorname{rank}(A) = r$. Consider the restriction of the linear map $T(\mathbf{x}) = A\mathbf{x}$ to the row space $\operatorname{Row}(A) \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$:

$$
A|_{\operatorname{Row}(A)} : \operatorname{Row}(A) \longrightarrow \operatorname{Col}(A)
$$

Then this restricted map is always a **one-to-one and onto linear isomorphism (bijection)**; that is, for every $\mathbf{p} \in \operatorname{Col}(A)$, there **exists a unique** vector $\mathbf{x}_r \in \operatorname{Row}(A)$ such that $A\mathbf{x}_r = \mathbf{p}$.

</TheoremCard>

<ProofCollapse title="Rigorous Proof: Bijection Theorem (Surjectivity + Injectivity via Orthogonal Complements)">

**Step 1: Prove Surjectivity (Onto)**
Take any vector $\mathbf{p} \in \operatorname{Col}(A)$. By definition of the column space, there exists at least one vector $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ such that $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{p}$.
Using the orthogonal direct-sum decomposition $\mathbb{R}^n = \operatorname{Row}(A) \oplus \operatorname{Null}(A)$ from the Four Fundamental Subspaces, decompose $\mathbf{x}$ uniquely as:

$$
\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{x}_r + \mathbf{x}_n, \qquad \mathbf{x}_r \in \operatorname{Row}(A), \quad \mathbf{x}_n \in \operatorname{Null}(A)
$$

Applying $A$ and using $A\mathbf{x}_n = \mathbf{0}$:

$$
A\mathbf{x}_r = A(\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}_n) = A\mathbf{x} - A\mathbf{x}_n = \mathbf{p} - \mathbf{0} = \mathbf{p}
$$

Thus every vector in $\operatorname{Col}(A)$ is hit by a vector in $\operatorname{Row}(A)$, proving that $A|_{\operatorname{Row}(A)}$ is **onto (surjective)**.

**Step 2: Prove Injectivity (One-to-One)**
Suppose two row-space vectors $\mathbf{x}_1, \mathbf{x}_2 \in \operatorname{Row}(A)$ have the same image, $A\mathbf{x}_1 = A\mathbf{x}_2$. Rearranging gives:

$$
A(\mathbf{x}_1 - \mathbf{x}_2) = \mathbf{0} \implies \mathbf{x}_1 - \mathbf{x}_2 \in \operatorname{Null}(A)
$$

On the other hand, since $\operatorname{Row}(A)$ is a linear subspace closed under subtraction, we also have $\mathbf{x}_1 - \mathbf{x}_2 \in \operatorname{Row}(A)$. Hence:

$$
\mathbf{x}_1 - \mathbf{x}_2 \in \operatorname{Row}(A) \cap \operatorname{Null}(A)
$$

By the Fundamental Theorem of Linear Algebra ($\operatorname{Row}(A) \perp \operatorname{Null}(A)$), any vector $\mathbf{z} \in \operatorname{Row}(A) \cap \operatorname{Null}(A)$ must be orthogonal to itself:

$$
\mathbf{z}^T \mathbf{z} = \|\mathbf{z}\|_2^2 = 0 \implies \mathbf{z} = \mathbf{0}
$$

Therefore $\mathbf{x}_1 - \mathbf{x}_2 = \mathbf{0}$, so $\mathbf{x}_1 = \mathbf{x}_2$! Combining this with $\dim \operatorname{Row}(A) = \dim \operatorname{Col}(A) = r$, the linear map is both injective and surjective, establishing a strict **bijection** between $\operatorname{Row}(A)$ and $\operatorname{Col}(A)$.

</ProofCollapse>

This single insight clears away all the fog: if we take the **exact inverse** of this bijection on $\operatorname{Col}(A) \to \operatorname{Row}(A)$, and simultaneously send the orthogonal complement $\operatorname{Null}(A^T)$ straight to the zero vector $\mathbf{0} \in \mathbb{R}^n$, we naturally obtain the unique, ultimate generalized inverse for **every matrix**—the **Moore-Penrose Pseudoinverse $A^+$**!

## 3. Explicit Construction of $A^+$ via the SVD

The Bijection Theorem guarantees the existence of this inverse abstractly, and the SVD $A = U\Sigma V^T$ from Lecture 30 hands us a surgical scalpel to **write down $A^+$ explicitly in three seconds**!

Recall the full and compact SVD of $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ with $\operatorname{rank}(A) = r$:

$$
A = U \Sigma V^T = U_r \Sigma_r V_r^T = \sum_{i=1}^r \sigma_i \mathbf{u}_i \mathbf{v}_i^T
$$

Here:
- $\{\mathbf{v}_1, \dots, \mathbf{v}_r\}$ forms an orthonormal basis of $\operatorname{Row}(A)$, and $A$ maps those vectors to positive multiples of the orthonormal basis $\{\mathbf{u}_1, \dots, \mathbf{u}_r\}$ of $\operatorname{Col}(A)$:
  $$
  A \mathbf{v}_i = \sigma_i \mathbf{u}_i \quad (\sigma_i \gt 0, \; 1 \le i \le r)
  $$
- The remaining right singular vectors $\{\mathbf{v}_{r+1}, \dots, \mathbf{v}_n\}$ form an orthonormal basis of $\operatorname{Null}(A)$ ($A\mathbf{v}_i = \mathbf{0}$);
- The remaining left singular vectors $\{\mathbf{u}_{r+1}, \dots, \mathbf{u}_m\}$ form an orthonormal basis of $\operatorname{Null}(A^T)$.

Therefore, for $A^+$ to act as the exact inverse of $A$ on $\operatorname{Col}(A) \to \operatorname{Row}(A)$ while sending $\operatorname{Null}(A^T)$ to zero, we simply require:

$$
A^+ \mathbf{u}_i = \frac{1}{\sigma_i} \mathbf{v}_i \quad (1 \le i \le r), \qquad A^+ \mathbf{u}_i = \mathbf{0} \quad (r + 1 \le i \le m)
$$

Writing this in matrix factorization form immediately yields one of the most celebrated definitions in linear algebra:

<DefinitionCard label="Definition" title="SVD Construction of the Moore-Penrose Pseudoinverse and the Four Penrose Equations">

Let any real matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ have SVD $A = U \Sigma V^T$ with $\operatorname{rank}(A) = r$, where

$$
\Sigma = \begin{bmatrix} \Sigma_r & O \\ O & O \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}, \qquad \Sigma_r = \operatorname{diag}(\sigma_1, \dots, \sigma_r) \quad (\sigma_1 \ge \dots \ge \sigma_r \gt 0).
$$

1. **Pseudoinverse Singular-Value Matrix $\Sigma^+ \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times m}$**: Transpose $\Sigma$ to **$n \times m$ shape**, invert every nonzero singular value $\sigma_i \mapsto \frac{1}{\sigma_i}$, and **leave every zero entry unchanged as $0$**:
   $$
   \Sigma^+ := \begin{bmatrix} \Sigma_r^{-1} & O_{r \times (m-r)} \\ O_{(n-r) \times r} & O_{(n-r) \times (m-r)} \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times m}, \qquad \Sigma_r^{-1} = \operatorname{diag}\!\left(\frac{1}{\sigma_1}, \dots, \frac{1}{\sigma_r}\right)
   $$
   For $r=0$, $\Sigma^+$ and $A^+$ are zero matrices of the indicated sizes, and the sum below is empty.
2. **Moore-Penrose Pseudoinverse $A^+ \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times m}$**:
   $$
   A^+ := V \Sigma^+ U^T = V_r \Sigma_r^{-1} U_r^T = \sum_{i=1}^r \frac{1}{\sigma_i} \mathbf{v}_i \mathbf{u}_i^T
   $$
   (For a complex matrix $A \in \mathbb{C}^{m \times n}$, replace transpose with conjugate transpose: $A^+ = V \Sigma^+ U^H$.)
3. **The Four Penrose Equations**: For any matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$, $A^+$ is the **unique** $n \times m$ matrix $X$ satisfying all four matrix equations simultaneously:
   - **(P1) Generalized Inverse**: $A X A = A$
   - **(P2) Reflexivity**: $X A X = X$
   - **(P3) Left Symmetry**: $(A X)^T = A X$ (in fact, $AA^+ = P_{\operatorname{Col}(A)}$)
   - **(P4) Right Symmetry**: $(X A)^T = X A$ (in fact, $A^+A = P_{\operatorname{Row}(A)}$)

</DefinitionCard>

Why does $A^+ = V\Sigma^+ U^T$ satisfy all four Penrose equations? Why is the matrix satisfying these four equations necessarily unique? And how does it unify the square inverse, canonical left inverse, and canonical right inverse from Section 1?

<ProofCollapse title="Rigorous Proof: VΣ⁺Uᵀ Satisfies All Four Penrose Equations, Uniqueness, and Reduction to Special Cases">

**Step 1: Verify the Four Penrose Equations and Orthogonal Projections**
First compute $\Sigma \Sigma^+ \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times m}$ and $\Sigma^+ \Sigma \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ directly:

$$
\Sigma \Sigma^+ = \begin{bmatrix} I_r & O \\ O & O_{(m-r) \times (m-r)} \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times m}, \qquad \Sigma^+ \Sigma = \begin{bmatrix} I_r & O \\ O & O_{(n-r) \times (n-r)} \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}
$$

Using $U^T U = I_m$ and $V^T V = I_n$, we immediately obtain the two products:

$$
A A^+ = (U \Sigma V^T)(V \Sigma^+ U^T) = U (\Sigma \Sigma^+) U^T = U_r U_r^T = P_{\operatorname{Col}(A)}
$$

$$
A^+ A = (V \Sigma^+ U^T)(U \Sigma V^T) = V (\Sigma^+ \Sigma) V^T = V_r V_r^T = P_{\operatorname{Row}(A)}
$$

By symmetry of $U_r U_r^T$ and $V_r V_r^T$, we have $(AA^+)^T = AA^+$ and $(A^+A)^T = A^+A$, verifying **(P3)** and **(P4)**!

Next verify **(P1)** and **(P2)**: since $\Sigma (\Sigma^+ \Sigma) = \Sigma$ and $\Sigma^+ (\Sigma \Sigma^+) = \Sigma^+$, we get:

$$
A A^+ A = U (\Sigma \Sigma^+ \Sigma) V^T = U \Sigma V^T = A
$$

$$
A^+ A A^+ = V (\Sigma^+ \Sigma \Sigma^+) U^T = V \Sigma^+ U^T = A^+
$$

All four Penrose conditions hold!

**Step 2: Prove Global Uniqueness of $X$ Satisfying (P1)–(P4)**
Suppose $X, Y \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times m}$ both satisfy (P1)–(P4). Using (P2) and (P3) for $X$, together with (P1) and (P3) for $Y$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
X &= X A X = X (A X)^T = X X^T A^T \\
  &= X X^T (A Y A)^T = X X^T A^T Y^T A^T \\
  &= X (A X)^T (A Y)^T = X (A X)(A Y) = (X A X) A Y = X A Y
\end{aligned}
$$

Symmetrically, using (P2) and (P4) for $Y$, together with (P1) and (P4) for $X$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
Y &= Y A Y = (Y A)^T Y = A^T Y^T Y \\
  &= (A X A)^T Y^T Y = A^T X^T A^T Y^T Y \\
  &= (X A)^T (Y A)^T Y = (X A)(Y A) Y = X A (Y A Y) = X A Y
\end{aligned}
$$

Hence $X = X A Y = Y$, proving uniqueness of the pseudoinverse!

**Step 3: Verify That $A^+$ Automatically Reduces to Cases 1, 2, and 3**
- **Case 1 ($r = m = n$)**: $\Sigma^+ = \Sigma^{-1}$, so $A^+ = V\Sigma^{-1}U^T = (U\Sigma V^T)^{-1} = A^{-1}$.
- **Case 2 (Full Column Rank $r = n \lt m$)**: Here $V_r = V$ is an $n \times n$ orthogonal matrix, so $A = U_r \Sigma_r V^T$ and $A^T A = V \Sigma_r^2 V^T$ is invertible. Substituting into the canonical left inverse gives:
  $$
  (A^T A)^{-1} A^T = (V \Sigma_r^{-2} V^T)(V \Sigma_r U_r^T) = V \Sigma_r^{-1} U_r^T = A^+
  $$
- **Case 3 (Full Row Rank $r = m \lt n$)**: Here $U_r = U$ is an $m \times m$ orthogonal matrix, so $A = U \Sigma_r V_r^T$ and $A A^T = U \Sigma_r^2 U^T$ is invertible. Substituting into the canonical right inverse gives:
  $$
  A^T (A A^T)^{-1} = (V_r \Sigma_r U^T)(U \Sigma_r^{-2} U^T) = V_r \Sigma_r^{-1} U^T = A^+
  $$

Thus $A^+$ completely unifies all four matrix cases in linear algebra!

</ProofCollapse>

### Worked Example by Hand: Full Pseudoinverse of a Rank-1 Matrix

Consider the $2 \times 3$ rank-1 matrix (Case 4):

$$
A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 & 4 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 2 \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{2 \times 3} \qquad (r = 1 \lt 2, \; r = 1 \lt 3)
$$

Notice that both $A^T A$ and $AA^T$ have rank $1$, so **both are singular and non-invertible**! Yet constructing $A^+$ via the SVD is effortless:
1. Normalize the outer-product vectors:
   $$
   \mathbf{u}_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1^2+2^2}} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{5}} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{v}_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1^2+2^2+2^2}} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{3} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}
   $$
2. The single nonzero singular value is $\sigma_1 = 3\sqrt{5}$, giving the compact SVD $A = (3\sqrt{5})\,\mathbf{u}_1 \mathbf{v}_1^T$.
3. Invert $\sigma_1$ and swap $\mathbf{u}_1$ and $\mathbf{v}_1$ to write the $3 \times 2$ pseudoinverse:
   $$
   A^+ = \frac{1}{\sigma_1} \mathbf{v}_1 \mathbf{u}_1^T = \frac{1}{3\sqrt{5}} \left(\frac{1}{3} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}\right) \left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{5}} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\right) = \frac{1}{45} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \\ 2 & 4 \end{bmatrix}
   $$
4. Verify both orthogonal projection matrices:
   $$
   A^+ A = \frac{1}{9} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 & 4 \\ 2 & 4 & 4 \end{bmatrix} = \mathbf{v}_1 \mathbf{v}_1^T = P_{\operatorname{Row}(A)}, \qquad A A^+ = \frac{1}{5} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{bmatrix} = \mathbf{u}_1 \mathbf{u}_1^T = P_{\operatorname{Col}(A)}
   $$
5. Test an unreachable right-hand side $\mathbf{b} = [5, 0]^T \notin \operatorname{Col}(A)$:
   - The pseudoinverse returns the unique minimum-norm least-squares solution:
     $$
     \mathbf{x}^+ = A^+ \mathbf{b} = \frac{1}{45} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \\ 2 & 4 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{9} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}
     $$
     with squared length $\|\mathbf{x}^+\|_2^2 = \frac{1 + 4 + 4}{81} = \frac{1}{9}$.
   - Its image in the output space is $\mathbf{p} = A\mathbf{x}^+ = [1, 2]^T$, leaving the orthogonal residual $\mathbf{e} = \mathbf{b} - \mathbf{p} = [4, -2]^T \perp \mathbf{u}_1$ (minimum squared residual $\|\mathbf{e}\|_2^2 = 20$).
   - If we arbitrarily add a nullspace vector $\mathbf{x}_n = [2, -1, 0]^T \in \operatorname{Null}(A)$, we get another least-squares solution $\hat{\mathbf{x}} = \mathbf{x}^+ + \mathbf{x}_n = \frac{1}{9} [19, -7, 2]^T$. It produces the exact same projection $A\hat{\mathbf{x}} = [1, 2]^T$ and the same squared residual $20$, but its squared length blows up to $\|\hat{\mathbf{x}}\|_2^2 = \frac{361 + 49 + 4}{81} = \frac{46}{9} = \frac{1}{9} + 5 = \|\mathbf{x}^+\|_2^2 + \|\mathbf{x}_n\|_2^2$!

## 4. Core Application of $A^+$: The Unique Minimum-Norm Least-Squares Theorem

The numerical example above is no accident. It embodies the crowning property that makes the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse the gold standard of numerical linear algebra and modern scientific computing: when $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ has **no exact solution** and simultaneously has **infinitely many solutions minimizing the error**, $A^+\mathbf{b}$ automatically selects the **shortest vector** among all least-squares solutions!

<MinimumNormLeastSquaresGeometryDiagram caption="Geometry of the minimum-norm least-squares solution: in the output space, b projects orthogonally onto p = AA⁺b in Col(A); in the input space, the affine least-squares solution set x⁺ + Null(A) has a unique closest point to the origin at x⁺ = A⁺b in Row(A)." />

<TheoremCard label="Theorem" title="Unique Minimum-Norm Least-Squares Solution Theorem">

Let $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ be **any** real matrix (of arbitrary rank $r$) and $\mathbf{b} \in \mathbb{R}^m$ be any target vector. Set $\mathbf{x}^+ := A^+ \mathbf{b} \in \mathbb{R}^n$. Then:

1. **Least-Squares Optimality**: $\mathbf{x}^+$ minimizes the residual norm $\|A\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{b}\|_2$, satisfying $A\mathbf{x}^+ = P_{\operatorname{Col}(A)}\mathbf{b}$; moreover, the full set of least-squares minimizers forms an affine subspace parallel to $\operatorname{Null}(A)$:
   $$
   \mathcal{S}_{\text{LS}} := \left\{ \hat{\mathbf{x}} \in \mathbb{R}^n \;\middle|\; \|A\hat{\mathbf{x}} - \mathbf{b}\|_2 = \min_{\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n} \|A\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{b}\|_2 \right\} = \mathbf{x}^+ + \operatorname{Null}(A)
   $$
2. **Unique Minimum $\ell_2$-Norm**: Among all candidates in $\mathcal{S}_{\text{LS}}$, $\mathbf{x}^+$ is the **unique** vector achieving the strict global minimum Euclidean length $\|\mathbf{x}\|_2$:
   $$
   \|\mathbf{x}^+\|_2 \lt \|\hat{\mathbf{x}}\|_2 \qquad \left(\forall \hat{\mathbf{x}} \in \mathcal{S}_{\text{LS}}, \;\; \hat{\mathbf{x}} \neq \mathbf{x}^+\right)
   $$

</TheoremCard>

<ProofCollapse title="Rigorous Proof: Two-Step Proof of the Minimum-Norm Least-Squares Theorem (Orthogonal Projection + Pythagorean Theorem)">

**Step 1: Prove Least-Squares Optimality and the Solution Set $\mathcal{S}_{\text{LS}} = \mathbf{x}^+ + \operatorname{Null}(A)$**
Using the orthogonal direct-sum decomposition of the output space $\mathbb{R}^m = \operatorname{Col}(A) \oplus \operatorname{Null}(A^T)$, decompose $\mathbf{b}$ into its projection and residual components:

$$
\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{p} + \mathbf{e}, \qquad \mathbf{p} = P_{\operatorname{Col}(A)}\mathbf{b} = AA^+\mathbf{b} \in \operatorname{Col}(A), \quad \mathbf{e} = \mathbf{b} - \mathbf{p} \in \operatorname{Null}(A^T)
$$

For any $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n$, we have $A\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{p} \in \operatorname{Col}(A)$ and $\mathbf{e} \in \operatorname{Null}(A^T) = \operatorname{Col}(A)^\perp$, so the two vectors are strictly orthogonal. Applying the Pythagorean theorem:

$$
\|A\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{b}\|_2^2 = \|(A\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{p}) - \mathbf{e}\|_2^2 = \|A\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{p}\|_2^2 + \|\mathbf{e}\|_2^2 \ge \|\mathbf{e}\|_2^2
$$

Equality holds if and only if $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{p}$. Since $A\mathbf{x}^+ = A(A^+\mathbf{b}) = \mathbf{p}$, $\mathbf{x}^+$ attains the minimum residual $\|\mathbf{e}\|_2$; furthermore, for any $\hat{\mathbf{x}} \in \mathbb{R}^n$:

$$
\hat{\mathbf{x}} \in \mathcal{S}_{\text{LS}} \iff A\hat{\mathbf{x}} = \mathbf{p} = A\mathbf{x}^+ \iff A(\hat{\mathbf{x}} - \mathbf{x}^+) = \mathbf{0} \iff \hat{\mathbf{x}} - \mathbf{x}^+ \in \operatorname{Null}(A)
$$

This characterizes the entire least-squares solution set $\mathcal{S}_{\text{LS}} = \mathbf{x}^+ + \operatorname{Null}(A)$.

**Step 2: Prove the Unique Minimum-Norm Property**
Examine the SVD expansion of $\mathbf{x}^+$:

$$
\mathbf{x}^+ = A^+ \mathbf{b} = \sum_{i=1}^r \frac{\mathbf{u}_i^T \mathbf{b}}{\sigma_i} \mathbf{v}_i \in \operatorname{span}\{\mathbf{v}_1, \dots, \mathbf{v}_r\} = \operatorname{Row}(A)
$$

This shows that $\mathbf{x}^+$ **lies entirely inside the row space $\operatorname{Row}(A)$**. Now take any $\hat{\mathbf{x}} \in \mathcal{S}_{\text{LS}}$ and write it as $\hat{\mathbf{x}} = \mathbf{x}^+ + \mathbf{x}_n$, where $\mathbf{x}_n \in \operatorname{Null}(A)$.
Because $\operatorname{Row}(A) \perp \operatorname{Null}(A)$, we must have $(\mathbf{x}^+)^T \mathbf{x}_n = 0$. Applying the Pythagorean theorem a second time:

$$
\|\hat{\mathbf{x}}\|_2^2 = \|\mathbf{x}^+ + \mathbf{x}_n\|_2^2 = \|\mathbf{x}^+\|_2^2 + \|\mathbf{x}_n\|_2^2 \ge \|\mathbf{x}^+\|_2^2
$$

Equality holds if and only if $\|\mathbf{x}_n\|_2^2 = 0$, i.e., $\mathbf{x}_n = \mathbf{0} \iff \hat{\mathbf{x}} = \mathbf{x}^+$. Hence $\mathbf{x}^+ = A^+\mathbf{b}$ is the globally unique minimum-norm least-squares solution!

</ProofCollapse>

#### A Note on Ill-Conditioned Matrices and Regularization
In practical numerical computation, if a nonzero singular value $\sigma_r \gt 0$ is tiny (meaning $A$ is nearly rank-deficient), the pseudoinverse term $\frac{1}{\sigma_r} (\mathbf{u}_r^T \mathbf{b}) \mathbf{v}_r$ amplifies small measurement noise in $\mathbf{b}$ by a factor of $1/\sigma_r$. In engineering, one either uses a **Truncated SVD** (treating singular values below a threshold as zero) or introduces **Tikhonov (Ridge) Regularization**:

$$
\mathbf{x}_\lambda = (A^T A + \lambda I_n)^{-1} A^T \mathbf{b} \qquad (\lambda \gt 0)
$$

In the SVD basis, the filter factor for each singular value is $\frac{\sigma_i}{\sigma_i^2 + \lambda}$: when $\sigma_i \gt 0$, $\lim_{\lambda \to 0^+} \frac{\sigma_i}{\sigma_i^2 + \lambda} = \frac{1}{\sigma_i}$, whereas when $\sigma_i = 0$, the term is identically $0$ even before taking the limit! This perturbation limit gives a deep justification for why the pseudoinverse must define **$0^+ = 0$** rather than $\infty$.

<PitfallAlert title="Common Pitfalls & Pseudoinverse Errata">

### 1. Mistaking $(A^TA)^{-1}A^T$ for a Universal Pseudoinverse Formula
Many students memorize the least-squares formula $\hat{\mathbf{x}} = (A^TA)^{-1}A^T\mathbf{b}$ and assume $A^+ = (A^TA)^{-1}A^T$ holds for every matrix. **Serious error!**
- Whenever $r = \operatorname{rank}(A) \lt n$ (including both Case 3, short-and-wide full row rank $r = m \lt n$, and Case 4, general rank-deficient matrices), the matrix $A^T A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ also has rank $r \lt n$—its **determinant is strictly zero and it is not invertible**!
- Likewise, $A^T(AA^T)^{-1}$ holds **only when $A$ has full row rank $r = m$**. Only $A^+ = V\Sigma^+ U^T$ works in all weather for every matrix!

### 2. Assuming the Reverse-Order Law $(AB)^+ = B^+ A^+$ Holds for Pseudoinverses
For standard invertible square matrices, $(AB)^{-1} = B^{-1}A^{-1}$ is an iron law; for pseudoinverses, **$(AB)^+ = B^+ A^+$ is generally FALSE**!
- **Minimal Counterexample**: Take

$$
A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times 2}, \qquad B = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{2 \times 1}
$$
  - Their product is $AB = [1] \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times 1}$, so $(AB)^+ = [1]$.
  - However, $A$ has full row rank with $A^+ = A^T(AA^T)^{-1} = [1/2, 1/2]^T$, and $B$ has full column rank with $B^+ = (B^TB)^{-1}B^T = [1, 0]$.
  - Computing the reverse-order product gives:
    $$
    B^+ A^+ = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1/2 \\ 1/2 \end{bmatrix} = \left[\frac{1}{2}\right] \neq [1] = (AB)^+
    $$
- **Why**: $(AB)^+ = B^+ A^+$ holds only when the intermediate subspace suffers no oblique distortion (for example, when $A$ has orthonormal columns $A^TA = I$, or $B$ has orthonormal rows $BB^T = I$, or under Greville's general necessary and sufficient conditions).

### 3. Forgetting to Transpose the Shape of $\Sigma^+$, or Setting $0^+ = \infty$
- **Shape must be transposed**: If $A, \Sigma \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$, then $A^+, \Sigma^+ \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times m}$.
- **$0^+ = 0$, never $\infty$**: Zero singular values in $\Sigma^+$ must stay $0$ (mapping $\operatorname{Null}(A^T)$ to $\mathbf{0}$), which corresponds to discarding unreachable residuals in the output space—never divide by $0$.

### 4. Confusing an Arbitrary Left/Right Inverse with the Moore-Penrose Pseudoinverse $A^+$
A tall full-column-rank matrix has infinitely many left inverses (such as a truncated inverse $L_1$ built by dropping extra rows), all satisfying $L_1 A = I_n$. Yet every left inverse other than $A^+ = (A^TA)^{-1}A^T$ fails Penrose condition (P3), $(AL_1)^T = AL_1$ (giving an oblique projection instead of an orthogonal projection), so **none of them is the pseudoinverse**!

</PitfallAlert>

<SolutionIntuition>
The two columns of $A$ are perpendicular in $\mathbb{R}^3$, which makes inverting $A^T A$ mental arithmetic—just divide each row of $A^T$ by the squared length of the corresponding column of $A$. Every left inverse behaves identically on $\operatorname{Col}(A)$ (recovering $\mathbf{x}$ from $A\mathbf{x}$); they differ entirely in **how they treat the left nullspace $\operatorname{Null}(A^T)$**. The canonical left inverse $A^+$ ignores $\operatorname{Null}(A^T)$ completely (mapping it to $\mathbf{0}$), making $AA^+$ a clean orthogonal projection; by contrast, the hand-crafted left inverse $B$ smuggles in a nonzero response along $\operatorname{Null}(A^T)$, turning $AB$ into a tilted oblique projection.
</SolutionIntuition>
<SolutionRigorous>
**Part 1: Compute the Canonical Left Inverse $A^+$**
Compute $A^T A$:

$$
A^T A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 6 \end{bmatrix}
$$

This is an invertible diagonal matrix with $(A^T A)^{-1} = \operatorname{diag}(1/2, 1/6)$. Hence:

$$
A^+ = (A^T A)^{-1} A^T = \begin{bmatrix} 1/2 & 0 \\ 0 & 1/6 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1/2 & 1/2 & 0 \\ 1/6 & -1/6 & 1/3 \end{bmatrix}
$$

Verify the left product:

$$
A^+ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1/2 & 1/2 & 0 \\ 1/6 & -1/6 & 1/3 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1/2 + 1/2 & 1/2 - 1/2 \\ 1/6 - 1/6 & 1/6 + 1/6 + 2/3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = I_2
$$

**Part 2: Compute $AA^+$ and Check the Left Nullspace**

$$
AA^+ = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1/2 & 1/2 & 0 \\ 1/6 & -1/6 & 1/3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2/3 & 1/3 & 1/3 \\ 1/3 & 2/3 & -1/3 \\ 1/3 & -1/3 & 2/3 \end{bmatrix}
$$

Clearly $(AA^+)^T = AA^+$ (symmetric), and $\operatorname{tr}(AA^+) = 2/3 + 2/3 + 2/3 = 2 = \operatorname{rank}(A)$. Since $(AA^+)^2 = A(A^+A)A^+ = AA^+$, it is the orthogonal projection matrix onto $\operatorname{Col}(A)$.
Solving $A^T \mathbf{n} = \mathbf{0}$ gives $n_1 + n_2 = 0$ and $n_1 - n_2 + 2n_3 = 0$. Taking $n_1 = 1$ yields $n_2 = -1, n_3 = -1$, so $\mathbf{n} = [1, -1, -1]^T \in \operatorname{Null}(A^T)$. Checking:

$$
AA^+ \mathbf{n} = \frac{1}{3} \begin{bmatrix} 2 - 1 - 1 \\ 1 - 2 + 1 \\ 1 + 1 - 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}
$$

**Part 3: Construct Another Left Inverse $B \neq A^+$**
Let $A_1$ be the first two rows of $A$:

$$
A_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad A_1^{-1} = \frac{1}{2} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}
$$

Form the $2 \times 3$ matrix:

$$
B = \begin{bmatrix} 1/2 & 1/2 & 0 \\ 1/2 & -1/2 & 0 \end{bmatrix}
$$

Then $BA = A_1^{-1} A_1 + O = I_2$, so $B$ is indeed a valid left inverse of $A$. However, computing the reverse-order product gives:

$$
AB = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1/2 & 1/2 & 0 \\ 1/2 & -1/2 & 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & -1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}
$$

Clearly $(AB)^T \neq AB$, violating Penrose condition (P3) ($AB$ is an oblique projection, not an orthogonal projection, and $B\mathbf{n} = [0, 1]^T \neq \mathbf{0}$). Thus $B$ is not the pseudoinverse of $A$.

<SolutionIntuition>
This problem sets two classic traps. The first trap confuses "there is 1 canonical left inverse" with "there is only 1 left inverse"—in reality, the $(m - n)$-dimensional left nullspace leaves an entire free space where you can alter the left inverse's behavior on residuals. The second trap blindly copies the "socks-and-shoes" rule $(AB)^{-1} = B^{-1}A^{-1}$ onto pseudoinverses: whenever $A$ stretches different directions unequally ($A^T A \neq cI$) or $B$ projects onto a mismatched subspace, computing the pseudoinverses separately and multiplying them gets the directional weighting wrong!
</SolutionIntuition>
<SolutionRigorous>
**Both parts of the compound statement are false.** We refute each with an explicit construction:

**Part 1: Infinitely many left inverses when $r = n \lt m$**
Because $\operatorname{rank}(A) = n \lt m$, $\dim \operatorname{Null}(A^T) = m - n \ge 1$, so there exists a nonzero vector $\mathbf{y} \in \operatorname{Null}(A^T)$ (satisfying $\mathbf{y}^T A = \mathbf{0}^T$).
Construct the nonzero $n \times m$ matrix:

$$
D = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{y}^T \\ \mathbf{0}^T \\ \vdots \\ \mathbf{0}^T \end{bmatrix}
$$

Clearly $DA = O_{n \times n}$. For every scalar $t \in \mathbb{R}$, define $L(t) = (A^TA)^{-1}A^T + t D$; then:

$$
L(t) A = (A^TA)^{-1}A^T A + t (DA) = I_n + O = I_n
$$

Hence $L(t)$ is a distinct left inverse of $A$ for every $t \in \mathbb{R}$.

**Part 2: Counterexample to $(AB)^+ = B^+ A^+$ with $r = n \lt m$**
Choose $m = 3, n = 2, k = 1$:

$$
A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 2} \quad (\operatorname{rank}(A) = 2 = n \lt m), \qquad B = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{2 \times 1}
$$

1. Compute the product and its pseudoinverse:
   $$
   AB = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 1} \implies (AB)^+ = \frac{(AB)^T}{\|AB\|_2^2} = \frac{1}{5} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1/5 & 2/5 & 0 \end{bmatrix}
   $$
2. Compute $A^+$ and $B^+$ separately:
   - $A$ has full column rank with $A^TA = \operatorname{diag}(1, 4)$, so
     $$
     A^+ = (A^TA)^{-1}A^T = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1/2 & 0 \end{bmatrix}
     $$
   - $B$ has full column rank with $B^TB = [2]$, so $B^+ = [1/2, 1/2]$.
3. Multiply $B^+ A^+$:
   $$
   B^+ A^+ = \begin{bmatrix} 1/2 & 1/2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1/2 & 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1/2 & 1/4 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \neq \begin{bmatrix} 1/5 & 2/5 & 0 \end{bmatrix} = (AB)^+
   $$

The statement is completely refuted!
</SolutionRigorous>

<SolutionIntuition>

<SolutionRigorous>
**Part 1 Proof:**
- **Prove $(A^+)^+ = A$**: Check the four Penrose conditions for the base matrix $B := A^+$ and candidate pseudoinverse $Y := A$:
  1. $B Y B = A^+ A A^+ = A^+ = B$ (holds by P2 for $A^+$);
  2. $Y B Y = A A^+ A = A = Y$ (holds by P1 for $A^+$);
  3. $(B Y)^T = (A^+ A)^T = A^+ A = B Y$ (holds by P4 for $A^+$);
  4. $(Y B)^T = (A A^+)^T = A A^+ = Y B$ (holds by P3 for $A^+$).
  By uniqueness of the matrix satisfying the four Penrose equations, $B^+ = Y$, i.e., $(A^+)^+ = A$!
- **Prove $(A^T)^+ = (A^+)^T$**: Take the transpose of all four Penrose equations satisfied by $A^+$:
  1. $A^T (A^+)^T A^T = (A A^+ A)^T = A^T$;
  2. $(A^+)^T A^T (A^+)^T = (A^+ A A^+)^T = (A^+)^T$;
  3. $(A^T (A^+)^T)^T = (A^+ A)^T = A^+ A = (A^+ A)^T = A^T (A^+)^T$;
  4. $((A^+)^T A^T)^T = (A A^+)^T = A A^+ = (A^+)^T A^T$.
  By uniqueness, $(A^T)^+ = (A^+)^T$! (Alternatively, via SVD: $A^T = V \Sigma^T U^T \implies (A^T)^+ = U (\Sigma^T)^+ V^T = U (\Sigma^+)^T V^T = (V\Sigma^+ U^T)^T = (A^+)^T$.)

**Part 2 Proof:**
Using $A = U\Sigma V^T$ ($U^T U = I_m, V^T V = I_n$), compute $A^T A$:

$$
A^T A = V (\Sigma^T \Sigma) V^T = V \begin{bmatrix} \Sigma_r^2 & O \\ O & O_{(n-r) \times (n-r)} \end{bmatrix} V^T
$$

where $\Sigma_r^2 = \operatorname{diag}(\sigma_1^2, \dots, \sigma_r^2)$ with $\sigma_1^2 \ge \dots \ge \sigma_r^2 \gt 0$. Since $V$ is orthogonal, this is already the SVD (and spectral decomposition) of the symmetric positive semidefinite matrix $A^T A$! Its pseudoinverse is therefore:

$$
(A^T A)^+ = V \begin{bmatrix} \Sigma_r^{-2} & O \\ O & O_{(n-r) \times (n-r)} \end{bmatrix} V^T
$$

On the other hand, compute $A^+ (A^+)^T$:

$$
A^+ (A^+)^T = (V \Sigma^+ U^T)(U (\Sigma^+)^T V^T) = V \left(\Sigma^+ (\Sigma^+)^T\right) V^T = V \begin{bmatrix} \Sigma_r^{-2} & O \\ O & O_{(n-r) \times (n-r)} \end{bmatrix} V^T
$$

Both sides are identical, so $(A^T A)^+ = A^+ (A^+)^T$!

**Part 3 Proof and Explanation:**
Multiply $(A^T A)^+$ from Part 2 on the right by $A^T = V \Sigma^T U^T$:

$$
(A^T A)^+ A^T = V \begin{bmatrix} \Sigma_r^{-2} & O_{r \times (n-r)} \\ O_{(n-r) \times r} & O \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \Sigma_r & O_{r \times (m-r)} \\ O_{(n-r) \times r} & O \end{bmatrix} U^T = V \begin{bmatrix} \Sigma_r^{-1} & O_{r \times (m-r)} \\ O_{(n-r) \times r} & O \end{bmatrix} U^T = V \Sigma^+ U^T = A^+
$$

**Meaning**: When $A$ has full column rank ($r = n$), $A^T A$ is invertible, so $(A^TA)^+ = (A^TA)^{-1}$ and the formula collapses to the familiar $(A^TA)^{-1}A^T$. When $r \lt n$ and $A^T A$ becomes singular, $(A^TA)^+$ takes reciprocals of the $r$ positive eigenvalues while setting the zero eigenvalues to zero via $0^+ = 0$, cleanly filtering out $\operatorname{Null}(A)$ and still recovering the exact unique $A^+$!
</SolutionRigorous>

$$

### 1. Which least-squares solution does gradient descent select?

Fix a real feature matrix $A\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d}$ and targets $\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^N$. Full-batch gradient descent on the squared loss uses

$$
L(\mathbf{w})=\frac12\|A\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{y}\|_2^2,\qquad
\mathbf{w}_{t+1}=\mathbf{w}_t-\eta A^T(A\mathbf{w}_t-\mathbf{y}).
$$

When $\operatorname{rank}(A)\lt d$, the least-squares solution set is $A^+\mathbf{y}+\operatorname{Null}(A)$. If $\mathbf{y}\in\operatorname{Col}(A)$, these solutions all interpolate the training data. The update rule and initialization determine which solution is selected. This is the implicit bias in this example.

### 2. Prove convergence in SVD coordinates

Set $\mathbf{w}^+=A^+\mathbf{y}$. The normal equations give $A^T(A\mathbf{w}^+-\mathbf{y})=\mathbf{0}$. Subtracting the fixed-point equation for $\mathbf{w}^+$ from the update gives

$$
\mathbf{w}_{t+1}-\mathbf{w}^+=(I_d-\eta A^T A)(\mathbf{w}_t-\mathbf{w}^+).
$$

Along a right singular vector $\mathbf{v}_i$ with $\sigma_i\gt 0$, each step multiplies the error by $1-\eta\sigma_i^2$. For $A\ne O$ and $0\lt\eta\lt 2/\sigma_1(A)^2$, each such factor has absolute value less than $1$. Along the nullspace the factor is $1$, so its component stays fixed. Therefore

$$
\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathbf{w}_t=A^+\mathbf{y}+P_{\operatorname{Null}(A)}\mathbf{w}_0.
$$

If $A=O$, the gradient is $\mathbf{0}$ and the same limit holds for any $\eta\gt 0$. Zero initialization gives the unique minimum-norm least-squares solution $A^+\mathbf{y}$. For general initialization the limit is the least-squares solution closest to $\mathbf{w}_0$, by orthogonal projection onto the affine solution set.

### 3. How does the nullspace component affect a new prediction?

For a test feature vector $\mathbf{a}$, the initial nullspace component changes the prediction by $\mathbf{a}^T P_{\operatorname{Null}(A)}\mathbf{w}_0$. Its magnitude is bounded by $\|\mathbf{a}\|_2\|P_{\operatorname{Null}(A)}\mathbf{w}_0\|_2$, and it vanishes when $\mathbf{a}\in\operatorname{Row}(A)$. This result characterizes solution selection in the fixed linear model. Test error also depends on the data distribution.

"""
Verify the core theorem of Lecture 34 AI-15: Implicit Bias of Overparameterized Gradient Descent (GD)
1. On an overparameterized and rank-deficient linear model (N=10, d=40, rank=8):
   - Zero-initialized unregularized GD converges automatically and exactly to the
     Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse solution A^+ y (the unique minimum L2-norm solution);
   - Random nonzero-initialized GD achieves the exact same zero training loss, but
     permanently freezes the initial nullspace component P_Null(w0), converging to
     A^+ y + P_Null(w0) (the least-squares solution closest to w0), with strictly larger norm!
2. Also verify that in the inconsistent case (with orthogonal noise in Null(A^T)),
   zero-initialized GD still converges to A^+ y.
"""
import torch

def verify_implicit_bias_pseudoinverse():
    torch.manual_seed(34)

    # Construct a short-and-wide, rank-deficient matrix A in R^{10 x 40}, rank(A) = 8 < N < d
    N, d, r = 10, 40, 8
    U_sub, _ = torch.linalg.qr(torch.randn(N, r, dtype=torch.float64))
    V_sub, _ = torch.linalg.qr(torch.randn(d, r, dtype=torch.float64))
    S_sub = torch.linspace(3.0, 0.8, r, dtype=torch.float64)
    A = U_sub @ torch.diag(S_sub) @ V_sub.T

    # 1. Consistent overparameterized interpolation data: y = A @ w_true
    w_true = torch.randn(d, dtype=torch.float64)
    y = A @ w_true

    # Compute the theoretical unique minimum-norm pseudoinverse solution w^+ = A^+ y
    w_pinv = torch.linalg.pinv(A) @ y

    # Safe convergence step size: 0 < eta < 2 / sigma_1^2
    sigma_1 = S_sub[0].item()
    eta = 1.0 / (sigma_1 ** 2)

    # Run unregularized zero-initialized gradient descent
    w_gd_zero = torch.zeros(d, dtype=torch.float64)
    for _ in range(3000):
        grad = A.T @ (A @ w_gd_zero - y)
        w_gd_zero = w_gd_zero - eta * grad

    loss_zero = 0.5 * torch.norm(A @ w_gd_zero - y).item() ** 2
    dist_to_pinv = torch.norm(w_gd_zero - w_pinv).item()

    print("=" * 72)
    print("1. Overparameterized & Rank-Deficient Interpolation (N=10, d=40, rank=8):")
    print(f"   Zero-init GD final training loss:       {loss_zero:.2e}")
    print(f"   ||w_gd_zero - A^+ y||_2 discrepancy:    {dist_to_pinv:.2e}")
    print(f"   ||w_gd_zero||_2 = {torch.norm(w_gd_zero).item():.6f}  vs  ||A^+ y||_2 = {torch.norm(w_pinv).item():.6f}")

    # 2. Random nonzero-initialized GD: verify that the Null(A) component is permanently frozen
    w0 = torch.randn(d, dtype=torch.float64)
    P_row = V_sub @ V_sub.T
    w0_null = w0 - P_row @ w0  # Initial nullspace projection P_Null(w0)

    w_gd_rand = w0.clone()
    for _ in range(3000):
        grad = A.T @ (A @ w_gd_rand - y)
        w_gd_rand = w_gd_rand - eta * grad

    loss_rand = 0.5 * torch.norm(A @ w_gd_rand - y).item() ** 2
    w_theory_rand = w_pinv + w0_null
    dist_to_theory = torch.norm(w_gd_rand - w_theory_rand).item()

    print("\n2. Nonzero-init w0 Gradient Descent (Nullspace Freezing Theorem):")
    print(f"   Nonzero-init GD final training loss:    {loss_rand:.2e}")
    print(f"   ||w_gd_rand - (A^+ y + P_Null(w0))||_2 = {dist_to_theory:.2e}")
    print(f"   ||w_gd_rand||_2 = {torch.norm(w_gd_rand).item():.6f}  >  ||A^+ y||_2 = {torch.norm(w_pinv).item():.6f}")
    print("   Conclusion: Unregularized GD implicitly finds the least-squares solution closest to w0!")

    # 3. Inconsistent system check (with unreachable orthogonal noise in Null(A^T))
    noise = torch.randn(N, dtype=torch.float64)
    orthogonal_noise = noise - U_sub @ (U_sub.T @ noise)
    y_noisy = y + 0.5 * orthogonal_noise
    w_pinv_noisy = torch.linalg.pinv(A) @ y_noisy
    assert torch.allclose(A.T @ orthogonal_noise, torch.zeros_like(w_pinv), atol=1e-12)
    assert torch.allclose(w_pinv_noisy, w_pinv, atol=1e-12)
    w_gd_noisy = torch.zeros(d, dtype=torch.float64)
    for _ in range(3000):
        w_gd_noisy = w_gd_noisy - eta * (A.T @ (A @ w_gd_noisy - y_noisy))
    err_noisy = torch.norm(w_gd_noisy - w_pinv_noisy).item()
    print("\n3. Inconsistent System Check (with unreachable residual):")
    print(f"   ||w_gd_noisy - A^+ y_noisy||_2 = {err_noisy:.2e} (still locks onto minimum-norm LS solution!)")
    print("=" * 72)

if __name__ == "__main__":
    verify_implicit_bias_pseudoinverse()
