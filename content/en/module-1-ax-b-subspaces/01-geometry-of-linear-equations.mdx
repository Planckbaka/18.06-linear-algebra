The fundamental problem of linear algebra is to solve a system of $m$ linear equations in $n$ unknowns, written compactly as $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$. In high-school algebra, we view a system equation-by-equation. In his very first MIT 18.06 lecture, Prof. Gilbert Strang introduces the conceptual shift that powers all of modern linear algebra: **moving from the Row Picture (intersections of hyperplanes) to the Column Picture (linear combinations of column vectors)**.

---

## 1. Two Geometric Worlds in $\mathbb{R}^2$

Consider the $2\times 2$ linear system from Lecture 01:

$$
\begin{cases}
2x - y = 0 \\
-x + 2y = 3
\end{cases}
\quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad
\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}}_{A}
\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{x}}
=
\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{b}}
$$

### 1.1 The Row Picture: Intersection of Lines

Taking one row at a time gives two lines in the $xy$-plane $\mathbb{R}^2$:
- Row 1 ($2x - y = 0$) is a line through the origin with slope $2$.
- Row 2 ($-x + 2y = 3$) is a line passing through $(0, \frac{3}{2})$ and $(-3, 0)$.

Solving the system in the row picture means finding the unique point $(x, y) = (1, 2)$ lying on **both** lines simultaneously.

### 1.2 The Column Picture: Linear Combination of Columns

The column picture slices the system vertically, treating the unknowns $x$ and $y$ as scalar weights applied to the columns of $A$:

$$
x \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{a}_1}
+
y \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{a}_2}
=
\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{b}}
$$

Now the geometric question is dynamic: **What linear combination of column vectors $\mathbf{a}_1$ and $\mathbf{a}_2$ produces the target vector $\mathbf{b} = (0, 3)^T$?** Taking $1\mathbf{a}_1 + 2\mathbf{a}_2 = (2, -1)^T + (-2, 4)^T$ closes the parallelogram directly onto $(0, 3)^T$.

<DefinitionCard
  label="Definition 1.1"
  title="Matrix-Vector Multiplication by Columns & Column Space"
>
Let $A = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{a}_1 & \mathbf{a}_2 & \cdots & \mathbf{a}_n \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ with columns $\mathbf{a}_j \in \mathbb{R}^m$, and let $\mathbf{x} = (x_1, \dots, x_n)^T \in \mathbb{R}^n$.

1. **Product by Columns**: The matrix-vector product $A\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^m$ is defined as the **linear combination of the columns of $A$**:

$$
A\mathbf{x} := x_1 \mathbf{a}_1 + x_2 \mathbf{a}_2 + \cdots + x_n \mathbf{a}_n = \sum_{j=1}^{n} x_j \mathbf{a}_j
$$

2. **Column Space**: As $\mathbf{x}$ ranges over all of $\mathbb{R}^n$, the set of all reachable combinations forms the **column space** $\operatorname{Col}(A) \subseteq \mathbb{R}^m$:

$$
\operatorname{Col}(A) := \left\{ A\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^m : \mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n \right\} = \operatorname{span}\{\mathbf{a}_1, \dots, \mathbf{a}_n\}
$$
</DefinitionCard>

Consequently, **$A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ is solvable if and only if $\mathbf{b} \in \operatorname{Col}(A)$**.

---

## 2. Scaling to $\mathbb{R}^3$ and $\mathbb{R}^n$: Why Columns Win

In $\mathbb{R}^n$, a single linear equation defines an **$(n-1)$-dimensional hyperplane** (a line in $\mathbb{R}^2$, a plane in $\mathbb{R}^3$, an $8$-dimensional hyperplane in $\mathbb{R}^9$). Visualizing the intersection of nine $8$-dimensional hyperplanes is hopeless, whereas asking whether nine column vectors in $\mathbb{R}^9$ combine to reach $\mathbf{b}$ remains transparent in any dimension.

Even better, the column picture lets us solve special right-hand sides **by inspection**: for

$$
\begin{bmatrix}
2 & -1 & 0 \\
-1 & 2 & -1 \\
0 & -1 & 2
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ z \end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 2 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}
$$

since $\mathbf{b}$ equals the second column $\mathbf{a}_2$, we immediately read off $\mathbf{x} = (0, 1, 0)^T$ with zero elimination arithmetic!

## 3. Universal Solvability vs. Singular Collapse

Can $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ be solved for **every** $\mathbf{b} \in \mathbb{R}^n$? Only when the $n$ columns of square matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$ do not collapse into a lower-dimensional subspace.

  caption="Singular collapse: $\mathbf{a}_1 = (2, -1)^T$ and $\mathbf{a}_2 = (-4, 2)^T = -2\mathbf{a}_1$ are collinear, trapping $\operatorname{Col}(A)$ on a 1D line. Off-line $\mathbf{b} = (0, 3)^T$ has no solution; on-line $\tilde{\mathbf{b}} = (-2, 1)^T$ has infinitely many."
/>

<TheoremCard
  label="Theorem 1.1"
  title="Universal Solvability for Square Matrices"
>
For a **square** matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$, the following four statements are equivalent:
1. $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ has at least one solution for **every** $\mathbf{b} \in \mathbb{R}^n$;
2. The columns of $A$ span the entire space: $\operatorname{Col}(A) = \mathbb{R}^n$;
3. $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$ has only the trivial solution $\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$ (linearly independent columns);
4. $A$ is **nonsingular (invertible)**, and the solution to $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ is **unique** for every $\mathbf{b} \in \mathbb{R}^n$.
</TheoremCard>

<PitfallAlert title="Common Transcript Pitfalls & Rigor Checks">
1. **Pitfall 1 ("Singular means no solution")**: False! A singular system $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ has **no solution** when $\mathbf{b} \notin \operatorname{Col}(A)$, but **infinitely many solutions** whenever $\mathbf{b} \in \operatorname{Col}(A)$ (for instance, $\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{0}$ is always solvable).
2. **Pitfall 2 (Forgetting $A$ must be square in Theorem 1.1)**: If $A \in \mathbb{R}^{4\times 3}$ has $3$ independent columns, $\operatorname{Col}(A)$ is only a $3$-dimensional subspace inside $\mathbb{R}^4$—it cannot reach every $\mathbf{b} \in \mathbb{R}^4$.
</PitfallAlert>

$A$ is singular, yet every $\mathbf{x} = (1-t, t, s)^T$ ($t, s \in \mathbb{R}$) satisfies $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$.

Without performing Gaussian elimination, find: (1) a nonzero vector $\mathbf{x}_0$ with $A\mathbf{x}_0 = \mathbf{0}$, and (2) the exact condition on $\mathbf{b} = (b_1, b_2, b_3)^T$ for $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ to be solvable.

<DualSolution
  triggerLabel="Show Full Dual Solution"
  intuitionLabel="Geometric Intuition"
  rigorousLabel="Rigorous Proof"
>
<SolutionIntuition>

1. Adding the three columns gives $\mathbf{a}_1 + \mathbf{a}_2 + \mathbf{a}_3 = \mathbf{0}$, so $\mathbf{x}_0 = (1, 1, 1)^T$ immediately solves $A\mathbf{x}_0 = \mathbf{0}$.
2. Every column has entry sum $0$, so all three columns lie in the plane $b_1 + b_2 + b_3 = 0$. Since $\mathbf{a}_1$ and $\mathbf{a}_2$ are non-collinear, they span that entire plane!

</SolutionIntuition>
<SolutionRigorous>

Summing all three equations of $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ yields $0 = b_1 + b_2 + b_3$ (necessity). Conversely, whenever $b_3 = -b_1 - b_2$, choosing $\mathbf{x} = (b_1,\; b_1 + b_2,\; 0)^T$ gives $A\mathbf{x} = (b_1,\; b_2,\; -b_1-b_2)^T = \mathbf{b}$ (sufficiency).

</SolutionRigorous>
