If you memorize matrix multiplication $C = AB$ merely as the mechanical chant "row times column, sum the products," you miss more than half the insight in linear algebra. In MIT 18.06 Lecture 03, Professor Gilbert Strang reveals **five equivalent geometric and algebraic views of the exact same matrix product $AB$**—among which "combinations of columns," "combinations of rows," and "sums of outer products of rank at most one" immediately expose which subspaces the product matrix lives in. From there, using the nullspace and block elimination, we build the rigorous theory of the inverse matrix $A^{-1}$ for square matrices.

---

## 1. Five Golden Structural Views of Matrix Multiplication $C = AB$

Let $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ and $B \in \mathbb{R}^{s\times p}$. The product $AB$ is defined exactly when $s=n$, and in that case $C=AB\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times p}$. All five views below use $s=n$.

<MatrixMultFiveViewsDiagram caption="Five structural views of C = AB: (1) row-column dot product for single entries, (2) column combinations in Col(A), (3) row combinations in Row(B), (4) sum of outer products of rank at most one, and (5) block matrix multiplication." />

Below we unpack these five views layer by layer along with their structural corollaries:

### View ①: The Entrywise View (Inner Product of a Row and a Column)

If you only want to compute a single scalar entry $c_{ij}$ in row $i$ and column $j$ of $C$, simply take row $i$ of $A$ and column $j$ of $B$ and form their dot product (inner product):

$$
c_{ij} = (\text{row } i \text{ of } A) \cdot (\text{column } j \text{ of } B) = \sum_{k=1}^{n} a_{ik} b_{kj}
$$

This view is fine for computing isolated numbers by hand, but it obscures the overall subspace structure of the matrix product.

### View ②: The Column View (Columns of $C$ Are Linear Combinations of Columns of $A$)

Slice the right-hand matrix into its individual columns $B = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{b}_1 & \mathbf{b}_2 & \cdots & \mathbf{b}_p \end{bmatrix}$, where each column $\mathbf{b}_j \in \mathbb{R}^n$. Matrix multiplication acts by letting $A$ multiply each column of $B$ independently:

$$
AB = A \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{b}_1 & \mathbf{b}_2 & \cdots & \mathbf{b}_p \end{bmatrix}
= \begin{bmatrix} A\mathbf{b}_1 & A\mathbf{b}_2 & \cdots & A\mathbf{b}_p \end{bmatrix}
$$

Returning to the core definition from Lecture 01: $A\mathbf{b}_j$ is a linear combination of **the columns of $A$**, weighted by the entries of $\mathbf{b}_j$! This means **every single column of $C = AB$ lies without exception inside the column space $\operatorname{Col}(A)$ of $A$**.

### View ③: The Row View (Rows of $C$ Are Linear Combinations of Rows of $B$)

Symmetrically, slice the left-hand matrix into its rows $\mathbf{a}_1^T, \dots, \mathbf{a}_m^T \in \mathbb{R}^{1\times n}$:

$$
AB = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{a}_1^T \\ \mathbf{a}_2^T \\ \vdots \\ \mathbf{a}_m^T \end{bmatrix} B
= \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{a}_1^T B \\ \mathbf{a}_2^T B \\ \vdots \\ \mathbf{a}_m^T B \end{bmatrix}
$$

Every row $\mathbf{a}_i^T B = a_{i1}(\text{row } 1 \text{ of } B) + \cdots + a_{in}(\text{row } n \text{ of } B)$ is a **linear combination of the rows of $B$**!

### View ④: The Column-Times-Row View (Sum of Outer Products)

This is the view least familiar to beginners, yet the most powerful in advanced matrix factorizations ($LU$, spectral decomposition, and Singular Value Decomposition SVD)!

- "Row ($1\times n$) times column ($n\times 1$)" collapses into a single scalar (inner product);
- Conversely, **"column ($m\times 1$) times row ($1\times p$)" expands into a full-sized $m\times p$ matrix (outer product)!**

Denoting column $k$ of $A$ by $\mathbf{c}_k \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times 1}$ and row $k$ of $B$ by $\mathbf{r}_k^T \in \mathbb{R}^{1\times p}$, the matrix product is the exact **entrywise sum of $n$ outer-product slice matrices**:

$$
AB = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{c}_1 & \mathbf{c}_2 & \cdots & \mathbf{c}_n \end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{r}_1^T \\ \mathbf{r}_2^T \\ \vdots \\ \mathbf{r}_n^T \end{bmatrix}
= \sum_{k=1}^{n} \mathbf{c}_k \mathbf{r}_k^T
$$

See the magic in action with a concrete numerical example:

$$
\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 7 \\ 3 & 8 \\ 4 & 9 \end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 6 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}
=
\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 3 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 6 \end{bmatrix}}_{\text{1st rank-1 slice}}
+
\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 7 \\ 8 \\ 9 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}}_{\text{2nd all-zero slice}}
=
\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 12 \\ 3 & 18 \\ 4 & 24 \end{bmatrix}
$$

The first outer product has rank one. Its column space is the line spanned by $(2,3,4)^T$ and its row space is the line spanned by $(1,6)^T$, identifying rows with their transposes. An outer product $\mathbf{c}\mathbf{r}^T$ has rank one when both vectors are nonzero and rank zero when either is zero.

### View ⑤: Block Matrix Multiplication

As long as the column partitioning of $A$ matches the row partitioning of $B$ (so that all inner block dimensions align), submatrix blocks can be multiplied and added just like scalars following the row-times-column rule:

$$
\begin{bmatrix} A_{11} & A_{12} \\ A_{21} & A_{22} \end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix} B_{11} & B_{12} \\ B_{21} & B_{22} \end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
A_{11}B_{11} + A_{12}B_{21} & A_{11}B_{12} + A_{12}B_{22} \\
A_{21}B_{11} + A_{22}B_{21} & A_{21}B_{12} + A_{22}B_{22}
\end{bmatrix}
$$

**Crucial detail**: Because matrix multiplication is not commutative, when writing $A_{11}B_{11} + A_{12}B_{21}$, **the blocks of $A$ must stay strictly on the left and the blocks of $B$ strictly on the right**—never reverse the order!

<TheoremCard
  label="Theorem 3.1"
  title="Subspace Inclusion Theorem for Matrix Products"
>
For any conformable real matrices $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ and $B \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times p}$, the product $AB \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times p}$ always satisfies three fundamental subspace inclusions:

1. **Column space shrinks inside the left factor**: $\operatorname{Col}(AB) \subseteq \operatorname{Col}(A) \subseteq \mathbb{R}^m$;
2. **Row space shrinks inside the right factor** (writing $\operatorname{Row}(M) := \operatorname{Col}(M^T)$): $\operatorname{Row}(AB) \subseteq \operatorname{Row}(B) \subseteq \mathbb{R}^p$;
3. **Nullspace is inherited from the right factor**: $\operatorname{Null}(B) \subseteq \operatorname{Null}(AB)$, meaning if $B\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$, then necessarily $(AB)\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$.
</TheoremCard>

<ProofCollapse title="Rigorous Proof: Three-Line Subspace Inclusion Derivation">
1. Take any $\mathbf{y} \in \operatorname{Col}(AB)$. By the definition of column space, there exists $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^p$ such that $\mathbf{y} = (AB)\mathbf{x}$. By associativity of matrix multiplication, $\mathbf{y} = A(B\mathbf{x})$. Setting $\mathbf{w} = B\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ gives $\mathbf{y} = A\mathbf{w} \in \operatorname{Col}(A)$, so $\operatorname{Col}(AB) \subseteq \operatorname{Col}(A)$.
2. By View ③, every row of $AB$ is a linear combination of the rows of $B$. The same holds for their transposes, so $\operatorname{Row}(AB)\subseteq\operatorname{Row}(B)$.

3. If $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^p$ satisfies $B\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$, then $(AB)\mathbf{x} = A(B\mathbf{x}) = A\mathbf{0} = \mathbf{0}$.
</ProofCollapse>

## 2. Invertibility of Square Matrices and the Inverse Matrix

Now we focus our attention on **square matrices** $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$. If the matrix $A$ represents a linear operation (such as the elementary matrix $E_{21}$ in Lecture 02 that subtracts $3$ times row $1$ from row $2$), what kind of matrix can completely "undo" and reverse that operation?

<DefinitionCard
  label="Definition 3.1"
  title="Inverse of a Square Matrix"
>
Let $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$ be a **square matrix**. If there exists a square matrix $X \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$ of the same size satisfying both the left-inverse and right-inverse equations:

$$
XA = I_n \quad \text{and} \quad AX = I_n
$$

then $A$ is called an **invertible (or nonsingular) matrix**; the matrix $X$ satisfying this condition is **unique** (if $X_1 A = I_n$ and $AX_2 = I_n$, then $X_1 = X_1(AX_2) = (X_1 A)X_2 = X_2$) and is denoted by $A^{-1}$. If no such matrix exists, $A$ is called a **singular matrix**.
</DefinitionCard>

Why do some square matrices stubbornly refuse to have an inverse? Using **the column view and the nullspace**, you can see the essence in one second! Suppose there is a **nonzero vector** $\mathbf{x} \neq \mathbf{0}$ that $A$ crushes into the zero vector: $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$. If an inverse matrix $A^{-1}$ actually existed, multiplying both sides on the left by $A^{-1}$ would yield:

$$
A^{-1}(A\mathbf{x}) = A^{-1}\mathbf{0} \quad \Longrightarrow \quad (A^{-1}A)\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0} \quad \Longrightarrow \quad \mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}
$$

which directly contradicts $\mathbf{x} \neq \mathbf{0}$! Intuitively: **if $A$ flattens some nonzero vector $\mathbf{x}$ into $\mathbf{0}$, that information is permanently lost—no linear matrix in the universe can map the single vector $\mathbf{0}$ back to $\mathbf{0}$ and simultaneously back to the nonzero vector $\mathbf{x}$!**

<TheoremCard
  label="Theorem 3.2"
  title="One-Sided Inverse Equivalence & Inverse Rules for Square Matrices"
>
Let $A, B \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$ be $n\times n$ **square matrices**:

1. **One-sided inverses**: For $X,Y\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$, $XA=I_n$ implies $AX=I_n$, and $AY=I_n$ implies $YA=I_n$. Either identity gives the inverse of $A$.
2. **Nullspace criterion**: A square matrix $A$ is invertible $\iff$ the homogeneous equation $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$ has only the trivial solution $\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$ $\iff$ Gaussian elimination (with row exchanges allowed) produces a full set of $n$ nonzero pivots.
3. **Reverse order law for the inverse of a product**: If $A$ and $B$ are both invertible, then $AB$ is also invertible, and

$$
(AB)^{-1} = B^{-1} A^{-1}
$$

4. **Commutativity of transpose and inverse**: If $A$ is invertible, then $A^T$ is also invertible, and $(A^T)^{-1} = (A^{-1})^T$ (often written simply as $A^{-T}$).
</TheoremCard>

<ProofCollapse title="Rigorous Proof: Why a Left Inverse XA = I Automatically Satisfies AX = I for Square Matrices">
Suppose $X \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$ satisfies $XA = I_n$:
1. Consider $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$: multiplying on the left by $X$ gives $\mathbf{x} = I_n\mathbf{x} = X(A\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{0}$, so the $n$ columns of $A$ are linearly independent.
2. By Theorem 1.1, the columns of $A$ span all of $\mathbb{R}^n$. Thus for each standard basis vector $\mathbf{e}_j \in \mathbb{R}^n$ (column $j$ of $I_n$), the equation $A\mathbf{y}_j = \mathbf{e}_j$ has a solution $\mathbf{y}_j \in \mathbb{R}^n$.
3. Assemble these $n$ solution vectors as columns of a square matrix $Y = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{y}_1 & \cdots & \mathbf{y}_n \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$. By **View ② (the column view)** of matrix multiplication, we immediately obtain $AY = \begin{bmatrix} A\mathbf{y}_1 & \cdots & A\mathbf{y}_n \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{e}_1 & \cdots & \mathbf{e}_n \end{bmatrix} = I_n$!
4. Finally, link the left and right inverses via associativity: $X = X I_n = X(AY) = (XA)Y = I_n Y = Y$. Since $X = Y$, we automatically have $AX = AY = I_n$!
The nullspace criterion follows from Theorem 1.1 and the pivot test in Lecture 02.

A right inverse gives a left inverse by applying the result just proved to $Y^T A^T=I_n$ and transposing. Also, $(A^T)(A^{-1})^T=(A^{-1}A)^T=I_n$, which gives the transpose-inverse identity.
(We also verify the reverse order law: $(AB)(B^{-1}A^{-1}) = A(BB^{-1})A^{-1} = A I_n A^{-1} = I_n$, and left multiplication similarly yields $I_n$.)
</ProofCollapse>

## 3. Gauss-Jordan Elimination for Finding $A^{-1}$: $[A \mid I_n] \longrightarrow [I_n \mid A^{-1}]$

How do we systematically compute $A^{-1}$? Write the unknown inverse matrix column by column as $A^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{x}_1 & \mathbf{x}_2 & \cdots & \mathbf{x}_n \end{bmatrix}$. By **View ② (column-by-column multiplication)**, the matrix equation $AA^{-1} = I_n$ simply packs **$n$ linear systems that all share the exact same coefficient matrix $A$** side by side:

$$
A \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{x}_1 & \mathbf{x}_2 & \cdots & \mathbf{x}_n \end{bmatrix}
= \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{e}_1 & \mathbf{e}_2 & \cdots & \mathbf{e}_n \end{bmatrix} = I_n
\quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad
A\mathbf{x}_j = \mathbf{e}_j \;\; (j = 1, \dots, n)
$$

For invertible $A$, form the $n\times 2n$ augmented matrix $[A\mid I_n]$. Apply each row operation to both blocks. Allow row exchanges as needed, eliminate below and above the pivots in the coefficient block, and divide the pivot rows by their nonzero pivots to obtain $I_n$ on the left. A missing pivot in the coefficient block certifies that $A$ is singular.

Using View ⑤ (block multiplication), you can see at a glance why $A^{-1}$ magically emerges on the right-hand side: let $E \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$ be the combined product of all elementary row-operation matrices, and multiply the block-augmented matrix on the left by $E$:

$$
E \begin{bmatrix} A & \big| & I_n \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} EA & \big| & E I_n \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} EA & \big| & E \end{bmatrix}
$$

Once we reduce the left half to the identity matrix $EA = I_n$, Part 1 of Theorem 3.2 immediately forces **$E = A^{-1}$**! Thus the matrix $E$ sitting in the right half automatically becomes the desired inverse $A^{-1}$:

$$
\left[\begin{array}{cc|cc}
1 & 3 & 1 & 0 \\
2 & 7 & 0 & 1
\end{array}\right]
\xrightarrow{R_2 \leftarrow R_2 - 2R_1}
\left[\begin{array}{cc|cc}
1 & 3 & 1 & 0 \\
0 & 1 & -2 & 1
\end{array}\right]
\xrightarrow{R_1 \leftarrow R_1 - 3R_2}
\left[\begin{array}{cc|cc}
1 & 0 & 7 & -3 \\
0 & 1 & -2 & 1
\end{array}\right]
$$

We read off $A^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 7 & -3 \\ -2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$, and a quick mental check confirms $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 2 & 7 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 7 & -3 \\ -2 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$!

<PitfallAlert title="Common Pitfalls & Transcript Errata">
1. **Pitfall 1: Falsely applying "$XA = I \implies AX = I$" to rectangular matrices! (Extremely common trap)**
   The automatic symmetry between one-sided inverses in Theorem 3.2 **holds only for square matrices**! For rectangular matrices, take $X = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{1\times 2}$ and $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{2\times 1}$. Even though $XA = [1] = I_1$, reversing the order gives $AX = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \neq I_2$ (which has rank $1$ and is certainly not invertible)! Left and right one-sided inverses for rectangular matrices will be thoroughly dissected in Lecture 34.
2. **Pitfall 2: Blindly extrapolating "$AB = 0 \implies A = 0$ or $B = 0$" from scalar arithmetic**
   The product $AB$ is the zero matrix exactly when every column of $B$ lies in $\operatorname{Null}(A)$. For instance, $\begin{bmatrix}1&-1\\1&-1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1&2\\1&2\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\0&0\end{bmatrix}$. More generally, $AB=AC$ gives $A(B-C)=0$. Cancellation for all compatible $B,C$ is valid when $\operatorname{Null}(A)=\{\mathbf{0}\}$, which includes rectangular matrices with independent columns. If $\operatorname{Null}(A)$ contains a nonzero vector, using it as a column of $B-C$ yields unequal matrices with the same product.
3. **Pitfall 3: Writing $(AB)^{-1} = A^{-1}B^{-1}$ in the wrong order, or inventing $(A+B)^{-1} = A^{-1} + B^{-1}$**
   Remember Professor Strang's "shoes and socks rule": in the morning you put on your socks first (multiply by $B$) and then put on your shoes (multiply by $A$); at night, to undo this, you must **take off your shoes first (multiply by $A^{-1}$) and then take off your socks (multiply by $B^{-1}$)**, so $(AB)^{-1} = B^{-1}A^{-1}$. As for matrix addition, $(I + I)^{-1} = \frac{1}{2}I \neq I + I$—there is no simple formula splitting the inverse of a sum.
</PitfallAlert>

<DualSolution
  triggerLabel="Show Full Dual Solution (Intuition + Rigorous Proof)"
  intuitionLabel="Geometric Intuition"
  rigorousLabel="Rigorous Proof / Construction"
>
<SolutionIntuition>

Each of the two slices is an ultra-simple grid whose columns are multiples of a single vector and whose rows are multiples of a single vector. As for whether the $3\times 3$ matrix $C = AB$ is invertible, we don't need determinants or elimination at all—because all three columns of $C$ are linear combinations of **the mere two columns** of $A$ ($\operatorname{Col}(AB) \subseteq \operatorname{Col}(A)$). In 3D space, the subspace spanned by two vectors is at most a 2D plane, so the $3\times 3$ matrix $C$ **must be singular (non-invertible)**!

</SolutionIntuition>
<SolutionRigorous>

Expanding the column-times-row outer products via View ④:

$$
\mathbf{c}_1 \mathbf{r}_1^T =
\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 & -2 \end{bmatrix}
= \begin{bmatrix}
2 & 0 & -2 \\
4 & 0 & -4 \\
-2 & 0 & 2
\end{bmatrix}, \quad
\mathbf{c}_2 \mathbf{r}_2^T =
\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 & 0 \end{bmatrix}
= \begin{bmatrix}
0 & 0 & 0 \\
1 & 3 & 0 \\
3 & 9 & 0
\end{bmatrix}
$$

Adding them entrywise gives:

$$
C = AB = \mathbf{c}_1 \mathbf{r}_1^T + \mathbf{c}_2 \mathbf{r}_2^T =
\begin{bmatrix}
2 & 0 & -2 \\
5 & 3 & -4 \\
1 & 9 & 2
\end{bmatrix}
$$

**Singularity** follows from Theorem 3.1 since $\dim\operatorname{Col}(C)\le\dim\operatorname{Col}(A)\le2$. As a check, $\mathbf{x}_0=(3,-1,3)^T\neq\mathbf{0}$ satisfies $B\mathbf{x}_0=(6-6,3-3)^T=\mathbf{0}$, so $C\mathbf{x}_0=\mathbf{0}$ as well.

</SolutionRigorous>
</DualSolution>
</ExerciseCard>

<DualSolution
  triggerLabel="Show Full Dual Solution (Intuition + Rigorous Proof)"
  intuitionLabel="Geometric Intuition"
  rigorousLabel="Rigorous Proof / Construction"
>
<SolutionIntuition>

Matrix $A$ flattens every vector onto the $x$-axis (losing the $y$-direction, hence singular), while matrix $B$ flattens every vector onto the $y$-axis (losing the $x$-direction, hence singular). Yet when we add them together as $A + B$, their missing dimensions complement each other perfectly, restoring the fully invertible identity transformation $I$! (Conversely, adding two invertible matrices $I + (-I) = 0$ immediately produces a singular matrix.)

</SolutionIntuition>
<SolutionRigorous>

For any $n \ge 2$, construct the diagonal matrices:

$$
A = \operatorname{diag}(1, 0, \dots, 0) \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times n}, \quad
B = \operatorname{diag}(0, 1, \dots, 1) \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
$$

Since $A\mathbf{e}_2 = \mathbf{0}$ and $B\mathbf{e}_1 = \mathbf{0}$, both $A$ and $B$ have nonzero vectors in their nullspaces and are therefore singular; however, $A + B = I_n$ is clearly invertible (its inverse is $I_n$ itself).

</SolutionRigorous>
</DualSolution>

/>

<DualSolution
  triggerLabel="Show Full Dual Solution (Intuition + Rigorous Proof)"
  intuitionLabel="Geometric Intuition"
  rigorousLabel="Rigorous Proof / Construction"
>
<SolutionIntuition>

Think of $B: \mathbb{R}^3 \to \mathbb{R}^2$ as taking a 2D photograph of a 3D object: one direction of depth information is inevitably crushed to zero (there is a nonzero vector $\mathbf{z} \in \mathbb{R}^3$ with $B\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{0}$). Even if we follow it with $A: \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^3$ to lift the 2D photograph back into 3D space, the lost depth can never be recovered—$(AB)\mathbf{z} = A\mathbf{0} = \mathbf{0}$, so $AB$ is forever non-invertible!

</SolutionIntuition>
<SolutionRigorous>

We give a universal proof by contradiction holding for **all** $A \in \mathbb{R}^{3\times 2}$ and $B \in \mathbb{R}^{2\times 3}$:
Suppose $AB \in \mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}$ were invertible. By Theorem 1.1, we would have $\operatorname{Col}(AB) = \mathbb{R}^3$. However, Theorem 3.1 gives:

$$
\mathbb{R}^3 = \operatorname{Col}(AB) \subseteq \operatorname{Col}(A) = \operatorname{span}\{\mathbf{a}_1, \mathbf{a}_2\}
$$

This would mean just two 3D vectors $\mathbf{a}_1, \mathbf{a}_2$ could span all of $\mathbb{R}^3$—a contradiction (as shown in Lecture 01 Exercise `l01-ex3`, elimination on a $3\times 2$ matrix always leaves at least one zero row and a compatibility constraint, failing to cover $\mathbb{R}^3$). Hence $AB$ must be singular!

</SolutionRigorous>
</DualSolution>

>
Let $N \in \mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}$ be a strictly upper triangular matrix of the form:

$$
N = \begin{bmatrix}
0 & a & b \\
0 & 0 & c \\
0 & 0 & 0
\end{bmatrix} \quad (a, b, c \in \mathbb{R})
$$

1. Explain why the only possibly nonzero entry of $N^2$ is $(N^2)_{13}=ac$, and why $N^3$ is the zero matrix.
2. By analogy with the high-school geometric series identity $(1 - x)(1 + x + x^2) = 1 - x^3$, **without doing any Gauss-Jordan elimination**, derive in a single line the exact inverse $A^{-1}$ of the unit upper triangular matrix:

$$
A = I_3 - N = \begin{bmatrix}
1 & -a & -b \\
0 & 1 & -c \\
0 & 0 & 1
\end{bmatrix}
$$

<ProgressiveHint
  buttonPrefix="Unlock Hint"
  hints={[
    "Because the identity matrix $I_3$ commutes with $N$ ($I_3 N = N I_3$), expand the product $(I_3 - N)(I_3 + N + N^2)$ directly and watch the middle terms telescope!",
    "Since $N^3 = 0$, what does $(I_3 - N)(I_3 + N + N^2)$ equal? Then invoke Part 1 of Theorem 3.2 (a right inverse of a square matrix is its two-sided inverse)!"
  ]}
/>

<DualSolution
  triggerLabel="Show Full Dual Solution (Intuition + Rigorous Proof)"
  intuitionLabel="Geometric Intuition"
  rigorousLabel="Rigorous Proof / Construction"
>
<SolutionIntuition>

A nonzero summand in an entry of $N^k$ must follow $k$ strictly increasing index steps. For $N^2$ the only possible path is $1\to2\to3$, with weight $ac$. There is no path of length three. The finite geometric identity therefore gives the inverse directly.

</SolutionIntuition>
<SolutionRigorous>

1. Computing the matrix powers step by step:

$$
N^2 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & a & b \\ 0 & 0 & c \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix} 0 & a & b \\ 0 & 0 & c \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}
= \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & ac \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad
N^3 = N^2 N = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}
$$

2. By the distributive law of matrix multiplication and $I_3 N = N I_3$:

$$
(I_3 - N)(I_3 + N + N^2) = I_3 + N + N^2 - N - N^2 - N^3 = I_3 - N^3 = I_3
$$

By Part 1 of Theorem 3.2 (a right inverse of a square matrix is its unique two-sided inverse), we immediately obtain the exact closed-form expression:

$$
(I_3 - N)^{-1} = I_3 + N + N^2 =
\begin{bmatrix}
1 & a & b + ac \\
0 & 1 & c \\
0 & 0 & 1
\end{bmatrix}
$$

(Notice why the top-right entry is $b + ac$ rather than $+b$: the $ac$ term is the exact back-substitution compensation from the two-step indirect path $1 \to 2 \to 3$ superimposed on the direct path $1 \to 3$!)

</SolutionRigorous>
</DualSolution>

(Classic MIT 18.06 Midterm Problem) Let $M \in \mathbb{R}^{(m+n)\times(m+n)}$ be a square matrix with the following $2\times 2$ block upper triangular structure, where $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times m}$ and $D \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$ are **invertible square matrices**, $B \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ is an arbitrary rectangular matrix, and $0$ is the $n\times m$ zero block:

$$
M = \begin{bmatrix}
A & B \\
0 & D
\end{bmatrix}
$$

1. Use block multiplication to find a matrix $\begin{bmatrix}X&Y\\ Z&W\end{bmatrix}$ that satisfies $M\begin{bmatrix}X&Y\\ Z&W\end{bmatrix}=I_{m+n}$. Derive the four blocks and give $M^{-1}$.
2. When $m = n = 1$ with $A = [1]$ and $D = [1]$, verify that your formula reduces to the scalar elimination matrix inverse $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & b \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -b \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$.

<ProgressiveHint
  buttonPrefix="Unlock Hint"
  hints={[
    "Write out the block equation $\\begin{bmatrix} A & B \\\\ 0 & D \\end{bmatrix} \\begin{bmatrix} X & Y \\\\ Z & W \\end{bmatrix} = \\begin{bmatrix} I_m & 0 \\\\ 0 & I_n \\end{bmatrix}$ and look first at the two block equations from the second row: $DZ = 0$ and $DW = I_n$!",
    "After solving $Z = 0$ and $W = D^{-1}$, substitute $W$ into the top-right equation $AY + BW = 0$. When multiplying on the left by $A^{-1}$, watch carefully the left-to-right order of $A^{-1}$, $B$, and $D^{-1}$!"
  ]}
/>

<DualSolution
  triggerLabel="Show Full Dual Solution (Intuition + Rigorous Proof)"
  intuitionLabel="Geometric Intuition"
  rigorousLabel="Rigorous Proof / Construction"
>
<SolutionIntuition>

In block Gauss-Jordan elimination on $\begin{bmatrix} A & B & \mid & I_m & 0 \\ 0 & D & \mid & 0 & I_n \end{bmatrix}$:
- First normalize the bottom-right block $D$ to $I_n$ by **multiplying the second block row on the left by $D^{-1}$**;
- Next eliminate the top-right block $B$ upward using the second block row, subtracting $B$ times the new second row from the first row (which produces $-BD^{-1}$);
- Finally normalize the top-left block $A$ to $I_m$ by **multiplying the entire first block row on the left by $A^{-1}$**—so the top-right block is naturally sandwiched in the middle as **$-A^{-1} B D^{-1}$**! Not only do the dimensions $(m\times m)(m\times n)(n\times n) = m\times n$ fit together seamlessly, but $B$ never needs to be square!

</SolutionIntuition>
<SolutionRigorous>

1. Let $X \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times m}, Y \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n}, Z \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times m}, W \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$, and expand $M \begin{bmatrix} X & Y \\ Z & W \end{bmatrix} = I_{m+n}$ via View ⑤:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}
AX + BZ & AY + BW \\
DZ & DW
\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
I_m & 0 \\
0 & I_n
\end{bmatrix}
$$

Solve the four block matrix equations one by one (given that $A$ and $D$ are invertible):
- From $DZ = 0$, multiplying on the left by $D^{-1}$ gives $Z = 0$;
- From $DW = I_n$, multiplying on the left by $D^{-1}$ gives $W = D^{-1}$;
- Substituting $Z = 0$ into $AX + BZ = I_m$ gives $AX = I_m \implies X = A^{-1}$;
- Substituting $W = D^{-1}$ into $AY + BW = 0$ gives $AY = -B D^{-1}$, and **multiplying on the left** by $A^{-1}$ yields $Y = -A^{-1} B D^{-1}$.

By Part 1 of Theorem 3.2 (a right inverse of a square matrix is its unique two-sided inverse), the complete block inverse formula is:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}
A & B \\
0 & D
\end{bmatrix}^{-1}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
A^{-1} & -A^{-1} B D^{-1} \\
0 & D^{-1}
\end{bmatrix}
$$

2. When $A = [1], D = [1], B = [b]$, we get $-A^{-1}BD^{-1} = -1 \cdot b \cdot 1 = -b$, recovering $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -b \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ exactly.

</SolutionRigorous>
</DualSolution>

<FrontierAIReading
  id="AI-01"
  title="Scaled Dot-Product Attention, KV Cache, and FlashAttention"
  paperRef="Vaswani et al. (NeurIPS 2017) Attention Is All You Need · Dao et al. (NeurIPS 2022) FlashAttention"
>

In modern Large Language Models (LLMs) and multimodal Transformer architectures, the single most important operator is **Scaled Dot-Product Self-Attention**. Remarkably, from the semantic aggregation of vanilla attention to **KV Cache** acceleration during autoregressive inference, all the way to the hardware-aware memory revolution of **FlashAttention**, there is no arcane black magic underneath—**every single piece is a direct tensor application of the five views of matrix multiplication from Lecture 03!**

### 1. Dissecting $O = \operatorname{softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^T}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right) V$ via Lecture 03 Views ① and ③

Suppose an input sequence contains $L$ tokens, with single-head query/key feature dimension $d_k$ and value feature dimension $d_v$. Stack the $L$ token vectors as **rows** into three matrices:

$$
Q = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{q}_1^T \\ \vdots \\ \mathbf{q}_L^T \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{L\times d_k}, \quad
K = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{k}_1^T \\ \vdots \\ \mathbf{k}_L^T \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{L\times d_k}, \quad
V = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{v}_1^T \\ \vdots \\ \mathbf{v}_L^T \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{L\times d_v}
$$

The attention computation proceeds via two matrix multiplications:

1. **Step 1: Score Matrix $S = \frac{1}{\sqrt{d_k}} Q K^T \in \mathbb{R}^{L\times L}$ — View ① (Row-Column Inner Product)**:
   Notice that **column** $j$ of $K^T \in \mathbb{R}^{d_k \times L}$ is precisely the key vector $\mathbf{k}_j$ of token $j$! Therefore, by View ①, the $(i, j)$ entry of $S$ is the scaled dot product between query vector $i$ and key vector $j$:

$$
s_{ij} = \frac{\mathbf{q}_i^T \mathbf{k}_j}{\sqrt{d_k}}
$$

   Next, applying row-wise softmax independently to each row of $S$ yields the attention probability matrix $P \in \mathbb{R}^{L\times L}$ (satisfying $p_{ij} \ge 0$ and $\sum_{j=1}^L p_{ij} = 1$ for every row).

2. **Step 2: Context Aggregation $O = P V \in \mathbb{R}^{L\times d_v}$ — View ③ (Linear Combinations of Rows) & View ④ (Sum of Rank-1 Outer Products)**:
   Do not look at $PV$ entry by entry! Invoke **View ③ (the row-combination view)** directly: row $i$ of the output matrix $O$ is a **convex combination of the rows of $V$** weighted by row $i$ of $P$:

$$
\mathbf{o}_i = \sum_{j=1}^{L} p_{ij}\mathbf{v}_j \in \operatorname{Row}(V)\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d_v}
$$

   Each $\mathbf{o}_i$ lies in the convex hull of the individual value vectors $\{\mathbf{v}_1,\dots,\mathbf{v}_L\}$. Their linear span is $\operatorname{Row}(V)$, so Part 2 of Theorem 3.1 gives $\operatorname{Row}(PV)\subseteq\operatorname{Row}(V)$ for this single-head aggregation.
   The outer-product view gives $PV=\sum_{j=1}^L\mathbf{p}_{:,j}\mathbf{v}_j^T$, where $\mathbf{p}_{:,j}\in\mathbb{R}^L$ is column $j$ of $P$. Each term distributes value $j$ across the query positions and has rank at most one.

---

### 2. Understanding Why LLM Inference Needs a KV Cache at a Glance via View ⑤ (Block Matrix Multiplication)

In GPT-style causal autoregressive generation, suppose the first $t$ tokens have already been processed and their keys and values are cached. We now process token $t+1$ to predict token $t+2$. Each matrix naturally acquires one new row at the bottom, forming a **$2\times 1$ block structure**:

$$
Q_{t+1} = \begin{bmatrix} Q_t \\ \mathbf{q}_{t+1}^T \end{bmatrix}, \quad
K_{t+1} = \begin{bmatrix} K_t \\ \mathbf{k}_{t+1}^T \end{bmatrix} \implies K_{t+1}^T = \begin{bmatrix} K_t^T & \mathbf{k}_{t+1} \end{bmatrix}, \quad
V_{t+1} = \begin{bmatrix} V_t \\ \mathbf{v}_{t+1}^T \end{bmatrix}
$$

Substitute **View ⑤ (block multiplication)** directly into $Q_{t+1} K_{t+1}^T$ and apply the causal mask (setting future positions to $-\infty$ so their softmax probabilities become strictly $0$):

$$
Q_{t+1} K_{t+1}^T =
\begin{bmatrix} Q_t \\ \mathbf{q}_{t+1}^T \end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix} K_t^T & \mathbf{k}_{t+1} \end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
Q_t K_t^T & Q_t \mathbf{k}_{t+1} \\
\mathbf{q}_{t+1}^T K_t^T & \mathbf{q}_{t+1}^T \mathbf{k}_{t+1}
\end{bmatrix}
\xrightarrow{\text{scale, mask, row-wise softmax}}
P_{t+1} =
\begin{bmatrix}
P_t & \mathbf{0} \\
\tilde{\mathbf{p}}_{t+1, 1:t}^T & p_{t+1, t+1}
\end{bmatrix}
$$

Now multiply by the block-partitioned $V_{t+1}$ (the lower-triangular factor plays the same block-multiplication role as the upper-triangular matrix in Exercise `l03-ex5`!):

$$
O_{t+1} = P_{t+1} V_{t+1} =
\begin{bmatrix}
P_t & \mathbf{0} \\
\tilde{\mathbf{p}}_{t+1, 1:t}^T & p_{t+1, t+1}
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix} V_t \\ \mathbf{v}_{t+1}^T \end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
P_t V_t \\
\tilde{\mathbf{p}}_{t+1, 1:t}^T V_t + p_{t+1, t+1} \mathbf{v}_{t+1}^T
\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
O_t \\
\mathbf{o}_{t+1}^T
\end{bmatrix}
$$

Here $P_t$ uses the same scale and causal mask on the prefix. The upper-right zero block is a column in $\mathbb{R}^{t}$, and the bottom-left block is a row of length $t$.
1. The upper block of historical outputs $O_t = P_t V_t$ **remains completely unchanged** and never needs to be recomputed;
2. The new query $\mathbf{q}_{t+1}^T$ scores the cached keys and the new key, then combines the cached values and the new value. The per-head attention work for this token is $\mathcal{O}(t(d_k+d_v))$, compared with $\mathcal{O}(t^2(d_k+d_v))$ for recomputing attention on the whole prefix. The cache stores $\mathcal{O}(t(d_k+d_v))$ numbers per head.

---

### 3. Block Tiling and Online Softmax in FlashAttention

At $L=131072$, one dense $L\times L$ score matrix alone has $2^{34}$ entries (32 GiB at two bytes per entry). FlashAttention tiles the computation to reduce transfers between GPU high-bandwidth memory (HBM) and on-chip SRAM.

For a block of $B$ keys and values, the shapes are $B\times d_k$ and $B\times d_v$. For one query row, let $s_j$ denote the scaled scores after masking. For each block $J$ with a finite score, keep the running maximum $m$ of processed scores, the shifted sum $\ell$ and the shifted weighted value sum $\mathbf{u}$. Start from $m=-\infty$, $\ell=0$ and $\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{0}$. Set $m'=\max(m,\max_{j\in J}s_j)$ and let $\rho=e^{m-m'}$ (use $\rho=0$ on the first block). Update

$$
\ell'=\rho\ell+\sum_{j\in J}e^{s_j-m'},\qquad
\mathbf{u}'=\rho\mathbf{u}+\sum_{j\in J}e^{s_j-m'}\mathbf{v}_j
$$

and carry forward $(m',\ell',\mathbf{u}')$ to the next block. With at least one finite score, the output is $\mathbf{o}=\mathbf{u}/\ell$. The recurrence computes the same softmax attention in exact arithmetic without storing the full scores or probabilities in HBM. Floating-point results can differ by rounding, and the arithmetic for dense attention is still quadratic in $L$.

<CollapsibleCode title="PyTorch check of outer-product aggregation and the last causal attention row">

```python
import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F

torch.manual_seed(42)
L, d_k, d_v = 6, 4, 5
Q = torch.randn(L, d_k)
K = torch.randn(L, d_k)
V = torch.randn(L, d_v)

# 1. Standard causal self-attention O = P @ V
mask = torch.tril(torch.ones(L, L, dtype=torch.bool))
scores = (Q @ K.T) / (d_k ** 0.5)
P = F.softmax(scores.masked_fill(~mask, float('-inf')), dim=-1)  # (L, L)
O_standard = P @ V                                               # (L, d_v)

# 2. Lecture 03 View 4 check: sum of column-times-row outer products of rank at most 1 ∑ p_{:, j} v_j^T
O_rank1_sum = sum(P[:, j:j+1] @ V[j:j+1, :] for j in range(L))

# 3. Model a decode step: use prefix keys/values plus the new token, then query only the final row.
q_last = Q[-1:, :]                                             # (1, d_k)
K_cached = torch.cat((K[:-1], K[-1:]), dim=0)                   # (L, d_k)
V_cached = torch.cat((V[:-1], V[-1:]), dim=0)                   # (L, d_v)
p_last = F.softmax((q_last @ K_cached.T) / (d_k ** 0.5), dim=-1)  # (1, L)
o_last_cached = p_last @ V_cached                               # (1, d_v)

torch.testing.assert_close(O_standard, O_rank1_sum, rtol=0, atol=1e-6)
torch.testing.assert_close(O_standard[-1:, :], o_last_cached, rtol=0, atol=1e-6)
print("Both attention checks pass with absolute tolerance 1e-6.")
```
