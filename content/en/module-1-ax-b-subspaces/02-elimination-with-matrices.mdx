In Lecture 01, we learned how to understand the full geometric picture of $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ through linear combinations of column vectors. Yet in real-world scientific computing, how does a computer systematically solve for thousands or millions of unknowns? The answer remains the classic algorithm established by Gauss two centuries ago: **Gaussian Elimination**.

However, Lecture 02 of MIT 18.06 is far more than a review of high-school elimination arithmetic. Here, Professor Gilbert Strang carries out one of the most pivotal conceptual upgrades in modern linear algebra: **translating the verbal instruction "subtract a multiple of one row from another" into a clean, compact left-multiplication by an elementary matrix $E_{ij} A$**. This not only opens the door to inverse matrices and matrix factorizations, but also reveals for the first time the fundamental duality law: **left-multiplication acts on rows, while right-multiplication acts on columns**.

---

## 1. Gaussian Elimination and the Pivot Mechanism on a Concrete 3×3 System

Let us directly trace the $3\times 3$ linear system $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ that Professor Strang works out on the chalkboard in lecture:

$$
\begin{cases}
x + 2y + z = 2 \\
3x + 8y + z = 12 \\
\phantom{3x + {}} 4y + z = 2
\end{cases}
\quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad
\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}
1 & 2 & 1 \\
3 & 8 & 1 \\
0 & 4 & 1
\end{bmatrix}}_{A}
\begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ z \end{bmatrix}
=
\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}
2 \\ 12 \\ 2
\end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{b}}
$$

To update the right-hand side vector $\mathbf{b}$ simultaneously in the same table, we append $\mathbf{b}$ to the right of $A$ to form the $3\times 4$ **Augmented Matrix** $\begin{bmatrix} A \mid \mathbf{b} \end{bmatrix}$. The goal of elimination is crystal clear: annihilate the entries below the main diagonal column by column, reducing $A$ to an **Upper Triangular Matrix** $U$.

### 1.1 Two-Step Forward Elimination and Back Substitution

1. **Lock in the First Pivot**: The entry in the top-left $(1,1)$ position is $\boxed{1} \neq 0$; this is our first **Pivot**.
   - We want to eliminate the entry $3$ directly below it in the $(2,1)$ position. What multiple of Row 1 must we subtract from Row 2? This factor is called the **Multiplier**:

$$
\ell_{21} = \frac{a_{21}}{a_{11}} = \frac{3}{1} = 3
$$

   - Executing the row operation $R_2 \leftarrow R_2 - 3R_1$, Row 2 becomes $[3-3,\; 8-6,\; 1-3 \mid 12-6] = [0,\; 2,\; -2 \mid 6]$.
   - As for the $(3,1)$ position, it is already $0$ (corresponding to a multiplier $\ell_{31} = 0$), so we skip right past it!

2. **Lock in the Second Pivot**: Moving our eyes to the center $(2,2)$ position, after the update we just performed, we find the nonzero number $\boxed{2} \neq 0$, which becomes our second pivot!
   - To eliminate the entry $4$ directly below it at $(3,2)$, we compute the multiplier:

$$
\ell_{32} = \frac{4}{2} = 2
$$

   - Executing the row operation $R_3 \leftarrow R_3 - 2R_2$, Row 3 becomes $[0,\; 4-4,\; 1-(-4) \mid 2-12] = [0,\; 0,\; 5 \mid -10]$.

With that, forward elimination is complete! On the left, $A$ has been transformed into the staircase-shaped upper triangular matrix $U$, while on the right, $\mathbf{b}$ has been simultaneously transformed into a new vector $\mathbf{c}$:

$$
\left[\begin{array}{ccc|c}
\mathbf{1} & 2 & 1 & 2 \\
3 & 8 & 1 & 12 \\
0 & 4 & 1 & 2
\end{array}\right]
\xrightarrow{R_2 - 3R_1}
\left[\begin{array}{ccc|c}
\mathbf{1} & 2 & 1 & 2 \\
0 & \mathbf{2} & -2 & 6 \\
0 & 4 & 1 & 2
\end{array}\right]
\xrightarrow{R_3 - 2R_2}
\underbrace{\left[\begin{array}{ccc|c}
\mathbf{1} & 2 & 1 & 2 \\
0 & \mathbf{2} & -2 & 6 \\
0 & 0 & \mathbf{5} & -10
\end{array}\right]}_{[U \;\mid\; \mathbf{c}]}
$$

The three prominent nonzero numbers **$1, 2, 5$** along the main diagonal are all three pivots of matrix $A$. What makes the upper triangular system $U\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{c}$ so wonderfully friendly is that we can read off the unique solution immediately from bottom to top via **Back Substitution**:

$$
5z = -10 \implies z = -2 \;\longrightarrow\; 2y - 2(-2) = 6 \implies y = 1 \;\longrightarrow\; x + 2(1) + (-2) = 2 \implies x = 2
$$

### 1.2 When Does Elimination Break Down? (Temporary vs. Complete Failure)

The entire elimination process is governed by one ironclad rule: **Pivots can never be equal to $0$!** (Otherwise, the multiplier $\ell_{ij}$ would have a zero denominator, making it impossible to eliminate nonzero entries below.) If we hit a $0$ in the $k$-th pivot position, we must carefully distinguish between two radically different fates:

- **Temporary Failure (Rescued by a Row Exchange)**: A $0$ appears in the pivot position, **but there is still a nonzero entry somewhere below it in the same column**. For instance, if we swapped Row 1 and Row 3 in the original system above, the $(1,1)$ entry would be $0$, but the $(2,1)$ entry below it has $3 \neq 0$. A **row exchange** brings that nonzero entry into the pivot slot. This example remains nonsingular because the row exchange is invertible.
- **Missing pivot**: If the candidate and all entries below it in the active part of the column are zero, skip that column and continue elimination. A square coefficient matrix with a missing pivot is singular. For example, changing entry $(3,3)$ from $1$ to $-4$ gives the last equation $0z=-10$, so this right-hand side is inconsistent. In general, a real $n\times n$ matrix is invertible if and only if elimination with row exchanges produces $n$ pivots.

## 2. Rewriting Elimination in Matrix Language: Left-Multiplying by Elementary Matrices $E_{ij}$

Now we arrive at the centerpiece of this lecture: instead of writing verbal arrows like $\xrightarrow{R_2 - 3R_1}$, how do we construct a $3\times 3$ matrix $E_{21}$ so that multiplying $A$ by $E_{21}$ automatically executes "subtract $3$ times Row 1 from Row 2, while leaving the other rows unchanged"?

Here lies one of the most important keys to the entire course—the **Golden Duality Law of Row vs. Column Perspectives**:

- Lecture 01 taught us that **multiplying a matrix on the right by a column vector**, $A\mathbf{x}$, computes a **linear combination of the columns of $A$**;
- Completely symmetrically, **multiplying a matrix on the left by a row vector**, $\mathbf{r}^T A$, computes a **linear combination of the rows of $A$**!

Let us test this explicitly. Suppose we left-multiply a $3\times 3$ matrix $A$ by a row vector $\begin{bmatrix} c_1 & c_2 & c_3 \end{bmatrix}$:

$$
\begin{bmatrix} c_1 & c_2 & c_3 \end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
\text{— row } 1 \text{ of } A \text{ —} \\
\text{— row } 2 \text{ of } A \text{ —} \\
\text{— row } 3 \text{ of } A \text{ —}
\end{bmatrix}
= c_1 (\text{row } 1) + c_2 (\text{row } 2) + c_3 (\text{row } 3)
$$

Everything falls into place! To construct the left-multiplication operator $E_{21}$, we simply specify row by row how each row of the new matrix is formed as a weighted combination of the rows of the original matrix:

- $\text{New Row } 1 = 1\cdot(\text{row } 1) + 0\cdot(\text{row } 2) + 0\cdot(\text{row } 3) \implies$ Row 1 of $E_{21}$ is $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$;
- $\text{New Row } 2 = -3\cdot(\text{row } 1) + 1\cdot(\text{row } 2) + 0\cdot(\text{row } 3) \implies$ Row 2 of $E_{21}$ is $\begin{bmatrix} -3 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$;
- $\text{New Row } 3 = 0\cdot(\text{row } 1) + 0\cdot(\text{row } 2) + 1\cdot(\text{row } 3) \implies$ Row 3 of $E_{21}$ is $\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$!

In other words, **to find the elementary matrix for any row operation, simply perform that exact same row operation on the identity matrix $I$**! By the exact same logic, the second step—"subtract $2$ times Row 2 from Row 3"—corresponds to the elementary matrix $E_{32}$ with $-2$ in the $(3,2)$ entry:

$$
E_{21} = \begin{bmatrix}
1 & 0 & 0 \\
-3 & 1 & 0 \\
0 & 0 & 1
\end{bmatrix}, \qquad
E_{32} = \begin{bmatrix}
1 & 0 & 0 \\
0 & 1 & 0 \\
0 & -2 & 1
\end{bmatrix}
$$

<DefinitionCard
  label="Definition 2.1"
  title="Elementary Elimination Matrix & Permutation Matrix"
>
Let $I_n \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$ be the $n\times n$ identity matrix, and let $\mathbf{e}_i \in \mathbb{R}^n$ be the $i$-th standard basis column vector (with $1$ in the $i$-th component and $0$ elsewhere):

1. **Elementary Elimination Matrix**: For distinct $i,j\in\{1,\dots,n\}$ and a real scalar multiplier $\ell_{ij} \in \mathbb{R}$, the square matrix obtained by replacing the $(i, j)$ zero entry of $I_n$ with $-\ell_{ij}$ is denoted by $E_{ij}$:

$$
E_{ij} := I_n - \ell_{ij} \mathbf{e}_i \mathbf{e}_j^T \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
$$

   Left-multiplying any matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times m}$ by $E_{ij}$ is strictly equivalent to replacing Row $i$ of $A$ with $(\text{row } i) - \ell_{ij}(\text{row } j)$ while leaving the other $n-1$ rows unchanged.

2. **Row-Exchange Permutation Matrix**: The square matrix obtained by exchanging rows $i$ and $j$ (with the same index bounds) of the identity matrix $I_n$ is denoted by $P_{ij} \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$. Left-multiplying $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times m}$ by $P_{ij}$ exchanges Row $i$ and Row $j$ of $A$.
</DefinitionCard>

## 3. Associative Compression, Non-Commutativity, and Instant Inversion of Elementary Matrices

Our two steps give $E_{32}(E_{21}A)=U$. Matrix multiplication is associative for conformable factors, so $A(BC)=(AB)C$ whenever the inner dimensions match. Even when both $AB$ and $BA$ are defined, they can differ.

Because the associative law holds, we are free to shift the parentheses and multiply the individual elimination matrices together ahead of time, compressing the entire pipeline into a single combined elimination matrix $E$:

$$
E_{32} (E_{21} A) = (E_{32} E_{21}) A = U
\quad \Longrightarrow \quad
E = E_{32} E_{21} =
\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ -3 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
1 & 0 & 0 \\
-3 & 1 & 0 \\
\mathbf{6} & -2 & 1
\end{bmatrix}
$$

Look closely at the **$+6$** that pops up in the bottom-left $(3,1)$ corner of the product $E$! If we reversed the multiplication order to $E_{21} E_{32}$, that bottom-left entry would be $0$ (reversing the order of operations changes the outcome completely, proving $E_{32}E_{21} \neq E_{21}E_{32}$). Why does this annoying cross-term $+6$ appear here, and how does **inverting the elimination matrices** make this $+6$ vanish like magic? That is the central cliffhanger leading into the $A = LU$ factorization in Lecture 04!

Before we get there, let us see how to invert a single elementary elimination matrix $E_{21}$ at a glance: since the physical action of $E_{21}$ is "subtract $3$ times Row 1 from Row 2"—while Row 1 itself remains completely untouched—to undo this step and recover the original matrix, we simply "**add back** $3$ times Row 1 to Row 2"!

$$
\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}
1 & 0 & 0 \\
\mathbf{+3} & 1 & 0 \\
0 & 0 & 1
\end{bmatrix}}_{E_{21}^{-1}}
\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}
1 & 0 & 0 \\
\mathbf{-3} & 1 & 0 \\
0 & 0 & 1
\end{bmatrix}}_{E_{21}}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
1 & 0 & 0 \\
0 & 1 & 0 \\
0 & 0 & 1
\end{bmatrix}
= I_3
$$

We now distill the left/right multiplication duality, solution-set invariance, and elementary inverse formulas into a rigorous theorem:

<TheoremCard
  label="Theorem 2.1"
  title="Row vs. Column Action Duality & Inverse of Elementary Matrices"
>
Let $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$. For distinct $i,j \in \{1,\dots,m\}$, let $E_{ij}=I_m-\ell_{ij}\mathbf{e}_i\mathbf{e}_j^T$ with $\ell_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}$. For distinct $p,q\in\{1,\dots,m\}$, let $P_{pq}$ swap rows $p$ and $q$ of $I_m$. The tilded matrices are defined in the same way using $I_n$ and indices from $\{1,\dots,n\}$.

1. **Left-Multiplication Controls Rows vs. Right-Multiplication Controls Columns (Core Duality)**:
   - On the left, $E_{ij}A$ replaces row $i$ by row $i$ minus $\ell_{ij}$ times row $j$, and $P_{pq}A$ swaps rows $p$ and $q$.
   - On the right, $A\tilde E_{rs}=A-\ell_{rs}(A\mathbf{e}_r)\mathbf{e}_s^T$ replaces column $s$ by column $s$ minus $\ell_{rs}$ times column $r$. Right-multiplication by $\tilde P_{rs}$ exchanges columns $r$ and $s$.
2. **Exact Inverses of Single-Step Elimination and Permutation Matrices**: For any $i \neq j$ and any real scalar $\ell_{ij} \in \mathbb{R}$, both $E_{ij}$ and $P_{pq}$ are always invertible, with:

$$
E_{ij}^{-1} = \left(I_m - \ell_{ij}\mathbf{e}_i\mathbf{e}_j^T\right)^{-1} = I_m + \ell_{ij}\mathbf{e}_i\mathbf{e}_j^T, \qquad P_{pq}^{-1} = P_{pq}
$$

3. **Invariance of the Linear System Solution Set**: If $E \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times m}$ is the product of any finite sequence of elementary elimination and row-exchange matrices, then $E$ is invertible, and for any $\mathbf{b} \in \mathbb{R}^m$, the original system and the eliminated system share the **exact same solution set**:

$$
A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b} \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad (EA)\mathbf{x} = E\mathbf{b}
$$
</TheoremCard>

<ProofCollapse title="Rigorous Proof: Rank-One Outer Product Orthogonality & Equivalence of Solution Sets">
1. **Proof that $E_{ij}^{-1} = I_m + \ell_{ij}\mathbf{e}_i\mathbf{e}_j^T$**: Using the distributive and associative laws of matrix multiplication, we expand the product directly:

$$
(I_m + \ell_{ij}\mathbf{e}_i\mathbf{e}_j^T)(I_m - \ell_{ij}\mathbf{e}_i\mathbf{e}_j^T)
= I_m + \ell_{ij}\mathbf{e}_i\mathbf{e}_j^T - \ell_{ij}\mathbf{e}_i\mathbf{e}_j^T - \ell_{ij}^2 \mathbf{e}_i (\underbrace{\mathbf{e}_j^T \mathbf{e}_i}_{= 0}) \mathbf{e}_j^T = I_m
$$

   Notice how the crucial assumption **$i \neq j$** guarantees that the inner product $\mathbf{e}_j^T \mathbf{e}_i = 0$, causing the quadratic term to vanish identically! Multiplying in the reverse order yields $I_m$ by the exact same calculation. As for the row-exchange matrix $P_{pq}$, swapping the same pair of rows twice clearly restores the identity matrix, so $P_{pq}^2 = I_m \implies P_{pq}^{-1} = P_{pq}$.
2. **Proof of Solution Set Equivalence $(\iff)$**:
   - $(\implies)$ If $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$, left-multiplying both sides by $E$ immediately gives $E(A\mathbf{x}) = (EA)\mathbf{x} = E\mathbf{b}$;
   - $(\impliedby)$ Since each elementary factor is invertible, $E$ is invertible (its inverse is the reverse-order product of the inverses of its factors). Left-multiplying both sides of $(EA)\mathbf{x} = E\mathbf{b}$ by $E^{-1}$ recovers $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$. Because this implication is strictly bidirectional ($\iff$), no extraneous solutions are ever introduced and no solutions are ever lost!
</ProofCollapse>

<PitfallAlert title="Common Pitfalls & Transcript Errata">
1. **Misconception 1: "Gaussian elimination (elementary row operations) preserves the column space $\operatorname{Col}(A)$" (Public Enemy #1!)**
   For $A=\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{bmatrix}$, subtracting the first row from the second gives $U=\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\0&0\end{bmatrix}$. Their column spaces are respectively the lines spanned by $(1,1)^T$ and $(1,0)^T$. In general an invertible row-operation matrix $E$ maps $\operatorname{Col}(A)$ onto $\operatorname{Col}(EA)$ and preserves its dimension. It preserves $\operatorname{Row}(A)=\operatorname{Col}(A^T)$ because the rows of $EA$ are combinations of those of $A$, and $A=E^{-1}(EA)$ gives the reverse inclusion. Taking $\mathbf{b}=\mathbf{0}$ in Theorem 2.1 gives preservation of $\operatorname{Null}(A)=N(A)=\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^n:A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}\}$. For a general right-hand side, use $E\mathbf{b}$ with $EA$ to preserve the solutions.
2. **Misconception 2 — writing elementary factors in execution order**. The factors act **from right to left**, starting closest to $A$! Performing Step 1 ($E_{21}$) first and Step 2 ($E_{32}$) second must be written as $E_{32} E_{21} A$ (the closer a matrix sits to $A$, the earlier it acts). Reversing the order produces a completely wrong product.
3. **Misconception 3: Declaring a matrix "singular and non-invertible" the instant a $0$ appears on the main diagonal**
   Consider the permutation matrix $P_{12} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$: its main diagonal consists entirely of $0$s, yet it is perfectly invertible ($P_{12}^{-1} = P_{12}$)! A square matrix is singular exactly when elimination with row exchanges yields fewer pivots than columns. At a given step, a zero candidate forces you to skip the column only if all entries below it in that column are also zero.
</PitfallAlert>

>
<SolutionIntuition>

1. **Left-multiply by reading rows**: Row 2 of $E_{21}$ is $[-4, 1, 0]$, so $E_{21}A$ simply replaces Row 2 of $A$ with $(\text{row } 2) - 4(\text{row } 1) = [0, -3, -6]$, leaving Rows 1 and 3 completely untouched!
   **Right-multiply by reading columns**: Column 1 of $E_{21}$ is $(1, -4, 0)^T$, so $AE_{21}$ simply replaces Column 1 of $A$ with $(\text{col } 1) - 4(\text{col } 2) = (-7, -16, -25)^T$, leaving Columns 2 and 3 completely untouched! The left and right products could not be more different!
2. Notice that the three rows of $A$ form an arithmetic progression: $R_1 + R_3 = 2R_2$. Once we eliminate down Column 1 and then Column 2, Row 3 inevitably collapses into $[0, 0, 0]$. The 3rd pivot vanishes completely, confirming that $A$ is a classic singular matrix!

</SolutionIntuition>
<SolutionRigorous>

1. By Part 1 of Theorem 2.1, we write down the left and right products directly:

$$
E_{21} A = \begin{bmatrix}
1 & 2 & 3 \\
0 & -3 & -6 \\
7 & 8 & 9
\end{bmatrix}, \qquad
A E_{21} = \begin{bmatrix}
1 - 4(2) & 2 & 3 \\
4 - 4(5) & 5 & 6 \\
7 - 4(8) & 8 & 9
\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}
-7 & 2 & 3 \\
-16 & 5 & 6 \\
-25 & 8 & 9
\end{bmatrix}
$$

2. Continuing elimination on $E_{21} A$: first left-multiply by $E_{31}$ ($R_3 \leftarrow R_3 - 7R_1$) so Row 3 becomes $[0, -6, -12]$; then left-multiply by $E_{32}$ ($R_3 \leftarrow R_3 - 2R_2$) to obtain:

$$
U = \begin{bmatrix}
1 & 2 & 3 \\
0 & -3 & -6 \\
0 & 0 & 0
\end{bmatrix}
$$

Because the $(3,3)$ pivot slot is $0$ and there are no rows below it to exchange, elimination produces only $2 \lt 3$ pivots. Therefore, $A$ is strictly singular (indeed, $A(1, -2, 1)^T = \mathbf{0}$).

</SolutionRigorous>
</DualSolution>

The shear $(x,y)^T\mapsto (x,y-3x)^T$ maps the column-space line $y=3x$ onto the $x$-axis. Applying the same invertible shear to the right-hand side gives $E\mathbf{b}$ and preserves which coefficient vectors solve the system.

For a $2\times 2$ counterexample, take

$$
A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 6 \end{bmatrix}, \quad
E = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ -3 & 1 \end{bmatrix}
\quad \Longrightarrow \quad
U = EA = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}
$$

Then:
- $\operatorname{Col}(A) = \operatorname{span}\{(1, 3)^T\} = \{(t, 3t)^T : t \in \mathbb{R}\}$;
- $\operatorname{Col}(U) = \operatorname{span}\{(1, 0)^T\} = \{(t, 0)^T : t \in \mathbb{R}\}$.

The vector $(1,3)^T$ lies in $\operatorname{Col}(A)$ and outside $\operatorname{Col}(U)$. In general, the spaces are equal exactly when $E$ maps $\operatorname{Col}(A)$ onto itself. This holds for $\operatorname{Col}(A)=\mathbb{R}^m$, and can also hold for a proper subspace. For example, the same $E$ fixes the $y$-axis pointwise.

The order in which we write down a system of equations is purely a human convention! Hitting a $0$ in the top-left corner is just a polite reminder to swap in a new leading row—never a death sentence for invertibility.

<SolutionRigorous>

We construct a $3\times 3$ counterexample using the permutation matrix:

$$
A = \begin{bmatrix}
0 & 1 & 0 \\
1 & 0 & 0 \\
0 & 0 & 1
\end{bmatrix}
$$

Although $a_{11} = 0$, left-multiplying by the row-exchange elementary matrix $P_{12} = A$ immediately reduces $A$ to the identity matrix $U = P_{12} A = I_3$, which possesses a full set of $3$ nonzero pivots $1, 1, 1$. For any $\mathbf{b} = (b_1, b_2, b_3)^T \in \mathbb{R}^3$, there is a unique solution $\mathbf{x} = (b_2, b_1, b_3)^T$, so $A$ is completely invertible.

<ExerciseCard

  tierLabel="L3 · Zero-Calculation Subspace Reasoning"
  title="Parameter Sensitivity Reasoning: When Do Pivots Vanish, and When Can Row Exchanges Save the Day?"
>
(Adapted from a classic MIT 18.06 lecture parameter problem) Consider two $3\times 3$ matrices depending on real parameters $a, b \in \mathbb{R}$:

$$
M_1(a) = \begin{bmatrix}
a & 2 & 3 \\
a & a & 4 \\
a & a & a
\end{bmatrix}, \qquad
M_2(b) = \begin{bmatrix}
1 & b & 0 \\
b & 1 & b \\
0 & b & 1
\end{bmatrix}
$$

1. Use row subtractions to put $M_1(a)$ in upper triangular form. Find its diagonal entries, determine when all three are pivots, and identify all real $a$ for which $M_1(a)$ is singular.
2. For the symmetric tridiagonal matrix $M_2(b)$, find the condition on $b$ under which elimination (allowing row exchanges) produces a full set of $3$ nonzero pivots (nonsingular), and identify whether there is any value of $b$ that triggers a **temporary failure** where the 2nd pivot slot becomes $0$ **but can be rescued by exchanging with Row 3**!

<ProgressiveHint
  buttonPrefix="Unlock Hint"
  hints={[
    "For $M_1(a)$, subtract the original row 2 from row 3 first, then subtract row 1 from row 2. In that order the two operations produce an upper triangular matrix.",
    "For $M_2(b)$: After Step 1 ($R_2 \\leftarrow R_2 - b R_1$), the $(2,2)$ pivot slot becomes $1 - b^2$! If $1 - b^2 = 0$ (i.e., $b = \\pm 1$), check what number is sitting in the $(3,2)$ slot of Row 3 and whether swapping rows rescues the pivot!"
  ]}
/>

<DualSolution
  triggerLabel="Show Full Dual Solution (Intuition + Rigorous Proof)"
  intuitionLabel="Geometric Intuition"
  rigorousLabel="Rigorous Proof / Construction"
>
<SolutionIntuition>

1. Subtracting the original row 2 from row 3 leaves $[0,0,a-4]$. Subtracting row 1 from row 2 then leaves $[0,a-2,1]$. The resulting upper triangular matrix has diagonal $a,a-2,a-4$. It is singular exactly when one of these entries is zero.
2. $M_2(b)$ is even more dramatic: after the first elimination step, the $(2,2)$ slot becomes $1 - b^2$. When $b = \pm 1$, the 2nd pivot slot drops to $0$, but Row 3 is $[0,\; \pm 1,\; 1]$, whose $(3,2)$ entry is nonzero! Swapping Rows 2 and 3 brings it back from the brink and secures all three nonzero pivots! What actually kills $M_2(b)$ permanently is $1 - 2b^2 = 0$, namely $b = \pm \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$!

</SolutionIntuition>
<SolutionRigorous>

1. **Analysis of $M_1(a)$**. For all real $a$, apply $R_2 \leftarrow R_2-R_1$ and $R_3 \leftarrow R_3-R_1$, then $R_3 \leftarrow R_3-R_2$. These row subtractions require no division.

$$
\begin{bmatrix}
a & 2 & 3 \\
a & a & 4 \\
a & a & a
\end{bmatrix}
\longrightarrow
\begin{bmatrix}
a & 2 & 3 \\
0 & a - 2 & 1 \\
0 & a - 2 & a - 3
\end{bmatrix}
\longrightarrow
\begin{bmatrix}
a & 2 & 3 \\
0 & a - 2 & 1 \\
0 & 0 & a - 4
\end{bmatrix}
$$

   - If $a \notin \{0, 2, 4\}$, the three pivots are $a,\; a-2,\; a-4$ (all nonzero);
   - The row subtractions preserve invertibility. The resulting triangular matrix is invertible exactly when all its diagonal entries are nonzero, so $M_1(a)$ is singular $\iff a\in\{0,2,4\}$.

2. **Analysis of $M_2(b)$**: The 1st pivot is $1$. Executing $R_2 \leftarrow R_2 - bR_1$ gives:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}
1 & b & 0 \\
0 & 1 - b^2 & b \\
0 & b & 1
\end{bmatrix}
$$

   - **Case A ($b = \pm 1$, temporary failure rescued by row exchange!)**: Here $1 - b^2 = 0$, so the intermediate matrix is $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & \pm 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & \pm 1 \\ 0 & \pm 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$. Exchanging Rows 2 and 3 immediately yields the upper triangular matrix $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & \pm 1 & 0 \\ 0 & \pm 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & \pm 1 \end{bmatrix}$ with three nonzero pivots $1, \pm 1, \pm 1$. Hence the matrix is **nonsingular**!
   - **Case B ($b \neq \pm 1$, continuing elimination without row exchange)**: Using the multiplier $\ell_{32} = \frac{b}{1-b^2}$, we execute $R_3 \leftarrow R_3 - \frac{b}{1-b^2} R_2$, and the bottom-right 3rd pivot slot becomes:

$$
d_3 = 1 - \frac{b^2}{1 - b^2} = \frac{1 - 2b^2}{1 - b^2}
$$

   Complete failure ($d_3 = 0$) occurs if and only if $1 - 2b^2 = 0 \iff b = \pm \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$. For all $b \neq \pm \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$, $M_2(b)$ is nonsingular!

</SolutionRigorous>
</DualSolution>

  title="MIT 18.06 Synthesis Challenge: One-Shot Construction and Reverse-Order Inversion of a Multi-Step Elimination Operator"
>
Suppose we want to find a single $3\times 3$ matrix $E$ such that left-multiplying **any** matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{3\times n}$ by $E$ executes the following three row operations in strict sequential order:
1. First, exchange Row 1 and Row 2;
2. Next, subtract $5$ times the (new) Row 1 from the (new) Row 3;
3. Finally, add $2$ times the (new) Row 3 to Row 2.

Complete the following two parts:
1. Write down the elementary matrices $M_1, M_2, M_3$ corresponding to each individual step, assemble the combined matrix $E$ in the proper left-to-right order, and verify that $E$ is strictly equal to the matrix obtained by applying those three row operations sequentially to the identity matrix $I_3$;
2. **Without performing augmented-matrix elimination**, use only the single-step elementary inverse formulas and the "shoes-and-socks reverse-order law" for inverses to write $E^{-1}$ as a product of elementary matrices and compute the explicit numerical matrix $E^{-1}$!

<ProgressiveHint
  buttonPrefix="Unlock Hint"
  hints={[
    "Step 1 acts on $A$ first, so $M_1$ must sit immediately to the left of $A$: $E A = M_3 M_2 M_1 A$! Here $M_1 = P_{12}$, $M_2$ has $-5$ in the $(3,1)$ entry, and $M_3$ has $+2$ in the $(2,3)$ entry.",
    "Notice that $E = E I_3 = M_3(M_2(M_1 I_3))$, which algebraically proves in one line that applying the three operations sequentially to $I_3$ must produce the exact same matrix $E$!",
    "When taking the inverse, remember to reverse the order: $E^{-1} = (M_3 M_2 M_1)^{-1} = M_1^{-1} M_2^{-1} M_3^{-1}$! This means you can undo the operations on $I_3$ in reverse order (first subtract 2 times Row 3 from Row 2, then add 5 times Row 1 to Row 3, and finally swap Rows 1 and 2)!"
  ]}
/>

<DualSolution
  triggerLabel="Show Full Dual Solution (Intuition + Rigorous Proof)"
  intuitionLabel="Geometric Intuition"
  rigorousLabel="Rigorous Proof / Construction"
>
<SolutionIntuition>

This problem fuses the core ideas of Lectures 02 and 03 into one cohesive picture!
- To construct $E$ in the forward direction, you don't actually need to multiply three matrices together: by associativity, $E = (M_3 M_2 M_1) I_3 = M_3(M_2(M_1 I_3))$. Just take the identity matrix $I_3$ and perform the three mental steps in order (swap Rows 1 & 2 $\to$ Row 3 minus 5 times Row 1 $\to$ Row 2 plus 2 times Row 3) to write down $E$ in ten seconds flat!
- Finding $E^{-1}$ in the reverse direction also takes ten seconds: inverting a sequence of operations is simply **playing the videotape backward**! Starting from $I_3$, first undo Step 3 (Row 2 minus 2 times Row 3), then undo Step 2 (Row 3 plus 5 times Row 1), and finally undo Step 1 (swap Rows 1 & 2 back)—saving yourself every last matrix multiplication!

</SolutionIntuition>
<SolutionRigorous>

1. **Constructing the Single-Step Elementary Matrices and the Combined Operator $E$**:

$$
M_1 = P_{12} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad
M_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ -5 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad
M_3 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}
$$

Multiplying from right to left in the order of execution gives $E = M_3 M_2 M_1$. Using $E = M_3(M_2(M_1 I_3))$ to act step by step on $I_3$:

$$
I_3 \xrightarrow{M_1}
\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}
\xrightarrow{M_2}
\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & -5 & 1 \end{bmatrix}
\xrightarrow{M_3}
E = \begin{bmatrix}
0 & 1 & 0 \\
1 & -10 & 2 \\
0 & -5 & 1
\end{bmatrix}
$$

2. **Reverse-Order Inversion $E^{-1} = M_1^{-1} M_2^{-1} M_3^{-1}$**:
By Part 2 of Theorem 2.1, $M_1^{-1} = P_{12}$, $M_2^{-1}$ flips $-5$ to $+5$, and $M_3^{-1}$ flips $+2$ to $-2$:

$$
E^{-1} =
\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{M_1^{-1}}
\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 5 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{M_2^{-1}}
\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & -2 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{M_3^{-1}}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
0 & 1 & -2 \\
1 & 0 & 0 \\
5 & 0 & 1
\end{bmatrix}
$$

We can also verify this by applying the inverse steps sequentially to $I_3$:

$$
I_3 \xrightarrow{M_3^{-1}}
\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & -2 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}
\xrightarrow{M_2^{-1}}
\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & -2 \\ 5 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}
\xrightarrow{M_1^{-1}}
E^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix}
0 & 1 & -2 \\
1 & 0 & 0 \\
5 & 0 & 1
\end{bmatrix}
$$

Direct verification of $E E^{-1}$:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}
0 & 1 & 0 \\
1 & -10 & 2 \\
0 & -5 & 1
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
0 & 1 & -2 \\
1 & 0 & 0 \\
5 & 0 & 1
\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
1 & 0 & 0 \\
0 - 10 + 10 & 1 & -2 + 0 + 2 \\
-5 + 5 & 0 & 1
\end{bmatrix}
= I_3
$$

Everything matches!
