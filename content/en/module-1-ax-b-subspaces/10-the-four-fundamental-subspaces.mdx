If only a single diagram could hang on the wall for all of MIT 18.06, Prof. Gilbert Strang would without hesitation hang the diagram born in today's lecture: **"The Big Picture of Linear Algebra" — the Four Fundamental Subspaces**.

In previous lectures, we became familiar with the column space $\operatorname{Col}(A)$ (Strang's $C(A)$) and the nullspace $\operatorname{Null}(A)$ (Strang's $N(A)$). Today in Lecture 10, we unite a matrix $A$ with its transpose $A^T$ to illuminate all **four fundamental subspaces** governed by any real matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$. Together, we will prove the conservation of dimension law that Strang calls the **"Fundamental Theorem of Linear Algebra, Part I,"** and walk step-by-step through the exact procedure for extracting bases for all four subspaces at once!

---

## 1. Meet the Four Stars: Where Do They Live, and What Are Their Dimensions?

Given an arbitrary rectangular matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ ($m$ rows, $n$ columns, with $r = \operatorname{rank}(A)$ pivots from elimination), it naturally bridges two Euclidean worlds: the **input space $\mathbb{R}^n$** (where rows have length $n$) and the **output space $\mathbb{R}^m$** (where columns have length $m$). Living inside each of these two worlds is an inseparable pair of subspaces:

### 1.1 The Pair Living in the Input Space $\mathbb{R}^n$ (Dimensions Sum to $r + (n - r) = n$)

1. **The Row Space $\operatorname{Row}(A) := \operatorname{Col}(A^T) \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$**:
   Spanned by all linear combinations of the rows of $A$. Because we prefer to write all vectors consistently as vertical **column vectors**, standing each row of $A$ upright produces precisely the columns of the transpose matrix $A^T \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times m}$! Thus, the standard algebraic notation for the row space is **$\operatorname{Col}(A^T)$**. What is its dimension? Remarkably, it matches the dimension of the column space **to the exact number: $r$**!
2. **The Nullspace $\operatorname{Null}(A) \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$**:
   The space of all solution vectors $\mathbf{x}$ satisfying $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$. As proved in Lecture 09, its dimension equals the number of free variables: **$n - r$**.

### 1.2 The Pair Living in the Output Space $\mathbb{R}^m$ (Dimensions Sum to $r + (m - r) = m$)

3. **The Column Space $\operatorname{Col}(A) \subseteq \mathbb{R}^m$**:
   Spanned by all linear combinations of the columns of $A$. As proved in Lecture 09, its dimension is **$r$**.
4. **The Left Nullspace $\operatorname{Null}(A^T) \subseteq \mathbb{R}^m$**:
   The nullspace of the transpose matrix $A^T$ — that is, all vectors $\mathbf{y} \in \mathbb{R}^m$ satisfying $A^T \mathbf{y} = \mathbf{0}$!
   - **Why on earth do we call it the "left" nullspace?** Take the equation $A^T\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{0}$ and transpose both sides. By the reverse-order law of transposes, it immediately becomes:

$$
(A^T \mathbf{y})^T = \mathbf{0}^T \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad \mathbf{y}^T A = \mathbf{0}^T
$$

   Mystery solved! The ordinary nullspace multiplies $A$ on the **right** by a column vector $\mathbf{x}$ to get zero ($A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$, searching for weights that crush the **columns** into the zero vector). The left nullspace multiplies $A$ on the **left** by a row vector $\mathbf{y}^T$ to get zero ($\mathbf{y}^T A = \mathbf{0}^T$, searching for weights that **combine the rows of $A$ into a zero row**)! Since $A^T \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times m}$ has $m$ columns and the exact same rank $r$, the number of free variables for $A^T$ is naturally **$m - r$**!

Over $\mathbb{R}$, $A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}$ means that $\mathbf{x}$ is orthogonal to every row of $A$, so $\operatorname{Null}(A)=\operatorname{Row}(A)^\perp$. Applying the same argument to $A^T$ gives $\operatorname{Null}(A^T)=\operatorname{Col}(A)^\perp$.

## 2. Four Birds with One Stone: Extracting Bases for All Four Subspaces via $[A \mid I_m] \to [R \mid E]$

Memorizing the four dimensions is only half the battle. The real test of mastery is: **if someone hands you an arbitrary rectangular matrix $A$, how do you cleanly extract a basis for each of the four subspaces in a single unified workflow?**

Let us bring back our old friend from Lectures 07–09, the $3\times 4$ matrix $A$. Just as in Lecture 03's Gauss-Jordan inversion method, we augment $A$ on the right with the $3\times 3$ identity matrix $I_3$ (which faithfully records the footprint of every row operation!) and reduce all the way to $R = \operatorname{rref}(A)$:

$$
\left[\begin{array}{cccc|ccc}
1 & 2 & 2 & 2 & 1 & 0 & 0 \\
2 & 4 & 6 & 8 & 0 & 1 & 0 \\
3 & 6 & 8 & 10 & 0 & 0 & 1
\end{array}\right]
\xrightarrow{\text{Forward + Upward Elimination}}
\underbrace{\left[\begin{array}{cccc|ccc}
\mathbf{1} & 2 & \mathbf{0} & -2 & 3 & -1 & 0 \\
\mathbf{0} & 0 & \mathbf{1} & 2 & -1 & 1/2 & 0 \\
0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & \mathbf{-1} & \mathbf{-1} & \mathbf{1}
\end{array}\right]}_{[R \;\mid\; E], \;\;\text{where } EA = R}
$$

(Quick mental check on the bottom row $[-1, -1, 1]$ of the recording matrix $E$ on the right: multiplying $A$ on the left by $[-1, -1, 1]$ takes $-1(\text{row }1) - 1(\text{row }2) + 1(\text{row }3)$, which cancels the rows of $A$ into the exact zero row $[0, 0, 0, 0]$ at the bottom of $R$!)

Now open your eyes wide — reading directly from this $[R \mid E]$ tableau, we can **collect bases for all four subspaces in ten seconds flat**:

1. **Basis for the Column Space $\operatorname{Col}(A)$ ($\dim = r = 2$)**:
   Look at $R$ to see that the pivots sit in columns 1 and 3, then **go back to the original matrix $A$** to grab columns 1 and 3 (never copy columns from $R$!):

$$
\mathcal{B}_{\text{Col}(A)} = \left\{ \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 6 \\ 8 \end{bmatrix} \right\} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^3
$$

2. **Basis for the Nullspace $\operatorname{Null}(A)$ ($\dim = n - r = 2$)**:
   Because $\operatorname{Null}(A) = \operatorname{Null}(R)$, read off the two special solutions directly from $R$ corresponding to the free variables $(x_2, x_4)$:

$$
\mathcal{B}_{\text{Null}(A)} = \left\{ \begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 0 \\ -2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \right\} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^4
$$

3. **Basis for the Row Space $\operatorname{Row}(A) = \operatorname{Col}(A^T)$ ($\dim = r = 2$) — The Effortless Surprise!**:
   Although elementary row operations can alter the column space, they are nothing more than invertible linear combinations of the rows of $A$. Therefore, **elementary row operations preserve the row space 100%: $\operatorname{Row}(R) = \operatorname{Row}(A)$**! You don't even need to look back at $A$ to figure out which rows are independent — **simply copy the first $r$ nonzero rows of $R$ (transposed into column vectors)** for the cleanest possible basis:

$$
\mathcal{B}_{\text{Row}(A)} = \left\{ \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 0 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} \right\} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^4
$$

4. **Basis for the Left Nullspace $\operatorname{Null}(A^T)$ ($\dim = m - r = 3 - 2 = 1$) — The Crowning Touch!**:
   We want all row-combination weights $\mathbf{y}^T$ satisfying $\mathbf{y}^T A = \mathbf{0}^T$. Look at the block equation $E A = R$! Since **the bottom $m - r = 1$ row of $R$ is all zeros**, it follows immediately that **the bottom $m - r = 1$ row of the recording matrix $E$ multiplied on the left of $A$ produces a zero row**! Furthermore, because $E$ itself is invertible, its rows are automatically linearly independent! Thus, pulling out the bottom $m - r$ rows of $E$ and writing them as column vectors hands us a basis for the left nullspace on the spot:

$$
\mathcal{B}_{\text{Null}(A^T)} = \left\{ \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \right\} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^3
$$

## 3. Textbook-Grade Formulation: The Fundamental Theorem of Linear Algebra (Part I)

Why does the "row rank" (dimension of the row space) always equal the "column rank" (dimension of the column space) for any matrix, no matter how tall or wide? Let us seal this foundational truth with a formal theorem card and rigorous proof.

<DefinitionCard
  label="Definition 10.1"
  title="The Four Fundamental Subspaces of a Matrix"
>
For any real matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$:

1. **Column Space**: $\operatorname{Col}(A) = \{A\mathbf{x} : \mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n\} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^m$;
2. **Nullspace**: $\operatorname{Null}(A) = \{\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n : A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}\} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$;
3. **Row Space**: $\operatorname{Row}(A) := \operatorname{Col}(A^T) = \{A^T\mathbf{y} : \mathbf{y} \in \mathbb{R}^m\} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$;
4. **Left Nullspace**: $\operatorname{Null}(A^T) = \{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^m : A^T\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{0}\} = \{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^m : \mathbf{y}^TA=\mathbf{0}^T\} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^m$.
</DefinitionCard>

<TheoremCard
  label="Theorem 10.1"
  title="Fundamental Theorem of Linear Algebra, Part I (Dimensions and Bases of the Four Subspaces)"
>
Let a real matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ be reduced via an invertible row-operation matrix $E \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times m}$ to its reduced row echelon form $EA = R = \operatorname{rref}(A)$, with $r = \operatorname{rank}(A)$ pivots. Then:

1. **Row Rank Equals Column Rank**: $\operatorname{rank}(A^T) = \operatorname{rank}(A) = r$, meaning the row space and the column space have the **exact same dimension**:

$$
\dim \operatorname{Row}(A) = \dim \operatorname{Col}(A) = r
$$

2. **Conservation of Dimension in Both Worlds**:
   - In $\mathbb{R}^n$: $\dim \operatorname{Row}(A) = r$ and $\dim \operatorname{Null}(A) = n - r$, satisfying $r + (n - r) = n$;
   - In $\mathbb{R}^m$: $\dim \operatorname{Col}(A) = r$ and $\dim \operatorname{Null}(A^T) = m - r$, satisfying $r + (m - r) = m$.
3. **Invariance Under Row Operations & Master Basis Table**:
   - **Two Input Subspaces Preserved by Row Operations**: $\operatorname{Row}(R) = \operatorname{Row}(A)$ (the first $r$ nonzero rows of $R$ form a basis for $\operatorname{Row}(A)$), and $\operatorname{Null}(R) = \operatorname{Null}(A)$ (the $n-r$ special solutions of $R$ form a basis for $\operatorname{Null}(A)$);
   - **Basis Construction for the Two Output Subspaces**: A basis for $\operatorname{Col}(A)$ is given by the **$r$ pivot columns of the original matrix $A$**; a basis for the left nullspace $\operatorname{Null}(A^T)$ is given by the **last $m - r$ rows of $E$** (transposed into column vectors).
</TheoremCard>

<ProofCollapse title="Rigorous Proof: Why dim Row(A) = dim Col(A) = r, and Why the Bottom m−r Rows of E Span Null(Aᵀ)">
1. **Proof that $\operatorname{Row}(A) = \operatorname{Row}(R)$ and $\dim\operatorname{Row}(A) = r$**:
   Since $R = EA$ with $E$ invertible ($A = E^{-1}R$), Theorem 3.1 Part 2 from Lecture 03 (the row space of a product is contained in the row space of the right factor) gives:
   - $R = EA \implies \operatorname{Row}(R) \subseteq \operatorname{Row}(A)$;
   - $A = E^{-1}R \implies \operatorname{Row}(A) \subseteq \operatorname{Row}(R)$.
   Hence $\operatorname{Row}(A) = \operatorname{Row}(R)$! Now inspect the first $r$ nonzero rows $\mathbf{r}_1^T, \dots, \mathbf{r}_r^T$ of $R$ (the remaining $m-r$ rows are zero vectors). Clearly they span $\operatorname{Row}(R)$. Moreover, at the $r$ pivot column indices $j_1 < \cdots < j_r$, row $k$ has entry $1$ in component $j_k$ while all other $r-1$ rows have entry $0$ there. Thus if $\sum_{k=1}^r c_k \mathbf{r}_k = \mathbf{0}$, inspecting component $j_k$ for each $k$ immediately forces $c_1 = \cdots = c_r = 0$! Hence the first $r$ rows of $R$ are linearly independent and form a basis for $\operatorname{Row}(A)$, proving $\dim\operatorname{Col}(A^T) = \dim\operatorname{Row}(A) = r = \dim\operatorname{Col}(A)$!
   (As an immediate bonus: since $\operatorname{rank}(A^T) = \dim\operatorname{Col}(A^T) = r$, applying the Rank-Nullity Theorem from Lecture 09 directly to the matrix $A^T \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times m}$ yields $\dim\operatorname{Null}(A^T) = m - \operatorname{rank}(A^T) = m - r$!)

2. **Proof that the last $m - r$ rows of $E$ form a basis for $\operatorname{Null}(A^T)$**:
   Denote the rows of the invertible matrix $E \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times m}$ by $\mathbf{e}'^T_1, \dots, \mathbf{e}'^T_m$.
   - Since the last $m - r$ rows of $EA = R$ are zero rows, for each $i = r+1, \dots, m$ we have $\mathbf{e}'^T_i A = \mathbf{0}^T \iff A^T \mathbf{e}'_i = \mathbf{0}$, so $\{\mathbf{e}'_{r+1}, \dots, \mathbf{e}'_m\} \subseteq \operatorname{Null}(A^T)$.
   - Because $E$ is invertible, all $m$ of its rows are linearly independent, so any subset $\{\mathbf{e}'_{r+1}, \dots, \mathbf{e}'_m\}$ (consisting of $m - r$ vectors) must be linearly independent!
   - Since we already know $\dim\operatorname{Null}(A^T) = m - r$, finding $m - r$ linearly independent vectors inside an $(m - r)$-dimensional subspace (by the Halfway Theorem in Exercise `l09-ex3`) automatically guarantees they form a basis for $\operatorname{Null}(A^T)$!
</ProofCollapse>

<PitfallAlert title="Common Pitfalls & Transcript Errata">
1. **Pitfall 1: Confusing which two subspaces can be read directly from $R$ and which two cannot! (Must-Memorize Exam Checklist)**
   - **Can be read directly from $R$ (both live in the input space $\mathbb{R}^n$!)**: $\operatorname{Row}(A) = \operatorname{Row}(R)$ (copy the nonzero rows of $R$) and $\operatorname{Null}(A) = \operatorname{Null}(R)$ (copy the special solutions of $R$);
   - **Output-side bases**: If $R=EA$, then $\operatorname{Col}(R)=E\operatorname{Col}(A)$ and $\operatorname{Null}(R^T)=E^{-T}\operatorname{Null}(A^T)$, where $E^{-T}=(E^{-1})^T$. These subspaces can change or coincide, as when $A=R$. Use the pivot columns of $A$ and the transposed bottom rows of $E$ to obtain bases for the original spaces.
2. **Pitfall 2: Seeing $\dim\operatorname{Row}(A) = \dim\operatorname{Col}(A) = r$ and falsely concluding "the row space equals the column space"**
   **Equal dimension alone does not imply equality of subspaces.** When $m \neq n$ (say, for a $3\times 4$ matrix), $\operatorname{Row}(A)$ lives in $\mathbb{R}^4$ while $\operatorname{Col}(A)$ lives in $\mathbb{R}^3$ — they don't even live in the same ambient space! Even when $m = n$ (a square matrix), consider the $2\times 2$ matrix with first row $[0, 1]$ and second row $[0, 0]$: its row space $\operatorname{Row}(A)$ is the $y$-axis $\operatorname{span}\{(0,1)^T\}$, whereas its column space $\operatorname{Col}(A)$ is the $x$-axis $\operatorname{span}\{(1,0)^T\}$ — the two spaces are mutually perpendicular! (When $A$ is symmetric, $A^T=A$, we automatically get $\operatorname{Row}(A) = \operatorname{Col}(A)$.)
3. **Pitfall 3: Writing vectors in the row space horizontally and mixing them with column vectors**
   Following the convention of this course and Strang's textbook, so that all four fundamental subspaces live cleanly as subspaces of standard Euclidean column-vector spaces $\mathbb{R}^n$ or $\mathbb{R}^m$, basis vectors of the row space are always transposed and written vertically as column vectors in $\mathbb{R}^n$ (i.e., treated uniformly as $\operatorname{Col}(A^T)$).
</PitfallAlert>

           A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}

           Its column space is $\operatorname{Col}(A) = \operatorname{span}\{[1, 0]^T\}$ (the $x$-axis), whereas its row space is $\operatorname{Row}(A) = \operatorname{Col}(A^T) = \operatorname{span}\{[0, 1]^T\}$ (the $y$-axis). Both are $1$-dimensional subspaces of $\mathbb{R}^2$, yet $\operatorname{Row}(A) \neq \operatorname{Col}(A)$ and $\operatorname{Row}(A) \cap \operatorname{Col}(A) = \{\mathbf{0}\}$.

        2. **Common Sufficient Conditions Guaranteeing $\operatorname{Row}(A) = \operatorname{Col}(A)$**:
           - **Condition 1: $A$ is invertible ($\operatorname{rank}(A) = n$)**. Then $\dim\operatorname{Row}(A) = \dim\operatorname{Col}(A) = n$, so $\operatorname{Row}(A) = \mathbb{R}^n = \operatorname{Col}(A)$.
           - **Condition 2: $A$ is symmetric ($A^T = A$) or skew-symmetric ($A^T = -A$)**. Then $\operatorname{Row}(A) = \operatorname{Col}(A^T) = \operatorname{Col}(\pm A) = \operatorname{Col}(A)$. More generally, for a real normal matrix (one satisfying $A^TA=AA^T$), $\|A\mathbf{x}\|_2^2=\|A^T\mathbf{x}\|_2^2$ for every $\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^n$. Thus $\operatorname{Null}(A)=\operatorname{Null}(A^T)$, and taking orthogonal complements gives $\operatorname{Row}(A)=\operatorname{Col}(A)$.

      </SolutionRigorous>
    </DualSolution>

    title="Conceptual Trap (II): Which Fundamental Subspaces Are Preserved or Altered by Elementary Column Operations?"
  >
    Determine whether the following statement is true or false:

    > **Statement**: Performing invertible elementary column operations on an $m \times n$ matrix $A$ (that is, multiplying on the right by an invertible $n \times n$ matrix $Q$ to obtain $B = AQ$) preserves the nullspace ($\operatorname{Null}(AQ) = \operatorname{Null}(A)$) while changing the column space.

    <TrueFalseInteractive

      correct={false}
      trueLabel="True"
      falseLabel="False (Counterexample Exists)"

    Let $Q \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$ be invertible and set $B = AQ$. Prove rigorously: (1) $\operatorname{Col}(B) = \operatorname{Col}(A)$ and $\operatorname{Null}(B^T) = \operatorname{Null}(A^T)$; (2) how does the nullspace transform under $Q$ (write down the exact linear isomorphism between $\operatorname{Null}(AQ)$ and $\operatorname{Null}(A)$)?

    <ProgressiveHint
      buttonPrefix="Unlock Hint"
      hints={[
        "To prove (1), take the transpose $B^T = Q^T A^T$: since $Q^T$ is invertible, $B^T$ is obtained from $A^T$ by invertible row operations! Directly applying our theorem for $EA$ from the main text finishes (1) in one line.",
        "For (2): a vector $\\mathbf{z} \\in \\operatorname{Null}(AQ) \\iff A(Q\\mathbf{z}) = \\mathbf{0} \\iff Q\\mathbf{z} \\in \\operatorname{Null}(A)$. Multiply by $Q^{-1}$ to isolate $\\mathbf{z}$!"
      ]}
    />

    <DualSolution
      triggerLabel="Show Full Dual Solution (Intuition + Rigorous Proof)"
      intuitionLabel="Geometric Intuition"
      rigorousLabel="Rigorous Proof / Construction"
    >
      <SolutionIntuition>

        Multiplying on the right by an invertible matrix $Q$ takes invertible linear combinations of the **columns** of $A$ (such as adding column 1 to column 2, or swapping two columns). Recombining the same set of building blocks obviously builds the exact same output span $\operatorname{Col}(A)$! However, because you rearranged or recombined the column blocks, the input weights $\mathbf{x}$ needed to cancel them out to zero must undergo the inverse coordinate change $\mathbf{z} = Q^{-1}\mathbf{x}$!

      </SolutionIntuition>
      <SolutionRigorous>

        **The statement is False**. Here is the rigorous proof:

        1. **Both Output-Side Subspaces Are Completely Unchanged**:
           - Since $B = AQ$ and $A = BQ^{-1}$, the column-space inclusion rule for matrix products (Theorem 3.1 in Lecture 03) gives $\operatorname{Col}(B) \subseteq \operatorname{Col}(A)$ and $\operatorname{Col}(A) \subseteq \operatorname{Col}(B)$, so $\operatorname{Col}(AQ) = \operatorname{Col}(A)$.
           - For the left nullspace: $\mathbf{y} \in \operatorname{Null}(B^T) \iff Q^T (A^T \mathbf{y}) = \mathbf{0}$. Because $Q^T$ is invertible (its nullspace is trivial), this is equivalent to $A^T \mathbf{y} = \mathbf{0} \iff \mathbf{y} \in \operatorname{Null}(A^T)$. Hence $\operatorname{Null}((AQ)^T) = \operatorname{Null}(A^T)$.

        2. **Exact Coordinate Transformation of the Input-Side Nullspace**:
           For any $\mathbf{z} \in \mathbb{R}^n$:

           $$
           \mathbf{z} \in \operatorname{Null}(AQ) \iff A(Q\mathbf{z}) = \mathbf{0} \iff Q\mathbf{z} \in \operatorname{Null}(A) \iff \mathbf{z} \in Q^{-1}\operatorname{Null}(A)
           $$

           That is, $\operatorname{Null}(AQ) = Q^{-1}\operatorname{Null}(A)$ (the dimension remains $n - r$, but the subspace itself generally changes; for example, if $A = [1, 0]$ and $Q$ is the $2\times 2$ column-swap matrix with rows $[0, 1]$ and $[1, 0]$, then $\operatorname{Null}(A) = \operatorname{span}\{(0,1)^T\} \neq \operatorname{span}\{(1,0)^T\} = \operatorname{Null}(AQ)$).

      </SolutionRigorous>
    </DualSolution>

    1. **Specification A**: Construct a $3 \times 3$ matrix $A$ whose column space $\operatorname{Col}(A)$ contains $(1, 1, 0)^T$ and $(0, 1, 1)^T$, and whose nullspace $\operatorname{Null}(A)$ contains $(1, 0, 1)^T$ and $(0, 1, 1)^T$.

      intuitionLabel="Geometric Intuition"
      rigorousLabel="Rigorous Proof / Construction"
    >
      <SolutionIntuition>

        The Fundamental Theorem of Linear Algebra acts as the "conservation of energy law" for all matrix construction problems:
        - Specification A tries to cram at least $2$ dimensions of rank and at least $2$ dimensions of nullspace into an $n = 3$ dimensional input space: $2 + 2 = 4 \gt 3$, blowing past the capacity of $\mathbb{R}^3$ — impossible!
        - Specification B demands a $1$-dimensional line as the column space but a $2$-dimensional plane as the row space, violating the iron law that "row rank must equal column rank" — impossible!
        - Specification C asks for a $1$-dimensional line on both sides ($r = 1$), so multiplying the column vector by the row vector as an outer product $\mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^T$ finishes the job in one second!

      </SolutionIntuition>
      <SolutionRigorous>

        1. **Specification A: Impossible!**
           Because $(1,1,0)^T$ and $(0,1,1)^T$ are linearly independent, we must have $r = \dim\operatorname{Col}(A) \ge 2$. Similarly, $(1,0,1)^T$ and $(0,1,1)^T$ are linearly independent, so $\dim\operatorname{Null}(A) \ge 2$. By the Rank-Nullity Theorem, the number of columns must satisfy

           $$
           n = \dim\operatorname{Col}(A) + \dim\operatorname{Null}(A) \ge 2 + 2 = 4 \gt 3
           $$

           which contradicts $A$ being a $3\times 3$ matrix ($n = 3$)!

        2. **Specification B: Impossible!**
           By assumption, $\dim\operatorname{Col}(A) = 1$, whereas $(1,0)^T$ and $(0,1)^T$ are linearly independent so $\dim\operatorname{Row}(A) = 2$. This directly contradicts the Fundamental Theorem of Linear Algebra, $\dim\operatorname{Row}(A) = \dim\operatorname{Col}(A)$!

        3. **Specification C: Possible!**
           Set $\mathbf{u} = [1, 2, 3]^T$ and $\mathbf{v} = [1, -1, 2]^T$, and directly construct the rank-$1$ outer product matrix:

           $$
           A = \mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^T = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 & 2 \\ 2 & -2 & 4 \\ 3 & -3 & 6 \end{bmatrix}
           $$

           Every column is a nonzero multiple of $\mathbf{u}$ (so $\operatorname{Col}(A) = \operatorname{span}\{\mathbf{u}\}$), and every row is a nonzero multiple of $\mathbf{v}^T$ (so $\operatorname{Row}(A) = \operatorname{span}\{\mathbf{v}\}$), satisfying all requirements.

      </SolutionRigorous>
    </DualSolution>

    title="The Crown Jewel Challenge: Prove That Every Matrix A Establishes a Perfect Bijection Between Its Row Space and Column Space!"
  >
    For any $A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$, consider the following restriction. If $r=\operatorname{rank}(A)\lt\min(m,n)$, the linear map $\mathbf{x} \mapsto A\mathbf{x}$ from all of $\mathbb{R}^n$ to $\mathbb{R}^m$ is neither injective (since the nullspace is nontrivial) nor surjective (since the column space does not fill $\mathbb{R}^m$).
    However, if we restrict the domain to the **row space $\operatorname{Row}(A)$** and the codomain to the **column space $\operatorname{Col}(A)$**, a miracle occurs! Using the fact that **the row space and nullspace intersect only at the zero vector** — i.e., $\operatorname{Row}(A) \cap \operatorname{Null}(A) = \{\mathbf{0}\}$ over $\mathbb{R}^n$ (if $\mathbf{z} \in \operatorname{Row}(A) \cap \operatorname{Null}(A)$, then $\mathbf{z} = A^T\mathbf{w}$ and $A\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{0}$, so $\mathbf{z}^T\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{w}^T A \mathbf{z} = 0 \implies \mathbf{z}=\mathbf{0}$!) — prove:

    2. **Injectivity**: If $\mathbf{x}_r, \mathbf{y}_r \in \operatorname{Row}(A)$ satisfy $A\mathbf{x}_r = A\mathbf{y}_r$, prove that $\mathbf{x}_r = \mathbf{y}_r$! This shows that every reachable target $\mathbf{b} \in \operatorname{Col}(A)$ originates from a **globally unique** source vector $\mathbf{x}_r$ inside the row space $\operatorname{Row}(A)$!

    <ProgressiveHint

    <DualSolution

      intuitionLabel="Geometric Intuition"

    >
      <SolutionIntuition>

        This problem lifts the curtain on the crowning vista of Module 2 (Orthogonality) and Module 4 (Singular Value Decomposition SVD and the Pseudoinverse $A^+$)!
        For each $\mathbf{b}\in\operatorname{Col}(A)$, the full solution set is a translate of $\operatorname{Null}(A)$. It has infinitely many points precisely when $r\lt n$. Once we filter out the nullspace and stand purely on the $r$-dimensional sanctuary of the row space $\operatorname{Row}(A)$, all redundancy vanishes: every vector $\mathbf{x}_r$ in the $r$-dimensional row space is mapped by $A$ one-to-one and onto a unique vector $\mathbf{b} = A\mathbf{x}_r$ in the $r$-dimensional column space! Restricted between these two $r$-dimensional subspaces, **every matrix $A$ is essentially invertible**!

      </SolutionIntuition>
      <SolutionRigorous>

        **Preliminary Lemma (Direct-Sum Decomposition $\mathbb{R}^n = \operatorname{Row}(A) \oplus \operatorname{Null}(A)$)**:
        Let $\{\mathbf{v}_1, \dots, \mathbf{v}_r\}$ be a basis for $\operatorname{Row}(A)$ and $\{\mathbf{z}_1, \dots, \mathbf{z}_{n-r}\}$ be a basis for $\operatorname{Null}(A)$. If $\sum_{i=1}^r \alpha_i \mathbf{v}_i + \sum_{j=1}^{n-r} \beta_j \mathbf{z}_j = \mathbf{0}$, set $\mathbf{w} = \sum_{i=1}^r \alpha_i \mathbf{v}_i = -\sum_{j=1}^{n-r} \beta_j \mathbf{z}_j$. Then $\mathbf{w} \in \operatorname{Row}(A) \cap \operatorname{Null}(A) = \{\mathbf{0}\}$, so $\mathbf{w} = \mathbf{0}$. Linear independence of each individual basis then forces all $\alpha_i = 0$ and $\beta_j = 0$. Thus these $r + (n-r) = n$ linearly independent vectors form a basis for $\mathbb{R}^n$, and every $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ decomposes uniquely as $\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{x}_r + \mathbf{x}_n$ with $\mathbf{x}_r \in \operatorname{Row}(A)$ and $\mathbf{x}_n \in \operatorname{Null}(A)$.

        1. **Proof of Surjectivity (Existence)**:
           Take any $\mathbf{b} \in \operatorname{Col}(A)$. By definition of the column space, there exists $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ such that $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$. Decompose $\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{x}_r + \mathbf{x}_n$ ($\mathbf{x}_r \in \operatorname{Row}(A), \mathbf{x}_n \in \operatorname{Null}(A)$) and multiply on the left by $A$:

           $$
           \mathbf{b} = A\mathbf{x} = A(\mathbf{x}_r + \mathbf{x}_n) = A\mathbf{x}_r + \mathbf{0} = A\mathbf{x}_r
           $$

           Hence there always exists $\mathbf{x}_r \in \operatorname{Row}(A)$ inside the row space that maps to $\mathbf{b}$!

        2. **Proof of Injectivity (Uniqueness)**:
           Suppose $\mathbf{x}_r, \mathbf{y}_r \in \operatorname{Row}(A)$ satisfy $A\mathbf{x}_r = A\mathbf{y}_r$. Consider the difference vector $\mathbf{d} = \mathbf{x}_r - \mathbf{y}_r$:
           - Since $\operatorname{Row}(A)$ is a subspace (closed under subtraction), $\mathbf{d} \in \operatorname{Row}(A)$;
           - Since $A\mathbf{d} = A\mathbf{x}_r - A\mathbf{y}_r = \mathbf{0}$, we also have $\mathbf{d} \in \operatorname{Null}(A)$.
           Therefore $\mathbf{d} \in \operatorname{Row}(A) \cap \operatorname{Null}(A) = \{\mathbf{0}\}$, which means $\mathbf{x}_r - \mathbf{y}_r = \mathbf{0} \implies \mathbf{x}_r = \mathbf{y}_r$!
           In conclusion, the restricted map $\left.A\right|_{\operatorname{Row}(A)} : \operatorname{Row}(A) \xrightarrow{\;\sim\;} \operatorname{Col}(A)$ is a linear isomorphism (bijection) between two $r$-dimensional subspaces!

      </SolutionRigorous>
