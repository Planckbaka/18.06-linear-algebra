At the close of Lecture 05, we established the golden test for any subspace: **it must contain the origin $\mathbf{0}$ and be closed under all linear combinations**. Entering MIT 18.06 Lecture 06, Professor Gilbert Strang begins with a deceptively simple geometric warm-up that shatters a common illusion about the union of subspaces, and then introduces the first two stars among the Four Fundamental Subspaces of linear algebra: the **Column Space ($\operatorname{Col}(A)$)** and the **Nullspace ($\operatorname{Null}(A)$)**.

From today onward, whenever you look at a matrix equation $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$, you will automatically view it through a dual-universe lens: **in the output universe $\mathbb{R}^m$, the column space tells you whether a solution exists; in the input universe $\mathbb{R}^n$, the nullspace tells you whether that solution is unique!**

---

## 1. Set Operations on Subspaces: Why the Union $S \cup T$ Fails while the Intersection $S \cap T$ Always Succeeds

Suppose that inside three-dimensional space $\mathbb{R}^3$, you hold two genuine subspaces: a two-dimensional plane $P$ passing through the origin, and a line $L$ also passing through the origin that does not lie inside $P$. Let us ask two natural set-theoretic questions:

1. **Is the union $P \cup L$ (the set containing all vectors in the plane together with all vectors on the line) a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^3$?**
   **Emphatically no!** Even though the plane and the line each pass through the origin and are each closed on their own, the moment you pick a nonzero vector $\mathbf{p} \in P$ with $\mathbf{p} \notin L$ and a nonzero vector $\mathbf{l} \in L$ with $\mathbf{l} \notin P$, their sum $\mathbf{p} + \mathbf{l}$ forms the diagonal of a parallelogram that flies right out of the plane $P$ and veers off the line $L$, landing in the empty air between them ($\mathbf{p} + \mathbf{l} \notin P \cup L$)! (Note: the union $S \cup T$ of two subspaces is a subspace if and only if one is completely contained in the other, i.e., $S \subseteq T$ or $T \subseteq S$. If you want the smallest genuine subspace containing both $S$ and $T$, you must take their **sum of subspaces** $S + T := \{\mathbf{s} + \mathbf{t} : \mathbf{s} \in S,\; \mathbf{t} \in T\}$!)

2. **Is the intersection $S \cap T$ (the set of vectors belonging simultaneously to both subspaces $S$ and $T$) a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^n$?**
   **100% yes—always!** And this holds without exception for the intersection of any collection of subspaces:
   - First, $\mathbf{0} \in S$ and $\mathbf{0} \in T \implies \mathbf{0} \in S \cap T$ (the intersection is never empty; in our 3D example, $P \cap L = \{\mathbf{0}\}$, which is the valid zero-dimensional subspace!).
   - Second, take any two common vectors $\mathbf{v}, \mathbf{w} \in S \cap T$ and any scalars $c, d \in \mathbb{R}$. Because $\mathbf{v}, \mathbf{w}$ lie in $S$ and $S$ is closed under linear combinations, we have $c\mathbf{v} + d\mathbf{w} \in S$. By the exact same reasoning in $T$, we also have $c\mathbf{v} + d\mathbf{w} \in T$. Since $c\mathbf{v} + d\mathbf{w}$ lies in both $S$ and $T$, it necessarily lies in $S \cap T$!

## 2. The Star of the Output Space $\mathbb{R}^m$: The Column Space $\operatorname{Col}(A)$

Let us examine a concrete $4\times 3$ tall-and-thin matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{4\times 3}$:

$$
A = \begin{bmatrix}
1 & 1 & 2 \\
2 & 1 & 3 \\
3 & 1 & 4 \\
4 & 1 & 5
\end{bmatrix}
$$

First, let us answer the most fundamental ambient-space question: **Which space does the column space $\operatorname{Col}(A)$ (also denoted $C(A)$ by Strang) live in?**
Count how many components each column has: every column is a $4\times 1$ vector, so **$\operatorname{Col}(A)$ is a subspace of four-dimensional space $\mathbb{R}^4$** ($\operatorname{Col}(A) \subseteq \mathbb{R}^4$)!

Next, we ask: do all linear combinations of these $3$ columns fill up the entire space $\mathbb{R}^4$?
- First barrier: we only have $3$ column vectors. Even if they were completely independent, $3$ vectors could never span a $4$-dimensional space $\mathbb{R}^4$.
- Second revelation: look closely at column 3, $\mathbf{a}_3 = (2, 3, 4, 5)^T$—it is the exact sum of the first two columns, **$\mathbf{a}_3 = \mathbf{a}_1 + \mathbf{a}_2$**!

This means column 3 is a pure "free-rider" (a redundant, dependent column): any linear combination that uses $\mathbf{a}_3$, namely $x_1\mathbf{a}_1 + x_2\mathbf{a}_2 + x_3(\mathbf{a}_1 + \mathbf{a}_2) = (x_1+x_3)\mathbf{a}_1 + (x_2+x_3)\mathbf{a}_2$, could already be built using only the first two columns $\mathbf{a}_1$ and $\mathbf{a}_2$! If we throw column 3 away, $\operatorname{Col}(A)$ does not shrink by a single point—inside the four-dimensional ambient universe $\mathbb{R}^4$, $\operatorname{Col}(A)$ is merely a **two-dimensional flat plane** through the origin!

This also completely settles when the system $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ is solvable:
- When $\mathbf{b} = (1, 2, 3, 4)^T$, $\mathbf{b}$ is literally column 1 itself, so $\mathbf{b} \in \operatorname{Col}(A)$ and a solution exists (in fact, more than one: $\mathbf{x} = (1, 0, 0)^T$ works, and so does $(0, -1, 1)^T$!).
- When $\mathbf{b} = (1, 0, 0, 0)^T$, no linear combination of the first two columns (whose entries always form an arithmetic progression) can ever produce $(1,0,0,0)^T$; thus $\mathbf{b} \notin \operatorname{Col}(A)$, and no solution exists!

## 3. The Star of the Input Space $\mathbb{R}^n$: The Nullspace $\operatorname{Null}(A)$

When we noticed the dependency relation $\mathbf{a}_1 + \mathbf{a}_2 - \mathbf{a}_3 = \mathbf{0}$ a moment ago, we bumped straight into the second fundamental subspace of a matrix: the **Nullspace** (also called the **Kernel** in pure mathematics), denoted $\operatorname{Null}(A)$ or $N(A)$.

Continuing with the exact same $4\times 3$ matrix $A$ above:
- **Which ambient space does $\operatorname{Null}(A)$ live in?** We are looking for all unknown input vectors $\mathbf{x} = (x_1, x_2, x_3)^T$ that satisfy $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$! Since there are $n = 3$ unknowns, **$\operatorname{Null}(A)$ is a subspace of the three-dimensional input space $\mathbb{R}^3$** ($\operatorname{Null}(A) \subseteq \mathbb{R}^3$)—never confuse its home with the column space living in $\mathbb{R}^4$!
- **Finding the complete nullspace for this example**: Because $\mathbf{a}_1 + \mathbf{a}_2 - \mathbf{a}_3 = \mathbf{0}$, the vector $\mathbf{s} = (1, 1, -1)^T$ clearly satisfies $A\mathbf{s} = \mathbf{0}$. Multiplying $\mathbf{s}$ by any real scalar $c \in \mathbb{R}$ gives $c(1, 1, -1)^T$, which still sends the combination to zero (and since the first two columns are not collinear, there are no other independent nullspace directions). Therefore, the nullspace of $A$ is precisely a **one-dimensional line** in $\mathbb{R}^3$ passing through the origin $(0,0,0)^T$ and $(1,1,-1)^T$:

$$
\operatorname{Null}(A) = \left\{ c \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} : c \in \mathbb{R} \right\} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^3
$$

Below we formalize the rigorous definitions of both fundamental subspaces, the proof of subspace closure, and the crucial contrast with non-homogeneous solution sets:

<DefinitionCard
  label="Definition 6.1"
  title="Column Space Col(A) and Nullspace Null(A) of a Matrix"
>
Let $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ be a real matrix with $m$ rows and $n$ columns:

1. The **Column Space** $\operatorname{Col}(A)$ is a subset of the **output space $\mathbb{R}^m$**, consisting of all real linear combinations of the $n$ columns of $A$:

$$
\operatorname{Col}(A) := \left\{ A\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^m : \mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n \right\} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^m
$$

2. The **Nullspace (Kernel)** $\operatorname{Null}(A)$ is a subset of the **input space $\mathbb{R}^n$**, consisting of all solution vectors to the homogeneous linear system $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$:

$$
\operatorname{Null}(A) := \left\{ \mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n : A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0} \right\} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n
$$
</DefinitionCard>

<TheoremCard
  label="Theorem 6.1"
  title="Intersection of Subspaces, Subspace Property of Null(A), and Non-Homogeneous Failure"
>
1. **Arbitrary Intersections Are Subspaces**: If $S, T \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ are subspaces of $\mathbb{R}^n$, then their intersection $S \cap T$ is always a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^n$; whereas their union $S \cup T$ is a subspace **if and only if** $S \subseteq T$ or $T \subseteq S$.
2. **Column Space and Nullspace Are Genuine Subspaces**: For any $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$, $\operatorname{Col}(A)$ is always a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^m$, and $\operatorname{Null}(A)$ is always a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^n$.
3. **Non-Homogeneous Solution Sets Are Never Subspaces**: Whenever the right-hand side vector $\mathbf{b} \neq \mathbf{0}$, the solution set $K_{\mathbf{b}} := \{\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n : A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}\}$ of $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ is **never** a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^n$ (if no solution exists, $K_{\mathbf{b}} = \varnothing$; if a solution exists, $\mathbf{0} \notin K_{\mathbf{b}}$ because $A\mathbf{0} = \mathbf{0} \neq \mathbf{b}$).
4. **Trivial Nullspace and Linear Independence of Columns**: $\operatorname{Null}(A) = \{\mathbf{0}\} \iff$ the $n$ columns of $A$ are linearly independent $\iff$ for every solvable $\mathbf{b} \in \operatorname{Col}(A)$, the solution to $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ is **unique**.
</TheoremCard>

<ProofCollapse title="Rigorous Proof: Two-Line Closure Proof for Null(A) & Necessary/Sufficient Condition for S ∪ T">
1. **Two-line proof that $\operatorname{Null}(A)$ is a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^n$**:
   - Non-emptiness: $A\mathbf{0} = \mathbf{0} \implies \mathbf{0} \in \operatorname{Null}(A)$.
   - Closure under linear combinations: Take any $\mathbf{v}, \mathbf{w} \in \operatorname{Null}(A)$ (meaning $A\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$ and $A\mathbf{w} = \mathbf{0}$) and any scalars $c, d \in \mathbb{R}$. By the linearity (distributive law) of matrix multiplication:

$$
A(c\mathbf{v} + d\mathbf{w}) = c(A\mathbf{v}) + d(A\mathbf{w}) = c\mathbf{0} + d\mathbf{0} = \mathbf{0}
$$

     Hence $c\mathbf{v} + d\mathbf{w} \in \operatorname{Null}(A)$! Notice that this proof never writes down a single matrix entry or elimination step—it follows purely and effortlessly from operator linearity!

2. **Proof that "$S \cup T$ is a subspace $\iff S \subseteq T$ or $T \subseteq S$"**:
   - $(\impliedby)$ If $S \subseteq T$, then $S \cup T = T$, which is already a subspace; the case $T \subseteq S$ is identical.
   - $(\implies)$ By contradiction: suppose $S \cup T$ is a subspace, yet neither $S \subseteq T$ nor $T \subseteq S$ holds. Then there exist $\mathbf{s} \in S \setminus T$ and $\mathbf{t} \in T \setminus S$. Since $\mathbf{s}, \mathbf{t} \in S \cup T$ and $S \cup T$ is closed under addition, their sum $\mathbf{u} := \mathbf{s} + \mathbf{t}$ must lie in $S \cup T$.
     - If $\mathbf{u} \in S$, then because $\mathbf{s} \in S$ and $S$ is a subspace, $\mathbf{t} = \mathbf{u} - \mathbf{s} \in S$, contradicting $\mathbf{t} \notin S$!
     - If $\mathbf{u} \in T$, then because $\mathbf{t} \in T$ and $T$ is a subspace, $\mathbf{s} = \mathbf{u} - \mathbf{t} \in T$, contradicting $\mathbf{s} \notin T$!
     Thus the assumption fails, and we must have $S \subseteq T$ or $T \subseteq S$.
</ProofCollapse>

<PitfallAlert title="Common Pitfalls & Transcript Errata">
1. **Pitfall 1: Drawing $\operatorname{Col}(A)$ and $\operatorname{Null}(A)$ inside the same coordinate axes! (Dimensional mismatch hazard)**
   For an $m\times n$ rectangular matrix (such as a $4\times 3$ matrix), vectors in $\operatorname{Col}(A)$ have $4$ components (living in $\mathbb{R}^4$), whereas vectors in $\operatorname{Null}(A)$ have $3$ components (living in $\mathbb{R}^3$)! They don't even have the same number of entries, so you can never intersect them or plot them in the same ambient space! (Even when $m=n$ so $A$ is square, the input space and output space should still be kept conceptually distinct.)
2. **Pitfall 2: Writing $\operatorname{Null}(A) = \varnothing$ or $\operatorname{Null}(A) = 0$ when the nullspace contains only the zero vector**
   When the columns of $A$ are linearly independent (no nonzero solutions), the homogeneous system $A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}$ still has the unique trivial solution $\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}$! Proper set notation requires **$\operatorname{Null}(A) = \{\mathbf{0}\}$** (a zero-dimensional subspace containing one vector, the zero vector), never the empty set $\varnothing$!
3. **Pitfall 3: Assuming that the solutions to $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ form a subspace**
   Whenever $\mathbf{b} \neq \mathbf{0}$, adding two solutions $\mathbf{x}_1, \mathbf{x}_2$ gives $A(\mathbf{x}_1 + \mathbf{x}_2) = \mathbf{b} + \mathbf{b} = 2\mathbf{b} \neq \mathbf{b}$! The sum of two solutions is no longer a solution to the original equation (whereas their **difference** $\mathbf{x}_1 - \mathbf{x}_2$ really does land back inside the nullspace $\operatorname{Null}(A)$, since $A(\mathbf{x}_1 - \mathbf{x}_2) = \mathbf{b} - \mathbf{b} = \mathbf{0}$!).
</PitfallAlert>

<DualSolution
  triggerLabel="Show Full Dual Solution (Intuition + Rigorous Proof)"
  intuitionLabel="Geometric Intuition"

>
<SolutionIntuition>

1. Instant ambient-space check: $\operatorname{Col}(A) \subseteq \mathbb{R}^2$ and $\operatorname{Null}(A) \subseteq \mathbb{R}^4$!
2. The first two columns are the 2D unit coordinate vectors $(1,0)^T$ and $(0,1)^T$. Using just those two, we can reach every point in the 2D plane, so **$\operatorname{Col}(A) = \mathbb{R}^2$**, and $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ is 100% solvable for every $\mathbf{b} \in \mathbb{R}^2$!
3. Column 3 is $(2, -3)^T$, so we cancel it with $-2$ times column 1 plus $3$ times column 2, giving $\mathbf{s}_1 = (-2, 3, 1, 0)^T$. Column 4 is $(-1, 4)^T$, so we cancel it with $+1$ times column 1 minus $4$ times column 2, giving $\mathbf{s}_2 = (1, -4, 0, 1)^T$! (This previews the systematic "special solution" construction we will master in Lecture 07!)

</SolutionIntuition>
<SolutionRigorous>

1. Since $A \in \mathbb{R}^{2\times 4}$, we have $\operatorname{Col}(A) \subseteq \mathbb{R}^2$ and $\operatorname{Null}(A) \subseteq \mathbb{R}^4$.
2. For any $\mathbf{b} = (b_1, b_2)^T \in \mathbb{R}^2$, choosing $\mathbf{x} = (b_1, b_2, 0, 0)^T \in \mathbb{R}^4$ immediately gives $A\mathbf{x} = b_1\mathbf{e}_1 + b_2\mathbf{e}_2 = \mathbf{b}$. Hence $\operatorname{Col}(A) = \mathbb{R}^2$, and a solution exists for every $\mathbf{b} \in \mathbb{R}^2$.
3. Solving the homogeneous system $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$:

$$
\begin{cases}
x_1 + 2x_3 - x_4 = 0 \\
x_2 - 3x_3 + 4x_4 = 0
\end{cases}
\iff
\begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \\ x_3 \\ x_4 \end{bmatrix}
= x_3 \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ 3 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{s}_1}
+ x_4 \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -4 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{s}_2}
$$

   Because components 3 and 4 of $\mathbf{s}_1, \mathbf{s}_2$ are $(1,0)$ and $(0,1)$ respectively, the two vectors are strictly non-collinear and span the 2D nullspace plane $\operatorname{Null}(A) = \operatorname{span}\{\mathbf{s}_1, \mathbf{s}_2\} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^4$.

</SolutionRigorous>

<SolutionIntuition>

Picture two planes through the origin like two intersecting glass panels of a revolving door: their intersection is the vertical central axle (a line through the origin, perfectly closed). </SolutionIntuition>
<SolutionRigorous>

We construct an explicit counterexample. Let

$$
P_1 = \{(x, y, 0)^T : x, y \in \mathbb{R}\}, \qquad
P_2 = \{(0, y, z)^T : y, z \in \mathbb{R}\}
$$

Both are 2D subspaces of $\mathbb{R}^3$ passing through the origin, and neither contains the other (their intersection $P_1 \cap P_2 = \{(0, y, 0)^T : y \in \mathbb{R}\}$ is the $y$-axis subspace). Their sum $\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v} = (1, 0, 1)^T$ satisfies neither $z=0$ nor $x=0$, so $\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v} \notin P_1 \cup P_2$. Thus the union is not closed under addition and is not a subspace.

</SolutionRigorous>

<DualSolution
  triggerLabel="Show Full Dual Solution (Intuition + Rigorous Proof)"
  intuitionLabel="Geometric Intuition"
  rigorousLabel="Rigorous Proof / Construction"
>
<SolutionIntuition>

This question is an all-time classic on MIT 18.06 exams! The only way the new column $\mathbf{b}$ fails to expand the column space by even an inch is if $\mathbf{b}$ was already living inside $\operatorname{Col}(A)$ all along! Since $\mathbf{b}$ is a combination of the old columns, $A\mathbf{x}_0 = \mathbf{b}$, we can rearrange this as $A\mathbf{x}_0 - 1\cdot\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{0}$. Look at that: the tall vector $(\mathbf{x}_0, -1)$ has $-1$ in its last entry, so it is guaranteed to be nonzero, and it lands squarely inside the nullspace of the augmented matrix $B$!

</SolutionIntuition>
<SolutionRigorous>

1. Since $\mathbf{b} = B\mathbf{e}_{n+1} \in \operatorname{Col}(B)$, the equality $\operatorname{Col}(B) = \operatorname{Col}(A)$ implies $\mathbf{b} \in \operatorname{Col}(A)$.
2. By definition of the column space, there exists $\mathbf{x}_0 \in \mathbb{R}^n$ such that $A\mathbf{x}_0 = \mathbf{b}$ (so $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ is solvable).
3. Construct the vector $\mathbf{z} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{x}_0 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{n+1}$. Because its last component is $-1 \neq 0$, we have $\mathbf{z} \neq \mathbf{0}$. Using block matrix multiplication:

$$
B \mathbf{z} = \begin{bmatrix} A & \mathbf{b} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{x}_0 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} = A\mathbf{x}_0 - \mathbf{b} = \mathbf{0}
$$

   Hence $\mathbf{z} \in \operatorname{Null}(B) \setminus \{\mathbf{0}\}$, so the nullspace of $B$ necessarily contains a nonzero vector. The statement holds rigorously.

</SolutionRigorous>
</DualSolution>

<SolutionIntuition>

1. Building a matrix is like snapping Lego bricks together:
   - Put $(1, 1, 0)^T$ directly into column 1 and $(0, 1, 1)^T$ into column 2, guaranteeing that both belong to $\operatorname{Col}(A)$.
   - Requiring $(1, 0, 1)^T$ to be crushed to zero by $A$ simply means "column 1 + column 3 = $\mathbf{0}$," so we fill column 3 with the negative of column 1, namely $(-1, -1, 0)^T$, and we are done! Because there are only two independent columns left, $\operatorname{Col}(A)$ is only a 2D plane and cannot possibly be all of $\mathbb{R}^3$!
2. **Impossible!** For a $3\times 3$ square matrix, having the nonzero vector $(1, 1, 1)^T$ in the nullspace means the three columns sum to zero (they are coplanar and redundant)—and two independent columns can never span three-dimensional space $\mathbb{R}^3$!

</SolutionIntuition>
<SolutionRigorous>

1. Let $\mathbf{a}_1 = (1, 1, 0)^T$ and $\mathbf{a}_2 = (0, 1, 1)^T$. To enforce $A(1, 0, 1)^T = \mathbf{a}_1 + \mathbf{a}_3 = \mathbf{0}$, set $\mathbf{a}_3 = -\mathbf{a}_1 = (-1, -1, 0)^T$, yielding the desired matrix:

$$
A = \begin{bmatrix}
1 & 0 & -1 \\
1 & 1 & -1 \\
0 & 1 & 0
\end{bmatrix}
$$

   Verification: $A\mathbf{e}_1 = (1,1,0)^T \in \operatorname{Col}(A)$, $A\mathbf{e}_2 = (0,1,1)^T \in \operatorname{Col}(A)$, and $A(1,0,1)^T = \mathbf{0}$. Since $\operatorname{Col}(A) = \operatorname{span}\{\mathbf{a}_1, \mathbf{a}_2\}$ (for instance, $(1, 0, 0)^T \notin \operatorname{Col}(A)$), we have $\operatorname{Col}(A) \neq \mathbb{R}^3$.

2. **Rigorous impossibility proof**: Suppose there existed a square matrix $M \in \mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}$ with $(1, 1, 1)^T \in \operatorname{Null}(M)$. By Theorem 3.2 (Part 2), $M$ is singular (non-invertible). By Lecture 01 Theorem 1.1 (a square matrix is invertible $\iff \operatorname{Col}(M) = \mathbb{R}^3$), we must have $\operatorname{Col}(M) \subsetneq \mathbb{R}^3$, a contradiction! Thus no such $3\times 3$ matrix exists. (Note: if a $3\times 4$ rectangular matrix were allowed, such as $\begin{bmatrix} I_3 & -I_3\mathbf{1} \end{bmatrix}$, both properties could hold simultaneously!)

Let $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ and $B \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times p}$ be nonzero matrices:
1. **Core Subspace Characterization**: Prove that the matrix product is the zero matrix ($AB = 0 \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times p}$) **if and only if** the entire column space of the right matrix $B$ lies inside the nullspace of the left matrix $A$:

$$
AB = 0 \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad \operatorname{Col}(B) \subseteq \operatorname{Null}(A)
$$

2. **Preview of Projection Operators**: Let a square matrix $P \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$ satisfy the idempotent law **$P^2 = P$** (such as a projection onto a plane). Define $Q := I_n - P$:
   - Use Part 1 to show that $\operatorname{Col}(Q) \subseteq \operatorname{Null}(P)$, and then prove the stronger equality **$\operatorname{Col}(I_n - P) = \operatorname{Null}(P)$**!
   - Prove that every vector $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ decomposes uniquely as $\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}$ with $\mathbf{u} \in \operatorname{Col}(P)$ and $\mathbf{v} \in \operatorname{Null}(P)$, and that the intersection of the two subspaces is trivial: $\operatorname{Col}(P) \cap \operatorname{Null}(P) = \{\mathbf{0}\}$!

  intuitionLabel="Geometric Intuition"
  rigorousLabel="Rigorous Proof / Construction"
>
<SolutionIntuition>

1. How can two nonzero matrices multiply to produce the all-zero matrix $0$? Think of $B$ as a launcher whose outgoing projectiles all land inside the target zone $\operatorname{Col}(B)$, while $\operatorname{Null}(A)$ is the black hole of $A$ that annihilates everything inside it. As long as the entire landing zone $\operatorname{Col}(B)$ of $B$ sits 100% inside the black hole $\operatorname{Null}(A)$ of $A$, any input $\mathbf{x}$ first gets mapped to $B\mathbf{x} \in \operatorname{Null}(A)$ and is then annihilated by $A$ into $\mathbf{0}$!
2. For an idempotent matrix $P^2 = P$, the identity decomposition $\mathbf{x} = P\mathbf{x} + (I_n - P)\mathbf{x}$ works like magic: the first piece $P\mathbf{x}$ automatically lives in $\operatorname{Col}(P)$, while multiplying the second piece $(I_n - P)\mathbf{x}$ on the left by $P$ gives $(P - P^2)\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$, so it automatically lives in $\operatorname{Null}(P)$! And if a vector $\mathbf{w}$ lies in both $\operatorname{Col}(P)$ (where $P$ leaves it untouched: $P\mathbf{w} = \mathbf{w}$) and $\operatorname{Null}(P)$ (where $P$ kills it: $P\mathbf{w} = \mathbf{0}$), then $\mathbf{w}$ has no choice but to be the zero vector $\mathbf{w} = \mathbf{0}$!

</SolutionIntuition>
<SolutionRigorous>

1. **Proof that $AB = 0 \iff \operatorname{Col}(B) \subseteq \operatorname{Null}(A)$**:
   - $(\implies)$ Suppose $AB = 0$. For any $\mathbf{y} \in \operatorname{Col}(B)$, there exists $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^p$ such that $\mathbf{y} = B\mathbf{x}$. Then $A\mathbf{y} = A(B\mathbf{x}) = (AB)\mathbf{x} = 0\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0} \implies \mathbf{y} \in \operatorname{Null}(A)$. Hence $\operatorname{Col}(B) \subseteq \operatorname{Null}(A)$.
   - $(\impliedby)$ Suppose $\operatorname{Col}(B) \subseteq \operatorname{Null}(A)$. For each column $\mathbf{b}_j = B\mathbf{e}_j \in \operatorname{Col}(B)$ ($j = 1, \dots, p$), we have $\mathbf{b}_j \in \operatorname{Null}(A) \implies A\mathbf{b}_j = \mathbf{0}$. Thus $AB = \begin{bmatrix} A\mathbf{b}_1 & \cdots & A\mathbf{b}_p \end{bmatrix} = 0$.

2. **Proof of the direct-sum subspace decomposition for an idempotent matrix**:
   - Since $P(I_n - P) = P - P^2 = 0$, Part 1 yields $\operatorname{Col}(I_n - P) \subseteq \operatorname{Null}(P)$. Conversely, for any $\mathbf{z} \in \operatorname{Null}(P)$, we have $P\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{0}$, so $\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{z} - P\mathbf{z} = (I_n - P)\mathbf{z} \in \operatorname{Col}(I_n - P)$, which proves $\operatorname{Null}(P) \subseteq \operatorname{Col}(I_n - P)$. By mutual inclusion, **$\operatorname{Col}(I_n - P) = \operatorname{Null}(P)$**.
   - **Trivial intersection**: Take any $\mathbf{w} \in \operatorname{Col}(P) \cap \operatorname{Null}(P)$. Since $\mathbf{w} \in \operatorname{Col}(P)$, there exists $\mathbf{y}$ such that $\mathbf{w} = P\mathbf{y}$, whence $P\mathbf{w} = P^2\mathbf{y} = P\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{w}$. Since $\mathbf{w} \in \operatorname{Null}(P)$, we also have $P\mathbf{w} = \mathbf{0}$. Combining the two gives $\mathbf{w} = \mathbf{0}$, so $\operatorname{Col}(P) \cap \operatorname{Null}(P) = \{\mathbf{0}\}$.
   - **Existence and uniqueness of decomposition**: For any $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n$, set $\mathbf{u} = P\mathbf{x} \in \operatorname{Col}(P)$ and $\mathbf{v} = (I_n - P)\mathbf{x} \in \operatorname{Null}(P)$; clearly $\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}$. If $\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{u}' + \mathbf{v}'$ is another such decomposition, subtracting gives $\mathbf{u} - \mathbf{u}' = \mathbf{v}' - \mathbf{v} \in \operatorname{Col}(P) \cap \operatorname{Null}(P) = \{\mathbf{0}\}$ (by subspace closure), whence $\mathbf{u}' = \mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}' = \mathbf{v}$.

</SolutionRigorous>

  title="High-Dimensional Representation Subspaces & Linear Probing: What Column Space Do Neural Hidden Layers Span?"
  paperRef="Alain & Bengio (ICLR 2017 Workshop) Understanding Intermediate Layers Using Linear Classifier Probes · Elhage et al. (2022) Toy Models of Superposition"
>

In modern deep learning and large language model interpretability (**Mechanistic Interpretability**), a central theoretical pillar is the **Linear Representation Hypothesis**: neural networks encode high-level semantic concepts (such as part of speech, truthfulness, geographic coordinates, or syntactic code roles) as **1D linear directions or low-dimensional linear subspaces** inside their high-dimensional hidden space $\mathbb{R}^d$. The standard experimental tool for testing this hypothesis—**Linear Probing**—is mathematically 100% equivalent to our Lecture 06 analysis of **column space inclusion $\mathbf{y} \in \operatorname{Col}(H)$** and **silent nullspace directions $\operatorname{Null}(W)$**!

### 1. The Column Space of the Activation Matrix $H$: When Can a Linear Probe Decode a Target Attribute?

Suppose we feed $N$ examples (say, $N = 10{,}000$ natural language sentences) into a frozen pre-trained Transformer and extract the hidden state vectors $\mathbf{h}_1, \dots, \mathbf{h}_N \in \mathbb{R}^d$ at layer $\ell$ (say, $d = 4096$), stacking them row by row into the **sample-by-feature activation matrix**:

$$
H = \begin{bmatrix}
\mathbf{h}_1^T \\
\mathbf{h}_2^T \\
\vdots \\
\mathbf{h}_N^T
\end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times d}
$$

Training a bias-free **linear probe** to predict a target scalar attribute $\mathbf{y} = (y_1, \dots, y_N)^T \in \mathbb{R}^N$ (such as sentiment polarity or world coordinates) means keeping $H$ frozen and searching for a probe weight vector $\mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ that solves the linear system:

$$
H \mathbf{w} = \mathbf{y}
$$

Applying **Definition 6.1** and our solvability criterion from this lecture reveals two immediate geometric truths in high dimensions:

1. **Perfect linear decodability holds if and only if $\mathbf{y} \in \operatorname{Col}(H) \subseteq \mathbb{R}^N$**:
   Inside the massive $N$-dimensional sample-outcome space $\mathbb{R}^N$ (typically $N \gg d$, so $H$ is a tall-and-thin matrix!), $H$ has only $d$ feature columns, so its column space $\operatorname{Col}(H)$ is at most a razor-thin $d$-dimensional subspace slice inside $\mathbb{R}^N$!
   - In shallow layers, before nonlinearly tangled features have been "unfolded and flattened," the target vector $\mathbf{y}$ sits far outside $\operatorname{Col}(H)$, and no linear probe can succeed.
   - As depth increases, Transformer attention and MLP blocks iteratively fold and rearrange the space, pulling the target semantic vector $\mathbf{y}$ cleanly toward the column space $\operatorname{Col}(H^{(L)})$ of the deep activation matrix, causing linear probe accuracy to surge!

2. **The Overparameterized Probe Trap (when $d \gt N$)**:
   Conversely, if you train a linear probe in a few-shot regime (say, sampling only $N = 512$ probing sentences while the hidden dimension is $d = 4096 \gt N$), then $H \in \mathbb{R}^{512 \times 4096}$ becomes a **short-and-wide matrix** (just like Exercise `l06-ex1` above)! Barring extreme degeneracy among the activations, its $4096$ columns will almost surely span the entire space $\mathbb{R}^{512}$ ($\operatorname{Col}(H) = \mathbb{R}^{512}$)—which means that **even if $\mathbf{y}$ consists of pure random coin-flip noise labels, the equation $H\mathbf{w} = \mathbf{y}$ is 100% guaranteed to have an exact solution (in fact, infinitely many solutions, with the nullspace $\operatorname{Null}(H) \subseteq \mathbb{R}^{4096}$ having at least $4096 - 512 = 3584$ dimensions of freedom)**! Pure linear algebra thus delivers a stern warning to AI researchers: whenever feature dimension $d \ge N$, achieving 100% probe fit on the training set is completely vacuous; regularization and held-out test evaluation are mandatory!

---

### 2. The Nullspace $\operatorname{Null}(W)$ of a Linear Layer Weight Matrix $W$: "Silent Perturbations" and Superposition

Now consider any linear projection layer $\mathbf{z} = W \mathbf{h}$ inside a neural network, with weight matrix $W \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{out}} \times d_{\text{in}}}$ (for example, the down-projection layer $W_{\text{down}} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times 4d}$ in a Transformer FFN that compresses dimension $4d$ back down to $d$):

- Here the number of columns $4d$ vastly exceeds the number of rows $d$. By the definition of the nullspace, the input space $\mathbb{R}^{4d}$ must contain a massive **nullspace $\operatorname{Null}(W_{\text{down}}) \subseteq \mathbb{R}^{4d}$** (of dimension at least $3d$!).
- **Physical meaning of the nullspace—a "silent subspace" invisible to downstream layers**: For any nonzero perturbation vector $\boldsymbol{\delta} \in \operatorname{Null}(W)$, no matter how large its magnitude, passing through the layer yields:

$$
W(\mathbf{h} + \boldsymbol{\delta}) = W\mathbf{h} + W\boldsymbol{\delta} = W\mathbf{h} + \mathbf{0} = W\mathbf{h}
$$

  In other words, the linear layer $W$ is **completely blind** to every activation component lying inside $\operatorname{Null}(W)$! In **Superposition** and **Activation Steering** experiments, if a steering vector injected into a large language model happens to align with the nullspace of a downstream linear projection, the final output logits will remain completely unchanged even if intermediate activation norms shift dramatically!

<CollapsibleCode title="PyTorch Tensor Dimension Verification (Confirming d > N Probes Overfit Pure Noise & Null(W) Perturbations Are 100% Silent)">

```python
import torch

torch.manual_seed(0)

# 1. Verify the overparameterized linear probe trap: when N=64 samples < d=256 hidden dim, Col(H) = R^N fits pure random noise!
N, d = 64, 256
H = torch.randn(N, d)                  # (64, 256) short-and-wide activation matrix
y_random_noise = torch.randn(N, 1)     # Completely random meaningless labels y in R^64

# Solve H w = y: since y in Col(H) = R^64, the residual is strictly 0!
w_probe = torch.linalg.lstsq(H, y_random_noise).solution
residual = torch.norm(H @ w_probe - y_random_noise).item()
assert residual < 1e-5, f"Expected zero residual, got {residual}"

# 2. Verify silent nullspace perturbations in FFN down-projection W_down in R^(64 x 256): W(h + delta) == W h
W_down = torch.randn(64, 256)
# Use SVD to extract orthonormal directions in Null(W_down) (right singular vectors in rows 64..255 satisfy W v = 0)
_, _, Vh = torch.linalg.svd(W_down, full_matrices=True)
null_basis = Vh[64:, :].T              # (256, 192), spanning the 192-dimensional Null(W_down)

h = torch.randn(256, 1)
delta_in_null = null_basis @ torch.randn(192, 1) * 100.0  # Huge perturbation scaled by 100x!

out_clean = W_down @ h
out_perturbed = W_down @ (h + delta_in_null)
assert torch.allclose(out_clean, out_perturbed, atol=1e-4)
print("Verified: Col(H)=R^N fits arbitrary noise & Null(W_down) perturbations are 100% silent!")
```
