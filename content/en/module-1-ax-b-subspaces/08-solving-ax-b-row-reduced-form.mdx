In Lecture 07, we completely mastered the nullspace construction for the homogeneous equation $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$. Today, entering MIT 18.06 Lecture 08, we restore a nonzero target vector $\mathbf{b}$ to the right-hand side and bring the entire theory of linear systems together: **For an arbitrary $m\times n$ matrix, when does the inhomogeneous system $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ actually have a solution? Once a solution exists, what is the geometric structure of all solutions? And how does the rank $r$ of the matrix decisively govern the four fundamental regimes of "$0$ solutions, $1$ solution, or $\infty$ solutions"?**

---

## 1. Augmented Elimination in Action: Decoding Solvability from $[A \mid \mathbf{b}]$

Let us bring back our familiar $3\times 4$ matrix $A$ from Lecture 07, but this time augment it on the right with a general target vector $\mathbf{b} = (b_1, b_2, b_3)^T \in \mathbb{R}^3$:

$$
\begin{cases}
x_1 + 2x_2 + 2x_3 + 2x_4 = b_1 \\
2x_1 + 4x_2 + 6x_3 + 8x_4 = b_2 \\
3x_1 + 6x_2 + 8x_3 + 10x_4 = b_3
\end{cases}
$$

Applying the exact same elementary row operations as in Lecture 07 to the augmented matrix $\begin{bmatrix} A \mid \mathbf{b} \end{bmatrix}$ (first $R_2 - 2R_1,\; R_3 - 3R_1$, then $R_3 - R_2$) yields:

$$
\left[\begin{array}{cccc|c}
1 & 2 & 2 & 2 & b_1 \\
2 & 4 & 6 & 8 & b_2 \\
3 & 6 & 8 & 10 & b_3
\end{array}\right]
\longrightarrow
\left[\begin{array}{cccc|c}
1 & 2 & 2 & 2 & b_1 \\
0 & 0 & 2 & 4 & b_2 - 2b_1 \\
0 & 0 & 2 & 4 & b_3 - 3b_1
\end{array}\right]
\longrightarrow
\left[\begin{array}{cccc|c}
\mathbf{1} & 2 & 2 & 2 & b_1 \\
0 & 0 & \mathbf{2} & 4 & b_2 - 2b_1 \\
0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & \mathbf{b_3 - b_2 - b_1}
\end{array}\right]
$$

Look closely at the bottom row! On the left-hand side of the equation, we have four zeros: $0x_1 + 0x_2 + 0x_3 + 0x_4 = 0$; on the right-hand side sits the algebraic expression $b_3 - b_2 - b_1$!
- If the right-hand side vector you supply is $\mathbf{b} = (1, 5, 7)^T$, the bottom row becomes the contradictory equation **$0 = 7 - 5 - 1 = 1$**, and the system has no solution!
- The **necessary and sufficient condition for solvability** appears immediately: every algebraic expression on the right-hand side opposite a zero row must equal $0$! In this example:

$$
b_3 - b_2 - b_1 = 0 \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad b_3 = b_1 + b_2
$$

Why is the condition specifically $b_3 = b_1 + b_2$? Look back at the three rows of the original matrix $A$: **because Row 3 of $A$ was originally equal to "Row 1 + Row 2"!** Whenever the rows on the left-hand side satisfy a linear dependence relation, the components $b_1, b_2, b_3$ on the right-hand side must obey that exact same linear relation—this is the direct algebraic translation of our geometric criterion from Lecture 01, **$\mathbf{b} \in \operatorname{Col}(A)$**!

## 2. Constructing the Complete Solution: One Particular Solution $\mathbf{x}_p$ + the Entire Nullspace $\mathbf{x}_n$

Now let us pick a right-hand side vector that satisfies the solvability condition, say $\mathbf{b} = (1, 5, \mathbf{6})^T$ (which obeys $6 = 1 + 5$). After elimination, our echelon system simplifies to:

$$
\left[\begin{array}{cccc|c}
\mathbf{1} & 2 & 2 & 2 & 1 \\
0 & 0 & \mathbf{2} & 4 & 3 \\
0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0
\end{array}\right]
\quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad
\begin{cases}
x_1 + 2x_2 + 2x_3 + 2x_4 = 1 \\
\phantom{x_1 + 2x_2 + {}} 2x_3 + 4x_4 = 3
\end{cases}
$$

What is the fastest way to find **one specific particular solution** $\mathbf{x}_p$?
Professor Strang's standard shortcut is wonderfully simple: **Since the free variables ($x_2, x_4$) can be assigned any real numbers we please, let us set all free variables equal to $0$ ($x_2 = 0,\; x_4 = 0$)!**

Once the free variables are zeroed out, the free columns vanish from the calculation, leaving a clean, square, invertible upper-triangular back-substitution for the pivot variables:
- Equation 2: $2x_3 = 3 \implies x_3 = \frac{3}{2}$;
- Equation 1: $x_1 + 2\left(\frac{3}{2}\right) = 1 \implies x_1 = -2$.

With almost no effort, we have our particular solution:

$$
\mathbf{x}_p = \begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ \mathbf{0} \\ 3/2 \\ \mathbf{0} \end{bmatrix}
$$

Next, we simply take the general nullspace solution $\mathbf{x}_n \in \operatorname{Null}(A)$ that we already computed in Lecture 07 and **add it directly to $\mathbf{x}_p$** to obtain the **complete solution** to $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$:

$$
\mathbf{x}_{\text{complete}} = \mathbf{x}_p + \mathbf{x}_n
= \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ 0 \\ 3/2 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{x}_p \;(A\mathbf{x}_p = \mathbf{b})}
+ c_1 \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{s}_1}
+ c_2 \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 0 \\ -2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{s}_2}
\qquad (c_1, c_2 \in \mathbb{R})
$$

Let us verify why this captures every solution without missing a single one:
1. **Every sum $\mathbf{x}_p + \mathbf{x}_n$ is a solution**: $A(\mathbf{x}_p + \mathbf{x}_n) = A\mathbf{x}_p + A\mathbf{x}_n = \mathbf{b} + \mathbf{0} = \mathbf{b}$;
2. **No solution is left out**: Suppose $\mathbf{x}$ is any solution to $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$. Subtract $\mathbf{x}_p$ from it:

$$
A(\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}_p) = A\mathbf{x} - A\mathbf{x}_p = \mathbf{b} - \mathbf{b} = \mathbf{0}
\quad \Longrightarrow \quad
\mathbf{x}_n := \mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}_p \in \operatorname{Null}(A)
\quad \Longrightarrow \quad
\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{x}_p + \mathbf{x}_n
$$

Savor the four-dimensional geometry here: the nullspace $\operatorname{Null}(A)$ is a **two-dimensional subspace plane passing through the origin $\mathbf{0}$** inside $\mathbb{R}^4$; the complete solution set $\mathbf{x}_p + \operatorname{Null}(A)$ is obtained by picking up that two-dimensional plane and **shifting it parallel to itself** by the vector $\mathbf{x}_p$, forming a **two-dimensional affine flat (shifted subspace)**—as long as $\mathbf{b} \neq \mathbf{0}$, it does not pass through the origin, and therefore **it is NOT a subspace**!

## 3. The Four Fundamental Families: How Rank $r$ Governs the Fate of Any $m\times n$ Matrix

Now let us step back and survey all possible real matrices $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$. A matrix has $m$ rows, $n$ columns, and $r = \operatorname{rank}(A)$ pivots. Because each row contains at most one pivot and each column contains at most one pivot, we always have $r \le m$ and $r \le n$. Depending on **whether $r$ reaches its upper bound $m$ or $n$**, every matrix in the universe falls cleanly into one of **four—and only four—rank regimes**:

<DefinitionCard
  label="Definition 8.1"
  title="Full Column Rank & Full Row Rank"
>
Let the matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ have rank $r = \operatorname{rank}(A)$:

1. **Full Column Rank**: $r = n$ (which necessarily requires $n \le m$), meaning every column is a pivot column and there are **no free variables**.
2. **Full Row Rank**: $r = m$ (which necessarily requires $m \le n$), meaning every row contains a pivot and $R = \operatorname{rref}(A)$ has **no zero rows**.
</DefinitionCard>

<TheoremCard
  label="Theorem 8.1"
  title="Complete Solution Structure & The Four Rank Regimes"
>
Let the real matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ have rank $r = \operatorname{rank}(A)$:

1. **Dual Equivalence Criterion for Solvability**: The system $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ is solvable $\iff$ **geometrically** $\mathbf{b} \in \operatorname{Col}(A)$ $\iff$ **algebraically** whenever augmented elimination reduces a row of $A$ to all zeros, the corresponding entry of $\mathbf{b}$ simultaneously reduces to $0$.
2. **Affine Complete Solution Decomposition**: Once a particular solution $\mathbf{x}_p$ satisfying $A\mathbf{x}_p = \mathbf{b}$ exists, the complete solution set of $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ is the shifted nullspace $\mathbf{x}_p + \operatorname{Null}(A) = \{\mathbf{x}_p + \mathbf{x}_n : \mathbf{x}_n \in \operatorname{Null}(A)\}$.
3. **Number of Solutions and Canonical Form of $R = \operatorname{rref}(A)$ Across the Four Rank Regimes** (up to column permutation):
   - **① Full Column & Row Rank (Invertible Square Matrix) $r = m = n$**: $R = I_n$, $\operatorname{Null}(A) = \{\mathbf{0}\}$ and $\operatorname{Col}(A) = \mathbb{R}^n$ $\implies$ **always a unique solution ($1$ solution)** for every $\mathbf{b}$.
   - **② Full Column Rank (Tall & Thin Matrix) $r = n \lt m$**: $R = \begin{bmatrix} I_n \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$, no free variables ($\operatorname{Null}(A) = \{\mathbf{0}\}$), but $m - n \gt 0$ zero-row constraints at the bottom $\implies$ **$0$ solutions or $1$ unique solution** depending on $\mathbf{b}$ (whenever a solution exists, it is guaranteed to be unique!).
   - **③ Full Row Rank (Short & Wide Matrix) $r = m \lt n$**: After column permutation, $R$ has the form $\begin{bmatrix} I_m & F \end{bmatrix}$, with no zero rows ($\operatorname{Col}(A) = \mathbb{R}^m$) and $n - m \gt 0$ free variables $\implies$ **always infinitely many solutions ($\infty$ solutions)** for **every** $\mathbf{b} \in \mathbb{R}^m$!
   - **④ Rank Deficient (Neither Full Row nor Full Column Rank) $r \lt m$ and $r \lt n$**: After column permutation, $R$ has the form $\begin{bmatrix} I_r & F \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$, with both $m - r \gt 0$ zero-row constraints and $n - r \gt 0$ free variables $\implies$ **$0$ solutions or infinitely many solutions (never a unique solution!)**.
</TheoremCard>

<ProofCollapse title="Rigorous Proof: Why Full Row Rank r = m Guarantees a Solution for Every b ∈ ℝᵐ">
Suppose $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ satisfies $r = m$, and let $E \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times m}$ denote the invertible product of elementary row operation matrices that reduces $A$ to $R = \operatorname{rref}(A)$ (so $EA = R$).
Because $r = m$, all $m$ rows of $R$ contain a pivot equal to $1$, so **there are no zero rows at all**! Let the column indices of the $m$ pivots be $j_1 \lt j_2 \lt \cdots \lt j_m$; then these $m$ pivot columns of $R$ are precisely the standard basis vectors $\mathbf{e}_1, \dots, \mathbf{e}_m$ of $\mathbb{R}^m$.
Now take any $\mathbf{b} \in \mathbb{R}^m$ and define $\mathbf{d} := E\mathbf{b} = (d_1, \dots, d_m)^T \in \mathbb{R}^m$. Construct the vector $\mathbf{x}_p \in \mathbb{R}^n$ directly by setting its $j_k$-th pivot variable entry to $d_k$ ($k = 1, \dots, m$) and all remaining $n - m$ free variable entries to $0$. Then:

$$
R \mathbf{x}_p = \sum_{k=1}^{m} d_k \mathbf{e}_k = \mathbf{d} = E\mathbf{b}
$$

Since $E$ is invertible, multiplying both sides on the left by $E^{-1}$ immediately gives $A\mathbf{x}_p = E^{-1}(R\mathbf{x}_p) = E^{-1}(E\mathbf{b}) = \mathbf{b}$! Thus a solution exists for every $\mathbf{b} \in \mathbb{R}^m$ (and when $m \lt n$, because there are $n - m \ge 1$ linearly independent special solutions in the nullspace, there are necessarily infinitely many solutions).
</ProofCollapse>

<PitfallAlert title="Common Pitfalls & Transcript Errata">
1. **Pitfall 1: "The particular solution $\mathbf{x}_p$ is unique—only the one obtained by setting all free variables to $0$ qualifies as a particular solution."**
   **False!** Setting all free variables to $0$ is merely the **most convenient hand-calculation convention** for finding one particular solution, not the only one! In fact, **any point** $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_p = \mathbf{x}_p + \mathbf{s}_1$ lying on the affine solution flat $\mathbf{x}_p + \operatorname{Null}(A)$ satisfies $A\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_p = \mathbf{b}$ and is completely qualified to serve as the particular solution: $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_p + \operatorname{Null}(A) = \mathbf{x}_p + \operatorname{Null}(A)$ describes the exact same shifted plane!
2. **Pitfall 2: Multiplying the particular solution $\mathbf{x}_p$ by an arbitrary constant and writing $c_0 \mathbf{x}_p + c_1 \mathbf{s}_1 + c_2 \mathbf{s}_2$! (A fatal exam mistake!)**
   **Never multiply $\mathbf{x}_p$ by a free parameter $c_0$!** As soon as you multiply by $c_0 \neq 1$, the left-hand side produces $A(c_0\mathbf{x}_p + \mathbf{x}_n) = c_0 \mathbf{b} \neq \mathbf{b}$! The coefficient in front of $\mathbf{x}_p$ must be locked strictly at **$1$**; arbitrary constants $c_1, c_2 \in \mathbb{R}$ may only multiply the nullspace special solutions $\mathbf{s}_k$!
3. **Pitfall 3: Concluding from "more equations $m$ than unknowns $n$ (an overdetermined system)" that there cannot be infinitely many solutions.**
   The behavior of solutions **depends strictly on the rank $r$, never on the outer dimensions $m, n$ alone**! Even for a tall $100 \times 3$ matrix, if its rank is only $r = 2 \lt 3$ (placing it in Regime ④, Rank Deficient), then for any $\mathbf{b}$ lying in its two-dimensional column space plane, it still has **infinitely many solutions**! Only when you strictly know **full column rank $r = n$** can you rule out infinitely many solutions.
</PitfallAlert>

>
<SolutionIntuition>

1. Inspect the three columns: Column 2, $(3, 6, -3)^T$, is exact $3$ times Column 1, while Column 3 is independent. Thus this $3\times 3$ matrix has rank only $r = 2 \lt 3$, placing it in Regime ④: **Rank Deficient** (either $0$ solutions or infinitely many solutions)! Elimination reveals that the right-hand side of the zero row is $3b_1 - b_2 + b_3 = 0$.
2. For $\mathbf{b} = (2, 8, 2)^T$ (checking $6 - 8 + 2 = 0$, it passes!), the free variable is $x_2$:
   - Setting $x_2 = 0$, two quick steps of back-substitution give $\mathbf{x}_p = (0, 0, 2)^T$ (which you can spot at a glance: $\mathbf{b} = (2,8,2)^T$ is simply $2$ times Column 3, $(1,4,1)^T$!);
   - Because Column 2 equals $3$ times Column 1, the nullspace special solution is immediately $\mathbf{s}_1 = (-3, 1, 0)^T$! Add them together and you are done!

</SolutionIntuition>
<SolutionRigorous>

1. Perform elementary row operations on the augmented matrix:

$$
\left[\begin{array}{ccc|c}
1 & 3 & 1 & b_1 \\
2 & 6 & 4 & b_2 \\
-1 & -3 & 1 & b_3
\end{array}\right]
\xrightarrow{\substack{R_2 - 2R_1 \\ R_3 + R_1}}
\left[\begin{array}{ccc|c}
1 & 3 & 1 & b_1 \\
0 & 0 & 2 & b_2 - 2b_1 \\
0 & 0 & 2 & b_3 + b_1
\end{array}\right]
\xrightarrow{R_3 - R_2}
\left[\begin{array}{ccc|c}
\mathbf{1} & 3 & 1 & b_1 \\
0 & 0 & \mathbf{2} & b_2 - 2b_1 \\
0 & 0 & 0 & 3b_1 - b_2 + b_3
\end{array}\right]
$$

   - The necessary and sufficient condition for solvability is **$3b_1 - b_2 + b_3 = 0$**;
   - Since $r = 2 \lt m = 3$ and $r = 2 \lt n = 3$, $A$ belongs to **Regime ④ (Rank Deficient)**.

2. Substituting $\mathbf{b} = (2, 8, 2)^T$ (which satisfies $3(2)-8+2=0$), the pivot variables are $x_1, x_3$ and the free variable is $x_2$:
   - Set $x_2 = 0$: $2x_3 = 8 - 2(2) = 4 \implies x_3 = 2$; $x_1 + 2 = 2 \implies x_1 = 0$, giving $\mathbf{x}_p = (0, 0, 2)^T$.
   - Reducing to $R = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$ and setting $x_2 = 1$ yields the nullspace special solution $\mathbf{s}_1 = (-3, 1, 0)^T$.
   - The complete solution is:

$$
\mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} + c \begin{bmatrix} -3 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \quad (c \in \mathbb{R})
$$

</SolutionRigorous>

Then $\operatorname{rank}(A) = 1 \lt n = 3$, so there are $3 - 1 = 2$ free variables ($x_2, x_3$), and the complete solution is

$$
\mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} + c_1 \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} + c_2 \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \quad (c_1, c_2 \in \mathbb{R})
$$

which has infinitely many solutions. Hence the statement is false.

<DualSolution
  triggerLabel="Show Full Dual Solution (Intuition + Rigorous Proof)"
  intuitionLabel="Geometric Intuition"
  rigorousLabel="Rigorous Proof / Construction"
>
<SolutionIntuition>

The necessary and sufficient condition for a unique solution is **zero free variables ($r = n$)**. A short, wide matrix inherently has too few rows ($m \lt n$): even if every single row produces a pivot, the total number of pivots $r$ is at most $m$ and can never reach $n$! Because at least one free variable is always left roaming the nullspace, either $\mathbf{b} \notin \operatorname{Col}(A)$ and there are $0$ solutions, or you find one foothold $\mathbf{x}_p$ and adding the nullspace line immediately turns it into $\infty$ solutions!

</SolutionIntuition>
<SolutionRigorous>

For any $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ with $m \lt n$, its rank satisfies $r = \operatorname{rank}(A) \le m \lt n$.
By Theorem 7.1(2), the number of free variables is $n - r \ge 1$, so there exists a nonzero special solution $\mathbf{s}_1 \in \operatorname{Null}(A) \setminus \{\mathbf{0}\}$.
For any $\mathbf{b} \in \mathbb{R}^m$:
- If $\mathbf{b} \notin \operatorname{Col}(A)$, the number of solutions is $0$;
- If $\mathbf{b} \in \operatorname{Col}(A)$, let $\mathbf{x}_p$ be a particular solution; then by Theorem 8.1(2), for every $c \in \mathbb{R}$, $\mathbf{x}_p + c\mathbf{s}_1$ gives a distinct solution (infinitely many solutions).

Hence under no circumstances can there be a unique solution, and the statement is always true.

</SolutionRigorous>
</DualSolution>

<SolutionIntuition>

1. Adding two inhomogeneous solutions doubles the right-hand side to $2\mathbf{b}$, so $\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}$ is certainly not a solution! However, whenever the two weights add up to $1$ (an **affine combination** $t\mathbf{u} + (1-t)\mathbf{v}$, which geometrically traces the entire line passing through $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$!), the right-hand side evaluates to $t\mathbf{b} + (1-t)\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{b}$, so every point on that line is a solution!
2. Subtracting the two solutions, $\mathbf{w} = \mathbf{u} - \mathbf{v} = (2, -2, 2)^T$, cancels $\mathbf{b}$ on the right-hand side to zero, handing us the nonzero nullspace vector $(1, -1, 1)^T$ on a silver platter! Since $A$ is neither the zero matrix (it produces $\mathbf{b} \neq \mathbf{0}$, so $r \ge 1$) nor invertible ($r \le 2$), the rank $r$ can only be **$1$ or $2$**!
3. Want to hit $3\mathbf{b}$? Scale the particular solution by $3$ to get $3\mathbf{u} = (6, 3, 3)^T$, then add any multiple of the nullspace direction $c(1, -1, 1)^T$ we just found!

</SolutionIntuition>
<SolutionRigorous>

1. By linearity, $A(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}) = A\mathbf{u} + A\mathbf{v} = 2\mathbf{b}$. Since $\mathbf{b} \neq \mathbf{0}$, we have $2\mathbf{b} \neq \mathbf{b}$, so $\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}$ is **not** a solution. On the other hand, for any $t \in \mathbb{R}$:

$$
A\big(t\mathbf{u} + (1-t)\mathbf{v}\big) = t A\mathbf{u} + (1-t) A\mathbf{v} = t\mathbf{b} + (1-t)\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{b}
$$

   Thus every vector on the affine line $\{t\mathbf{u} + (1-t)\mathbf{v} : t \in \mathbb{R}\}$ is a solution.

2. Subtracting the two equations yields $A(\mathbf{u} - \mathbf{v}) = \mathbf{b} - \mathbf{b} = \mathbf{0}$, so the nonzero vector

$$
\mathbf{s} = \mathbf{u} - \mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ -2 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} \in \operatorname{Null}(A) \setminus \{\mathbf{0}\}
$$

   - Because $\operatorname{Null}(A) \neq \{\mathbf{0}\}$, we have $n - r = 3 - r \ge 1 \implies r \le 2$;
   - Because there exists $\mathbf{u}$ with $A\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{b} \neq \mathbf{0}$, we know $A \neq 0 \implies r \ge 1$.
   Hence $r = \operatorname{rank}(A) \in \{\mathbf{1, 2}\}$ (both values are possible).

3. Since $A(3\mathbf{u}) = 3(A\mathbf{u}) = 3\mathbf{b}$ and $A\mathbf{s} = \mathbf{0}$, for every $c \in \mathbb{R}$ the vectors

$$
\mathbf{x}(c) = 3\mathbf{u} + c\left(\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{s}\right) = \begin{bmatrix} 6 \\ 3 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} + c \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}
$$

   all satisfy $A\mathbf{x}(c) = 3\mathbf{b}$.

</SolutionRigorous>
</DualSolution>

<SolutionRigorous>

1. **Determining Dimensions and Rank**:
   - Since $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^3$, $A$ must have **$n = 3$ columns**;
   - By Theorem 8.1(2), the solution set is $\mathbf{x}_p + \operatorname{Null}(A)$, so $\operatorname{Null}(A) = \operatorname{span}\{(1, 1, 1)^T\}$ is spanned by $1$ basis vector, meaning $n - r = 1 \implies r = \operatorname{rank}(A) = \mathbf{2}$;
   - From $r \le m$, the number of rows satisfies $m \ge \mathbf{2}$, so at least $2$ rows are required.

2. **Explicit Construction of a $2\times 3$ System** (taking the RREF directly):

$$
A = \begin{bmatrix}
1 & 0 & -1 \\
0 & 1 & -1
\end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{2\times 3}, \qquad
\mathbf{b} = A \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^2

<FrontierAIReading
  id="AI-03"
  title="Null-Space Knowledge Editing in LLMs (AlphaEdit): Updating Facts with Zero Interference via x_p + x_n"
  paperRef="Fang et al. (ICLR 2025 Oral) AlphaEdit: Null-Space Constrained Knowledge Editing for Language Models · Meng et al. (NeurIPS 2022) ROME"
>

Imagine a pressing real-world engineering challenge: a Large Language Model (LLM) trained at a cost of tens of millions of dollars stores a vast repository of accurate world knowledge (such as "The capital of France is Paris" or "The speed of light is approximately $3\times 10^8\text{ m/s}$"), but as time passes, certain facts change (for example, "a country inaugurates a new prime minister" or "an open-source library releases a new major version"). If you fine-tune the weights directly with gradient descent, updating just a few dozen new facts often causes catastrophic forgetting or cross-talk corruption of the model's existing knowledge!

**AlphaEdit**, winner of an **ICLR 2025 Oral (top paper award)**, presents a pure linear-algebraic solution that will make every reader of MIT 18.06 Lecture 08 cheer—**its entire core mechanism is an exact realization of Lecture 08's inhomogeneous complete solution decomposition $\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{x}_p + \mathbf{x}_n$ inside linear associative memory layers!**

### 1. Viewing a Transformer FFN Layer as a Linear Key-Value Store $W K_0 = V_0$

In a Transformer feed-forward network (FFN, consisting of two linear projections sandwiching a nonlinear activation), the second down-projection weight matrix $W \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{out}} \times d_{\text{mid}}}$ acts as a **Linear Associative Memory**:
- Suppose the intermediate activation key vectors corresponding to **$N_0$ existing facts we wish to strictly preserve** are stacked into the matrix $K_0 = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{k}_1 & \cdots & \mathbf{k}_{N_0} \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{mid}} \times N_0}$ (in practical LLMs, the effective rank of preserved activations satisfies $r_0 \lt d_{\text{mid}}$, meaning $K_0^T$ is a **short, wide matrix with plenty of free variables**!), and the current correct outputs are $V_0 = W K_0$;
- Now we want to write one (or a batch of) **new facts**: when the new key matrix $K_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{mid}} \times N_1}$ is fed in, we want the updated weights $W' = W + \Delta W$ to output the new target values $V_1^*$!

---

### 2. Isomorphism Between the Zero-Interference Constraint $\Delta W K_0 = 0$ and $\mathbf{x}_p + \mathbf{x}_n$

Let us translate "learn the new facts while causing zero interference to old facts" into simultaneous matrix equations for the weight update $\Delta W$ (taking the transpose makes each column identical to our standard Lecture 08 system $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$!):

1. **Hard Zero-Interference Constraint on Old Memory (Homogeneous Nullspace Part)**:

$$
(W + \Delta W) K_0 = W K_0 \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad \Delta W \, K_0 = 0 \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad K_0^T (\Delta W)^T = 0
$$

   Look at the rightmost equation: **it requires every row of the weight update $\Delta W$ (every column of its transpose) to lie strictly inside the nullspace $\operatorname{Null}(K_0^T)$ of the preserved key matrix!** As long as this holds, no matter how far $\Delta W$ moves inside that nullspace, whenever an old query $\mathbf{k}_j \in \operatorname{Col}(K_0)$ enters the layer, the perturbation term $\Delta W \mathbf{k}_j = \mathbf{0}$ vanishes identically—preserving prior memories with **mathematically zero error**!

2. **Hitting the New Fact Targets Exactly (Inhomogeneous Particular Solution Part)**:
   Simultaneously, within the degrees of freedom permitted by $\operatorname{Null}(K_0^T)$, we require the new keys to match the inhomogeneous target residual $R_1 := V_1^* - W K_1$:

$$
(W + \Delta W) K_1 = V_1^* \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad \Delta W \, K_1 = R_1
$$

   How do we satisfy both conditions at once? Let $P_{\text{null}} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{mid}} \times d_{\text{mid}}}$ be the orthogonal projection matrix onto the left nullspace $\operatorname{Null}(K_0^T)$ of the preserved keys (so $P_{\text{null}} K_0 = 0$). Parameterize the update as $\Delta W = \tilde{\Delta} P_{\text{null}}$:
   - On one hand, multiplying on the right by $K_0$ automatically satisfies $\Delta W K_0 = \tilde{\Delta}(P_{\text{null}} K_0) = 0$ (locked inside the nullspace!);
   - On the other hand, the new-fact equation reduces to solving a short, wide full-row-rank system (**Regime ③, $r = m \lt n$**, from this lecture!) for the projected new keys $K_{1,\perp} := P_{\text{null}} K_1$:

$$
\tilde{\Delta} (P_{\text{null}} K_1) = R_1
$$

   As long as the new fact keys $K_1$ do not lie entirely inside the subspace spanned by the old keys ($P_{\text{null}} K_1 \neq 0$), this wide system has infinitely many exact solutions—writing the new facts $(W + \Delta W)K_1 = V_1^*$ with pinpoint accuracy while leaving every preserved fact $(W + \Delta W)K_0 = WK_0$ untouched to the last decimal place!

<CollapsibleCode title="PyTorch Tensor Dimension Verification (20-Line AlphaEdit Null-Space Knowledge Editing Prototype)">

```python
import torch

torch.manual_seed(1806)

d_mid, d_out = 128, 64
N_old, N_new = 80, 4   # Preserve 80 old facts (r_0 = 80 < 128, leaving 48 nullspace degrees of freedom!), write 4 new facts

W = torch.randn(d_out, d_mid)
K_0 = torch.randn(d_mid, N_old)        # Activation keys of old facts to preserve
V_0_orig = W @ K_0                     # Original outputs on old facts

K_1 = torch.randn(d_mid, N_new)        # Activation keys of new facts to edit
V_1_target = torch.randn(d_out, N_new) # Desired target values for new facts

# 1. Extract an orthonormal basis (128 x 48) of Null(K_0^T) and build the nullspace projector P_null: P_null @ K_0 == 0
U, S, _ = torch.linalg.svd(K_0, full_matrices=True)
U_null = U[:, N_old:]                  # (128, 48) orthonormal basis of the nullspace
P_null = U_null @ U_null.T             # (128, 128) nullspace projection operator

# 2. Solve for the new-fact particular solution inside the nullspace: Delta_W @ K_1 = V_1_target - W @ K_1, with rows locked in Null(K_0^T)
R_1 = V_1_target - W @ K_1             # Target residual b for new facts
K_1_proj = P_null @ K_1                # New keys projected onto the nullspace (128, 4)
Delta_W = R_1 @ torch.linalg.pinv(K_1_proj) @ P_null  # Exact update constrained to the nullspace

W_edited = W + Delta_W

# 3. Dual verification: 100% exact hit on new facts, 0.000000 interference on all 80 old facts!
new_err = torch.norm(W_edited @ K_1 - V_1_target).item()
old_interference = torch.norm(W_edited @ K_0 - V_0_orig).item()
assert new_err < 1e-4 and old_interference < 1e-5
print(f"New fact error: {new_err:.2e} | Old memory interference: {old_interference:.2e}")
```
