## Beyond Euclidean Column Vectors: Matrices Are "Vectors" Too!

Across our first ten lectures, every vector we met looked like a vertical column of numbers $\mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{R}^n$. Yet back in Lecture 05, when we defined a vector space, we planted a seed of tremendous abstract power: **as long as a collection of objects can be added to one another, multiplied by real scalars, and obeys the eight fundamental arithmetic axioms, it is a genuine Real Vector Space**—whether its elements are column arrays, matrices, or smooth functions!

Gilbert Strang opens Lecture 11 by taking this decisive leap: consider the **Matrix Space** of all $3 \times 3$ real matrices, denoted $M = \mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}$. Even though two $3 \times 3$ matrices can be multiplied together, when we study $M$ as a vector space we **completely ignore matrix multiplication for now** and care only about matrix addition $A + B$ and scalar multiplication $cA$!

<DefinitionCard id="def-11-1" title="Matrix Space M = ℝ³ˣ³ and Its Three Classic Subspaces">
Let $M = \mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}$ be the vector space of all $3 \times 3$ real matrices under matrix addition and real scalar multiplication. Using the matrices $E_{ij}$ (which have a $1$ in the $(i,j)$ entry and $0$ in the other $8$ positions) as the standard basis—analogous to the standard coordinate vectors of $\mathbb{R}^9$—we clearly have $\dim(M) = 9$. Inside $M$, the three most important subspaces are:
1. **Subspace of Symmetric Matrices** $S = \{ A \in M : A^T = A \}$:
   Sums and scalar multiples of symmetric matrices remain symmetric. The $3$ main diagonal entries can be chosen freely, and once the $3$ entries above the diagonal are chosen, the $3$ entries below the diagonal are locked by mirror symmetry. Hence **$\dim(S) = 6$**! Its standard basis consists of the $3$ diagonal matrices $E_{11}, E_{22}, E_{33}$ together with the $3$ symmetric pair matrices $E_{12}+E_{21},\; E_{13}+E_{31},\; E_{23}+E_{32}$.
2. **Subspace of Upper-Triangular Matrices** $U = \{ A \in M : a_{ij} = 0 \; (\forall i \gt j) \}$:
   Every entry strictly below the main diagonal is $0$, leaving $3 + 2 + 1 = 6$ free entries on and above the main diagonal. Thus **$\dim(U) = 6$**! Its standard basis is $\{ E_{ij} : 1 \le i \le j \le 3 \}$.
3. **Subspace of Diagonal Matrices** $D = S \cap U$:
   Being both symmetric (upper-right equals lower-left) and upper-triangular (lower-left is all $0$) forces the upper-right entries to be all $0$ as well! Only the $3$ main diagonal entries remain free, so the intersection $D = S \cap U$ is also a subspace with **$\dim(S \cap U) = 3$**, and its basis is $\{E_{11}, E_{22}, E_{33}\}$.
</DefinitionCard>

### Intersection and Sum of Subspaces: Grassmann's Dimension Formula

The union $S\cup U$ is a subspace exactly when one of the two subspaces contains the other. If neither contains the other, take $\mathbf{s}\in S\setminus U$ and $\mathbf{u}\in U\setminus S$. If $\mathbf{s}+\mathbf{u}$ belonged to $S$, subtracting $\mathbf{s}$ would place $\mathbf{u}$ in $S$. Subtracting $\mathbf{u}$ gives the symmetric argument for $U$. Here $E_{12}+E_{21}\in S\setminus U$ and $E_{12}\in U\setminus S$, but their sum $2E_{12}+E_{21}$ belongs to neither. The smallest subspace containing both $S$ and $U$ is

$$
S + U \triangleq \{ A_1 + A_2 \mid A_1 \in S,\; A_2 \in U \}
$$

How large is $S + U$ inside $M = \mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}$? Given any arbitrary $3 \times 3$ matrix $A$, you can always copy its strictly lower-triangular part across the diagonal into the upper-right to form a symmetric matrix $A_1 \in S$, and then add an upper-triangular adjustment matrix $A_2 = A - A_1 \in U$ to restore the main diagonal and upper-right entries back to those of $A$! Therefore, **every $3 \times 3$ matrix can be written as a symmetric matrix plus an upper-triangular matrix**, which means $S + U = M$ and $\dim(S + U) = 9$!

Look closely at those four striking numbers: $\dim(S) = 6$, $\dim(U) = 6$, $\dim(S \cap U) = 3$, and $\dim(S + U) = 9$. They satisfy $6 + 6 = 3 + 9$! This is no coincidence—it is **Grassmann's Dimension Formula**, which holds in every finite-dimensional vector space.

<TheoremCard id="thm-11-1" title="Grassmann's Dimension Formula for Intersection and Sum of Subspaces">
Let $V$ be a finite-dimensional vector space over $\mathbb{R}$, and let $S$ and $U$ be any two subspaces of $V$. Then both the intersection $S \cap U$ and the sum space $S + U = \{ \mathbf{s} + \mathbf{u} : \mathbf{s} \in S, \mathbf{u} \in U \}$ are subspaces of $V$, and their dimensions always satisfy:

$$
\dim(S) + \dim(U) = \dim(S \cap U) + \dim(S + U)
$$

In particular, $\dim(S + U) = \dim(S) + \dim(U)$ if and only if $S \cap U = \{\mathbf{0}\}$. In that case, the sum space is called a **Direct Sum**, written $S \oplus U$, and every vector in $S \oplus U$ decomposes **uniquely** as $\mathbf{s} + \mathbf{u}$.
</TheoremCard>

<ProofCollapse title="Rigorous Proof via Basis Extension: Why Is the Intersection Dimension Counted Twice?">
Let $\dim(S \cap U) = k$, $\dim(S) = p$, and $\dim(U) = q$. Choose a basis $\{\mathbf{w}_1, \dots, \mathbf{w}_k\}$ for the intersection $S \cap U$:
1. Because $\{\mathbf{w}_1, \dots, \mathbf{w}_k\} \subseteq S$ is linearly independent, we can extend it to a basis of $S$: $\{\mathbf{w}_1, \dots, \mathbf{w}_k, \mathbf{s}_1, \dots, \mathbf{s}_{p-k}\}$. Similarly, we can extend it to a basis of $U$: $\{\mathbf{w}_1, \dots, \mathbf{w}_k, \mathbf{u}_1, \dots, \mathbf{u}_{q-k}\}$.
2. Consider the combined collection $\mathcal{B} = \{\mathbf{w}_1, \dots, \mathbf{w}_k, \mathbf{s}_1, \dots, \mathbf{s}_{p-k}, \mathbf{u}_1, \dots, \mathbf{u}_{q-k}\}$, which contains $k + (p-k) + (q-k) = p + q - k$ vectors:
   - **Spanning**: For any $\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{s} + \mathbf{u} \in S + U$, expanding $\mathbf{s}$ in the basis of $S$ and $\mathbf{u}$ in the basis of $U$ immediately shows that $\mathcal{B}$ spans $S + U$.
   - **Linear Independence**: Suppose $\sum_{i=1}^k \alpha_i \mathbf{w}_i + \sum_{j=1}^{p-k} \beta_j \mathbf{s}_j + \sum_{\ell=1}^{q-k} \gamma_\ell \mathbf{u}_\ell = \mathbf{0}$. Move the $\mathbf{u}_\ell$ terms to the right-hand side:

     $$
     \mathbf{z} \triangleq \sum_{i=1}^k \alpha_i \mathbf{w}_i + \sum_{j=1}^{p-k} \beta_j \mathbf{s}_j = -\sum_{\ell=1}^{q-k} \gamma_\ell \mathbf{u}_\ell
     $$

     The left-hand side shows $\mathbf{z} \in S$, while the right-hand side shows $\mathbf{z} \in U$, so $\mathbf{z} \in S \cap U$! Therefore, the right-hand vector $-\sum \gamma_\ell \mathbf{u}_\ell$ can be written as a linear combination of the intersection basis $\{\mathbf{w}_1,\dots,\mathbf{w}_k\}$: there exist scalars $\delta_i$ such that $\sum_{i=1}^k \delta_i \mathbf{w}_i + \sum_{\ell=1}^{q-k} \gamma_\ell \mathbf{u}_\ell = \mathbf{0}$. Since $\{\mathbf{w}_i, \mathbf{u}_\ell\}$ is a basis for $U$ (and hence linearly independent), every $\gamma_\ell = 0$! Substituting $\gamma_\ell = 0$ back into the original equation gives $\sum_{i=1}^k \alpha_i \mathbf{w}_i + \sum_{j=1}^{p-k} \beta_j \mathbf{s}_j = \mathbf{0}$, and linear independence of the basis of $S$ forces all $\alpha_i = 0$ and $\beta_j = 0$!
3. Thus $\mathcal{B}$ is a basis for $S + U$, giving $\dim(S + U) = p + q - k = \dim(S) + \dim(U) - \dim(S \cap U)$. Q.E.D.
</ProofCollapse>

### Crossing the Boundary: The "Nullspace" of a Differential Equation Has a Basis and Dimension Too!

The reach of linear algebra goes far beyond matrices. Consider the second-order homogeneous linear ordinary differential equation (ODE):

$$
\frac{d^2 y}{dx^2} + y = 0
$$

On an open interval $I\subseteq\mathbb{R}$ containing $0$, consider $\mathcal{L}:C^2(I,\mathbb{R})\to C(I,\mathbb{R})$. The linear differential operator $\mathcal{L}(y) = y'' + y$ satisfies $\mathcal{L}(c_1 y_1 + c_2 y_2) = c_1 \mathcal{L}(y_1) + c_2 \mathcal{L}(y_2)$. Consequently, **the set of all solutions to this ODE is literally the Nullspace of the linear operator $\mathcal{L}$**! What is a basis for this solution space? None other than your familiar functions $\cos x$ and $\sin x$ (or $e^{ix}$ and $e^{-ix}$ over the complex numbers)! The general solution is written as:

$$
y(x) = c_1 \cos x + c_2 \sin x
$$

The initial conditions are $y(0)=c_1$ and $y'(0)=c_2$. Existence and uniqueness for this ODE therefore show that $\{\cos x,\sin x\}$ is a basis and the real solution space has dimension $2$. More generally, a homogeneous second-order linear ODE in normal form with continuous coefficients on an interval has a two-dimensional solution space there.

## Rank-1 Matrices: The "Atomic Building Blocks" of All Linear Transformations

Returning to the world of $m \times n$ matrices, what is the simplest possible nonzero matrix? The answer is a **Rank-1 Matrix**—a matrix with a single pivot $r = 1$. For instance, examine this $2 \times 3$ matrix:

$$
A_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 4 & 5 \\ 2 & 8 & 10 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 4 & 5 \end{bmatrix} = \mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^T
$$

Look closely at $A_1$: Row 2 is $2$ times Row 1, so its row space is the $1$-dimensional line $\operatorname{span}\{(1,4,5)^T\}$; every column is a multiple of $(1,2)^T$, so its column space is also the $1$-dimensional line $\operatorname{span}\{(1,2)^T\}$! Even better, $A_1$ factors cleanly as **one column times one row (an outer product $\mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^T$)**! This is not a special coincidence—it is the exact algebraic signature of every rank-1 matrix, and the atomic building block from which every rank-$r$ matrix is constructed!

<TheoremCard id="thm-11-2" title="Rank-1 Outer Product Characterization, Rank-r Atomic Decomposition & Subadditivity of Rank">
Let $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$.
1. **Outer Product Characterization of Rank-1 Matrices**: $\operatorname{rank}(A) = 1$ **if and only if** there exist nonzero column vectors $\mathbf{u} \in \mathbb{R}^m \setminus \{\mathbf{0}\}$ and $\mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{R}^n \setminus \{\mathbf{0}\}$ such that $A = \mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^T$. In this case, $\operatorname{Col}(A) = \operatorname{span}\{\mathbf{u}\}$, $\operatorname{Row}(A) = \operatorname{span}\{\mathbf{v}\}$, and $\operatorname{Null}(A) = \{\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n : \mathbf{v}^T\mathbf{x} = 0\}$ (the $(n-1)$-dimensional hyperplane perpendicular to $\mathbf{v}$).
2. **Atomic Rank-$r$ Decomposition**: For any nonzero matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$, $\operatorname{rank}(A) = r$ **if and only if** $r$ is the **smallest positive integer** such that $A$ can be expressed as a sum of $r$ rank-1 matrices; that is, there exist a full-column-rank matrix $C \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times r}$ and a full-row-rank matrix $F \in \mathbb{R}^{r\times n}$ such that:

   $$
   A = C F = \sum_{k=1}^{r} \mathbf{u}_k \mathbf{v}_k^T \qquad (\text{where } \mathbf{u}_k \text{ is the } k\text{-th column of } C \text{ and } \mathbf{v}_k^T \text{ is the } k\text{-th row of } F)
   $$

3. **Subadditivity of Matrix Rank**: For any two matrices $A, B \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ of the same size:

   $$
   |\operatorname{rank}(A) - \operatorname{rank}(B)| \le \operatorname{rank}(A + B) \le \operatorname{rank}(A) + \operatorname{rank}(B)
   $$
</TheoremCard>

<ProofCollapse title="Rigorous Proof: From Pivot Column Factorization A = CR₀ to Rank Subadditivity">
1. **Rank-1 Characterization**: If $A = \mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^T$ with $\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\neq\mathbf{0}$, then since $\mathbf{v}$ has at least one nonzero entry $v_j \neq 0$, the $j$-th column $v_j \mathbf{u} \neq \mathbf{0}$, while every $k$-th column is $v_k \mathbf{u} \in \operatorname{span}\{\mathbf{u}\}$. Thus $\operatorname{Col}(A) = \operatorname{span}\{\mathbf{u}\}$ and $\operatorname{rank}(A) = 1$. Conversely, if $\operatorname{rank}(A) = 1$, pick a nonzero basis vector $\mathbf{u}$ of $\operatorname{Col}(A)$. Then each column $\mathbf{a}_k$ of $A$ is a scalar multiple $\mathbf{a}_k = v_k \mathbf{u}$ (not all zero); setting $\mathbf{v} = (v_1,\dots,v_n)^T \neq \mathbf{0}$ yields $A = \mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^T$.
2. **Rank-$r$ Atomic Decomposition and Minimality**:
   - **Constructing the $r$-term decomposition (the classic $A = CR_0$ factorization)**: Suppose $\operatorname{rank}(A) = r \ge 1$, and let $C \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times r}$ consist of the $r$ linearly independent pivot columns of $A$. Since every column $\mathbf{a}_j$ of $A$ is a unique linear combination of the pivot columns, collecting those combination coefficients forms a matrix $F \in \mathbb{R}^{r\times n}$ with $A=CF$. Applying the same row operations to $A=CF$ gives $R=(EC)F$, where $EC=\begin{bmatrix}I_r\\0\end{bmatrix}$ consists of the pivot columns of $R$. Thus $F$ is exactly the first $r$ rows of $R$ and contains $I_r$ in the pivot columns, giving full row rank. This gives $A = C F = \sum_{k=1}^r \mathbf{u}_k \mathbf{v}_k^T$ via the column-times-row outer product perspective from Lecture 03.
   - **Proving Part 3 first (Subadditivity of Rank)**: For any $\mathbf{y} \in \operatorname{Col}(A + B)$, there exists $\mathbf{x}$ such that $\mathbf{y} = (A+B)\mathbf{x} = A\mathbf{x} + B\mathbf{x} \in \operatorname{Col}(A) + \operatorname{Col}(B)$. Hence $\operatorname{Col}(A + B) \subseteq \operatorname{Col}(A) + \operatorname{Col}(B)$! Taking dimensions on both sides and applying Theorem 11.1 (Grassmann's formula) gives:

     $$
     \operatorname{rank}(A + B) = \dim\operatorname{Col}(A + B) \le \dim(\operatorname{Col}(A) + \operatorname{Col}(B)) \le \dim\operatorname{Col}(A) + \dim\operatorname{Col}(B) = \operatorname{rank}(A) + \operatorname{rank}(B)
     $$

     Substituting $A = (A+B) + (-B)$ into this inequality yields $\operatorname{rank}(A) \le \operatorname{rank}(A+B) + \operatorname{rank}(B)$; rearranging and using symmetry establishes the lower bound $|\operatorname{rank}(A) - \operatorname{rank}(B)| \le \operatorname{rank}(A + B)$.
   - **Proving the minimality of $r$**: If $A = \sum_{k=1}^s \mathbf{p}_k \mathbf{q}_k^T$ is a sum of $s$ rank-1 matrices, repeated application of rank subadditivity gives $r = \operatorname{rank}(A) \le \sum_{k=1}^s 1 = s$. Therefore, no matrix of rank $r$ can be built from fewer than $r$ rank-1 pieces!
</ProofCollapse>

### Zero-Sum Hyperplanes in $\mathbb{R}^4$ and a Preview of Small-World Graphs

Before wrapping up Lecture 11, Strang presents two delightful miniatures:

1. **The Subspace of Zero-Sum Vectors in $\mathbb{R}^4$**: Consider the set $S = \{ \mathbf{v} = (v_1, v_2, v_3, v_4)^T \in \mathbb{R}^4 : v_1 + v_2 + v_3 + v_4 = 0 \}$. Is $S$ a subspace? Of course! The single scalar equation $v_1 + v_2 + v_3 + v_4 = 0$ is literally the nullspace equation $A\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$ for the $1 \times 4$ row matrix $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$! Since $A$ has rank $r = 1$, we can read off all four fundamental subspaces of $A$ without a single step of elimination:
   - **Nullspace $S = \operatorname{Null}(A) \subseteq \mathbb{R}^4$**: Dimension $4 - 1 = 3$ (a $3$-dimensional hyperplane in $\mathbb{R}^4$), with special-solution basis $(-1,1,0,0)^T,\; (-1,0,1,0)^T,\; (-1,0,0,1)^T$;
   - **Row Space $\operatorname{Row}(A) \subseteq \mathbb{R}^4$**: Dimension $r = 1$, with basis $(1,1,1,1)^T$;
   - **Column Space $\operatorname{Col}(A) = \mathbb{R}^1$**: Dimension $r = 1$, with basis $(1)$;
   - **Left Nullspace $\operatorname{Null}(A^T) \subseteq \mathbb{R}^1$**: Dimension $m - r = 1 - 1 = 0$, containing only the zero scalar $\{0\}$ (**remember: the basis of a zero-dimensional subspace is the empty set $\varnothing$, never the number $0$!**).

2. **Small-world graphs**: The Watts–Strogatz model shows how a small amount of random rewiring can greatly reduce characteristic path length while retaining high clustering. The next lecture encodes the edges of a graph in an incidence matrix. [Watts and Strogatz (1998)](https://doi.org/10.1038/30918)

<PitfallAlert title="Common Pitfalls & Transcript Errata">
1. **Pitfall 1: Believing that "all matrices of a fixed positive rank" form a subspace of $M$!**
   **Never a subspace (a classic exam trap)!** First, the zero matrix has rank $0$, so it doesn't even belong to the set of rank-$k$ matrices ($k \ge 1$). Second, the set fails closure under addition completely: adding two rank-1 matrices can either increase the rank to $2$ (as for $E_{11} + E_{22}$) or cancel out to rank $0$ (such as $E_{11} + (-E_{11}) = 0$)! Memorize this iron rule: **sets of matrices of fixed positive rank, invertible matrices, or matrices with $\det(A) = 1$ are NEVER subspaces!**
2. **Pitfall 2: Thinking the zero subspace $\{\mathbf{0}\}$ has dimension $1$, or writing its basis as $\{\mathbf{0}\}$**
   By the strict definition of linear independence, the zero vector $\mathbf{0}$ by itself is linearly dependent ($1\cdot\mathbf{0} = \mathbf{0}$ has a nonzero coefficient), so **no basis may ever contain the zero vector $\mathbf{0}$**! The trivial zero subspace $\{\mathbf{0}\}$ (such as $\operatorname{Null}(A^T)$ above) has **dimension $0$**, and its basis is the **empty set $\varnothing$** (containing $0$ vectors).
3. **Pitfall 3: Assuming $\operatorname{rank}(A + B) = \operatorname{rank}(A) + \operatorname{rank}(B)$ holds in general**
   Rank satisfies only the inequality $\operatorname{rank}(A+B) \le \operatorname{rank}(A) + \operatorname{rank}(B)$, never equality in general! Equality holds if and only if $\operatorname{Col}(A) \cap \operatorname{Col}(B) = \{\mathbf{0}\}$ and $\operatorname{Row}(A) \cap \operatorname{Row}(B) = \{\mathbf{0}\}$ (both the column spaces and the row spaces of the two matrices have zero intersection).

   If either matrix is zero, rank additivity is immediate. For sufficiency in the other cases, write rank factorizations $A=C_AF_A$ and $B=C_BF_B$. The column-space condition gives $[C_A\; C_B]$ full column rank. The row-space condition gives $\begin{bmatrix}F_A\\F_B\end{bmatrix}$ full row rank, since the rows of $F_A$ and $F_B$ are bases of the respective row spaces. Their product is $A+B$, and the product has rank $\operatorname{rank}(A)+\operatorname{rank}(B)$ because the left factor is injective and the right factor is surjective. Exercise 5 proves necessity.
</PitfallAlert>

        Counting the dimension of a matrix subspace is simply counting "how many grid slots can be filled freely":
        - For a skew-symmetric matrix $A^T = -A$, each diagonal entry equals its own negative, so the diagonal is forced to be all $0$! Only the $3$ upper-right slots can be chosen freely (the lower-left slots automatically mirror them with a minus sign), so $\dim(K) = 3$.

        - Symmetric matrices ($6$-D) and skew-symmetric matrices ($3$-D) share only the zero matrix ($S \cap K = \{0\}$), and together their dimensions add up to $6 + 3 = 9$, spanning all of $M$—meaning every square matrix in the universe splits uniquely into a "symmetric part + skew-symmetric part"!

      <SolutionRigorous>

        1. **Subspace Verification and Dimensions**:
           - For any $A, B \in K$ and $\alpha, \beta \in \mathbb{R}$, $(\alpha A + \beta B)^T = \alpha A^T + \beta B^T = -\alpha A - \beta B = -(\alpha A + \beta B)$, so $K$ is a subspace. From $a_{ji} = -a_{ij}$, setting $i=j$ gives $a_{11}=a_{22}=a_{33}=0$, leaving only the three free parameters $a_{12}, a_{13}, a_{23}$. Thus **$\dim(K) = 3$**, with standard basis:

             $$
             \mathcal{B}_K = \left\{ E_{12} - E_{21},\; E_{13} - E_{31},\; E_{23} - E_{32} \right\}
             $$

           - The trace operator $\operatorname{tr}: M \to \mathbb{R}$ is linear: $\operatorname{tr}(\alpha A + \beta B) = \alpha\operatorname{tr}(A) + \beta\operatorname{tr}(B)$. Since $\operatorname{tr}(E_{11})=1$, the trace has rank $1$. Rank-nullity applied to $Z=\ker(\operatorname{tr})$ yields **$\dim(Z) = 9 - 1 = 8$**.

        2. **Grassmann's Formula and Direct Sum Decomposition**:
           Suppose $A \in S \cap K$. Then $A = A^T = -A \implies 2A = 0 \implies A = 0$, so $S \cap K = \{0\}$ and $\dim(S \cap K) = 0$. By Theorem 11.1 (Grassmann's Dimension Formula):

           $$
           \dim(S + K) = \dim(S) + \dim(K) - \dim(S \cap K) = 6 + 3 - 0 = 9 = \dim(M)
           $$

           Hence $M = S \oplus K$. For every $A\in M$, the two parts are

           $$
           A = \underbrace{\frac{A + A^T}{2}}_{\in S} + \underbrace{\frac{A - A^T}{2}}_{\in K}
           $$

           The same formula works for every real square size. The displayed parts have the stated symmetries, and the symmetric and skew-symmetric subspaces intersect only at zero in each size.

    Let $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ be two nonzero column vectors, and form the $n \times n$ rank-1 square matrix $A = \mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^T$.

    <DualSolution
      triggerLabel="Show Full Dual Solution (Intuition + Rigorous Proof)"
      intuitionLabel="Geometric Intuition"
      rigorousLabel="Rigorous Proof / Construction"
    >
      <SolutionIntuition>

        An outer product $\mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^T$ is "column times row," whereas an inner product $\mathbf{v}^T\mathbf{u}$ is "row times column" (a single number)! When you multiply two outer products $(\mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^T)(\mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^T)$, the middle pair meets head-on to form the inner-product scalar $c = \mathbf{v}^T\mathbf{u}$! That collapses every power of a rank-1 matrix into elementary scalar multiplication. Better still, if you patch the identity matrix with a rank-1 update $I + \mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^T$, its inverse needs only a rank-1 counter-patch along the exact same direction $\mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^T$!

      </SolutionIntuition>
      <SolutionRigorous>

        1. **Square and Trace of a Rank-1 Square Matrix**:
           By the associativity of matrix multiplication:

           $$
           A^2 = (\mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^T)(\mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^T) = \mathbf{u} \underbrace{(\mathbf{v}^T\mathbf{u})}_{\text{scalar } c \in \mathbb{R}} \mathbf{v}^T = (\mathbf{v}^T\mathbf{u})\, \mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^T = c A
           $$

           where the scalar is $c = \mathbf{v}^T\mathbf{u} = \sum_{i=1}^n u_i v_i$. Since the $(i,j)$ entry of $A$ is $a_{ij} = u_i v_j$, the sum of its main diagonal entries is $\operatorname{tr}(A) = \sum_{i=1}^n a_{ii} = \sum_{i=1}^n u_i v_i = \mathbf{v}^T\mathbf{u} = c$. Therefore, **$A^2 = \operatorname{tr}(A)\,A$**. By induction, $A^k=c^{k-1}A$ for every integer $k\ge 2$.
           - **Nilpotent criterion**: $A^2 = 0 \iff c = \mathbf{v}^T\mathbf{u} = 0$ (meaning $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$ are orthogonal, or equivalently $\operatorname{Col}(A) \subseteq \operatorname{Null}(A)$);
           - **Idempotent criterion**: $A^2 = A \iff c = \mathbf{v}^T\mathbf{u} = 1$. In this case $A$ projects onto $\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf{u}\}$ along $\mathbf{v}^\perp$.

        2. **Sherman-Morrison Rank-1 Inverse Formula**:
           Postulate an inverse of the form $B = I_n - \alpha \mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^T$. Multiplying $I_n + \mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^T$ by $B$ and using $(\mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^T)^2 = (\mathbf{v}^T\mathbf{u})\mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^T$ gives:

           $$
           (I_n + \mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^T)(I_n - \alpha \mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^T) = I_n + \mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^T - \alpha \mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^T - \alpha (\mathbf{v}^T\mathbf{u}) \mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^T = I_n + \bigl[1 - \alpha(1 + \mathbf{v}^T\mathbf{u})\bigr] \mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^T
           $$

           When $1 + \mathbf{v}^T\mathbf{u} \neq 0$, setting $\alpha = \dfrac{1}{1 + \mathbf{v}^T\mathbf{u}}$ makes the second term vanish, proving that $I_n + \mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^T$ is invertible with:

           $$
           (I_n + \mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^T)^{-1} = I_n - \frac{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^T}{1 + \mathbf{v}^T\mathbf{u}}
           $$

           (Conversely, if $1 + \mathbf{v}^T\mathbf{u} = 0$, then $(I_n + \mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^T)\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{u} + (\mathbf{v}^T\mathbf{u})\mathbf{u} = (1 - 1)\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{0}$, so the nonzero vector $\mathbf{u}$ lies in the nullspace and the matrix is singular! Hence $1 + \mathbf{v}^T\mathbf{u} \neq 0$ is both necessary and sufficient for invertibility.)

### 1. A rank constraint on the update

LoRA freezes a pretrained weight $W_0\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{out}}\times d_{\text{in}}}$ and learns factors $B\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{out}}\times r}$ and $A\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times d_{\text{in}}}$, where $r\ge 1$ is a chosen rank bound. With scale $s=\alpha/r$, the effective weight is

$$
W'=W_0+\Delta W,\qquad
\Delta W=sBA=s\sum_{k=1}^r\mathbf{b}_k\mathbf{a}_k^T.
$$

Here $\mathbf{b}_k$ is column $k$ of $B$ and $\mathbf{a}_k^T$ is row $k$ of $A$. Each term has rank at most $1$ and $\operatorname{rank}(\Delta W)\le r$. For nonzero $s$, every update of rank at most $r$ admits this parameterization by rank factorization and zero padding. The algorithm chooses the bound $r$ as a modeling constraint. [Hu et al., Section 4.1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.09685)

### 2. Parameter count and subspace geometry

Full fine-tuning of this weight trains $d_{\text{out}}d_{\text{in}}$ entries. LoRA trains $r(d_{\text{out}}+d_{\text{in}})$ factor entries. For a $4096\times4096$ weight and $r=8$, the ratio is $65{,}536/16{,}777{,}216=1/256\approx0.39\%$.

For input $\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{in}}}$, the correction is $sB(A\mathbf{x})$. It is zero on $\operatorname{Null}(A)$, whose dimension is at least $d_{\text{in}}-r$. The full space of unchanged inputs for this layer is $\operatorname{Null}(BA)$ when $s\ne0$. The corrections lie in $\operatorname{Col}(B)$ of dimension at most $r$, and span $\operatorname{Col}(BA)$ for $s\ne0$.

### 3. Initialization and inference

The original initialization draws the entries of $A$ from a Gaussian distribution and sets $B=0$, so the effective weight starts at $W_0$. For one example, write $\mathbf{h}=W'\mathbf{x}$ and $\mathbf{g}=\nabla_{\mathbf{h}}\mathcal{L}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{out}}}$. The adapter gradients are $\nabla_B\mathcal{L}=s\mathbf{g}(A\mathbf{x})^T$ and $\nabla_A\mathcal{L}=s(B^T\mathbf{g})\mathbf{x}^T$. At initialization the first gradient can be nonzero, and the second is zero. Initializing both factors to zero would make both gradients vanish.

For a fixed adapter, precompute $W'=W_0+sBA$. The identity $W'\mathbf{x}=W_0\mathbf{x}+sB(A\mathbf{x})$ means inference can use the same single matrix multiplication as the base layer, up to floating-point rounding.

<CollapsibleCode title="PyTorch: Outer products, numerical rank and merging">

```python
import torch

torch.manual_seed(1806)
dtype = torch.float64
d_out, d_in, r = 96, 128, 8
s = 16 / r
W_0 = torch.randn(d_out, d_in, dtype=dtype)
A = torch.randn(r, d_in, dtype=dtype) * 0.02
B = torch.zeros(d_out, r, dtype=dtype)
assert not torch.count_nonzero(B @ A)

B = torch.randn(d_out, r, dtype=dtype) * 0.05
delta_W = s * (B @ A)
outer_sum = s * sum(B[:, k:k+1] @ A[k:k+1, :] for k in range(r))
torch.testing.assert_close(delta_W, outer_sum)
assert int(torch.linalg.matrix_rank(delta_W)) == r

x = torch.randn(d_in, 16, dtype=dtype)
out_branch = W_0 @ x + s * (B @ (A @ x))
out_merged = (W_0 + delta_W) @ x
torch.testing.assert_close(out_branch, out_merged)
ratio = (B.numel() + A.numel()) / W_0.numel()
print(f"Rank: {r} | Factor parameter ratio in this example: {ratio:.2%}")
```
