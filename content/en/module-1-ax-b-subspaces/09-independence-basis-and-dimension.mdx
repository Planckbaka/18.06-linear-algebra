If the first eight lectures finished assembling the "engine" of linear algebra—elimination, matrix inverses, $R = \operatorname{rref}(A)$, column spaces, and nullspaces—then MIT 18.06 Lecture 09 is the moment we give the entire subject its precise conceptual language. Today we rigorously define four of the most central words in modern mathematics: **Linear Independence**, **Span**, **Basis**, and **Dimension**.

In many purely axiomatic textbooks, these four concepts trap beginners in tongue-twisting verbal games. In Professor Gilbert Strang's classroom, however, **every single abstract concept translates instantaneously into the familiar matrix language of rank $r$ and the nullspace**!

---

## 1. Core Warm-Up: Why Must More Than $m$ Vectors in $\mathbb{R}^m$ Be Linearly Dependent?

Let us jump right in using an ironclad matrix law proved in Lecture 07. Suppose someone hands you **3 arbitrary vectors** $\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2, \mathbf{v}_3 \in \mathbb{R}^2$ in the 2D plane $\mathbb{R}^2$ (or **4 vectors** in 3D space $\mathbb{R}^3$). Could they possibly be "completely independent of one another, with zero redundancy"?

**Absolutely impossible!** How can we be 100% certain without looking at a single number?
Assemble those three 2D vectors side by side as the columns of a short-and-wide $2\times 3$ matrix $V = [\mathbf{v}_1 \; \mathbf{v}_2 \; \mathbf{v}_3] \in \mathbb{R}^{2\times 3}$:
- The matrix has only $2$ rows, so it can have at most $r \le 2$ pivots;
- Yet it has $3$ columns (unknowns), so there must be at least $3 - r \ge 1$ free variable!
- Consequently, the homogeneous system $V\mathbf{c} = \mathbf{0}$ **is guaranteed to have a nonzero solution** $\mathbf{c} = (c_1, c_2, c_3)^T \neq \mathbf{0}$. Written out as a column combination, this says:

$$
c_1 \mathbf{v}_1 + c_2 \mathbf{v}_2 + c_3 \mathbf{v}_3 = \mathbf{0} \quad (\text{where } c_1, c_2, c_3 \text{ are not all } 0)
$$

Linear independence is a property of a list of vectors. In this lecture the list usually consists of the columns of a matrix. Keeping the list matters because repeating a vector makes the list dependent.

<DefinitionCard
  label="Definition 9.1"
  title="Linear Independence, Span, and Basis"
>
Let $\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2, \dots, \mathbf{v}_k \in \mathbb{R}^m$ be a sequence of vectors, and assemble them as the columns of the matrix $V = [\mathbf{v}_1 \; \cdots \; \mathbf{v}_k] \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times k}$:

1. **Linearly Independent**: The list $(\mathbf{v}_1, \dots, \mathbf{v}_k)$ is **linearly independent** if the *only* linear combination that gives the zero vector, $c_1\mathbf{v}_1 + c_2\mathbf{v}_2 + \cdots + c_k\mathbf{v}_k = \mathbf{0}$, is the trivial combination $c_1 = c_2 = \cdots = c_k = 0$.
   - **Equivalent Matrix Language**: $(\mathbf{v}_1, \dots, \mathbf{v}_k)$ is linearly independent $\iff \operatorname{Null}(V) = \{\mathbf{0}\} \iff \operatorname{rank}(V) = k$ (full column rank, with zero free variables!).
   - Conversely, if there exist scalars not all zero such that $\sum_{i=1}^k c_i\mathbf{v}_i = \mathbf{0}$ (i.e., $\operatorname{Null}(V) \neq \{\mathbf{0}\}$ and $\operatorname{rank}(V) \lt k$), the vectors are **linearly dependent**. (In particular, any set of vectors containing the zero vector $\mathbf{0}$ is automatically linearly dependent, since $1\cdot\mathbf{0} = \mathbf{0}$!)

2. **Spanning a Subspace**: If $\mathbf{v}_1,\dots,\mathbf{v}_k\in S\subseteq\mathbb{R}^m$ and every vector in $S$ can be written as a linear combination of $\mathbf{v}_1, \dots, \mathbf{v}_k$ (that is, $S = \operatorname{span}\{\mathbf{v}_1, \dots, \mathbf{v}_k\} = \operatorname{Col}(V)$), we say that this set of vectors **spans** the subspace $S$.

3. **Basis for a Subspace**: A **basis** for a subspace $S$ is a list $(\mathbf{v}_1, \dots, \mathbf{v}_d)$ of vectors in $S$ that satisfies two conditions:
   - **① Linearly independent** (not too many—no redundancy: appending any vector from $S$ makes the list dependent);
   - **② Spans the entire subspace $S$** (not too few—full coverage: removing any vector from the set leaves it unable to cover all of $S$).
The empty list is the basis of the zero subspace $\{\mathbf{0}\}$, with the empty linear combination equal to $\mathbf{0}$.
</DefinitionCard>

## 2. The "Goldilocks Principle" of a Basis and Uniqueness of Coordinates

Let us examine what makes a valid **basis** in the familiar 2D plane $\mathbb{R}^2$:
- The most familiar **standard basis** consists of the two perpendicular coordinate-axis vectors $\mathbf{e}_1 = (1, 0)^T,\; \mathbf{e}_2 = (0, 1)^T$;
- **Yet a basis is far from unique!** Pick any two non-collinear slanted vectors, say $\mathbf{v}_1 = (1, 2)^T$ and $\mathbf{v}_2 = (3, 1)^T$, and pack them into a $2\times 2$ square matrix:

$$
B = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}
$$

  Because $B$ is invertible ($r = 2$), its two columns are both linearly independent ($\operatorname{Null}(B)=\{\mathbf{0}\}$) and span the entire plane ($\operatorname{Col}(B)=\mathbb{R}^2$). Therefore, $\{(1,2)^T, (3,1)^T\}$ is a completely legitimate basis for $\mathbb{R}^2$!
- Generalizing to $\mathbb{R}^n$: **$n$ vectors $\mathbf{v}_1, \dots, \mathbf{v}_n \in \mathbb{R}^n$ form a basis for the entire space $\mathbb{R}^n$ if and only if the $n\times n$ square matrix $B = [\mathbf{v}_1 \; \cdots \; \mathbf{v}_n]$ having them as columns is invertible!**

Why do mathematicians insist on requiring both "linear independence" and "spanning" at the same time? Because putting the two together produces a priceless treasure—**the existence and uniqueness of coordinates relative to a basis**: for any vector $\mathbf{b} \in S$, the *spanning* property guarantees that there exists at least one set of coefficients with $\mathbf{b} = c_1\mathbf{v}_1 + \cdots + c_d\mathbf{v}_d$. If there were a second set of coefficients $\mathbf{b} = d_1\mathbf{v}_1 + \cdots + d_d\mathbf{v}_d$, subtracting the two equations immediately yields $(c_1 - d_1)\mathbf{v}_1 + \cdots + (c_d - d_d)\mathbf{v}_d = \mathbf{0}$, and *linear independence* instantly forces $c_i = d_i$ for every $i$!

## 3. Invariance of Dimension and the Dimension Formulas for the Two Subspaces

Although a positive-dimensional real subspace $S$ has infinitely many bases, a miracle occurs that supports the entire edifice of linear algebra: **no matter how wildly different two bases chosen by two different people for the same subspace $S$ may look, the *number* of vectors in their bases is guaranteed to be 100% identical!** This invariant integer—depending only on the subspace itself and never on the choice of basis—is called the **dimension** of the subspace $S$, denoted $\dim(S)$.

Next, let us project the definition of dimension back onto the column space $\operatorname{Col}(A)$ and nullspace $\operatorname{Null}(A)$ of an arbitrary matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ of rank $r$, testing it on our reduced row echelon form example from Lecture 07:

$$
A = \begin{bmatrix}
\mathbf{1} & 2 & \mathbf{2} & 2 \\
\mathbf{2} & 4 & \mathbf{6} & 8 \\
\mathbf{3} & 6 & \mathbf{8} & 10
\end{bmatrix}
\quad \xrightarrow{\text{rref}} \quad
R = \begin{bmatrix}
\mathbf{1} & 2 & \mathbf{0} & -2 \\
\mathbf{0} & 0 & \mathbf{1} & 2 \\
\mathbf{0} & 0 & \mathbf{0} & 0
\end{bmatrix}
$$

Look at the simpler matrix $R$ first: the pivot columns of $R$ are **Column 1 and Column 3** (namely $(1,0,0)^T$ and $(0,1,0)^T$), while free Column 2 is $2$ times Column 1, and free Column 4 is $-2$ times Column 1 plus $2$ times Column 3.
Now comes the crucial question: **Do the columns of the original matrix $A$ obey the exact same linear dependency relations?**
**100% yes!** Because $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0} \iff R\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$ (elementary row operations never alter the linear dependency coefficients among the columns!): since Column 2 $= 2\times$ Column 1 in $R$, Column 2 $(2,4,6)^T$ in the original matrix $A$ must also equal $2\times$ Column 1 $(1,2,3)^T$; and since Columns 1 and 3 are linearly independent in $R$, **Column 1 $(1,2,3)^T$ and Column 3 $(2,6,8)^T$ of the original matrix $A$ itself must also be linearly independent and span all of $\operatorname{Col}(A)$**!

From this single observation flow the two most famous dimension formulas in linear algebra!

<TheoremCard
  label="Theorem 9.1"
  title="Invariance of Dimension & the Rank-Nullity Theorem"
>
1. **Existence of a Basis and Invariance of Basis Size**. Every subspace $S\subseteq\mathbb{R}^m$ has a basis. If $\{\mathbf{v}_1, \dots, \mathbf{v}_p\}$ and $\{\mathbf{w}_1, \dots, \mathbf{w}_q\}$ are both bases for $S$, then necessarily **$p = q$**. This unique integer is called the **dimension** of $S$, denoted $\dim(S) := p$.
2. **Dimension of the Column Space & Standard Basis Extraction**: For any real matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$, the **$r$ pivot columns of the original matrix $A$** (corresponding to the column indices where pivots appear in $R$) form a basis for the column space $\operatorname{Col}(A)$. In particular, **the dimension of the column space equals the rank of the matrix**:

$$
\dim \operatorname{Col}(A) = \operatorname{rank}(A) = r
$$

3. **Dimension of the Nullspace & Rank-Nullity Conservation**: The $n - r$ **special solutions** constructed in Lecture 07 form a basis for the nullspace $\operatorname{Null}(A)$. Thus **the dimension of the nullspace (also called the nullity) equals the number of free variables $n - r$**:

$$
\dim \operatorname{Null}(A) = n - r \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad \dim \operatorname{Col}(A) + \dim \operatorname{Null}(A) = n
$$
</TheoremCard>

<ProofCollapse title="Proof of Basis Existence and Invariance of Basis Size">
Start with the empty list and append a vector of $S$ outside the current span whenever one exists. A dependence involving the new vector would express it in the previous span, so the list stays independent. It can never exceed $m$ vectors, so it eventually spans $S$ and is a basis.

This is one of the most elegant short proofs in the entire textbook, showcasing the sheer power of matrix reasoning! Let the columns of $V = [\mathbf{v}_1 \; \cdots \; \mathbf{v}_p] \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times p}$ and $W = [\mathbf{w}_1 \; \cdots \; \mathbf{w}_q] \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times q}$ be two bases for the same subspace $S$. We prove by contradiction that $q \gt p$ is impossible:

Suppose $q \gt p$. Since $\{\mathbf{v}_1, \dots, \mathbf{v}_p\}$ spans all of $S$ and each $\mathbf{w}_j \in S$, every vector $\mathbf{w}_j$ can be written as a linear combination of the columns of $V$: $\mathbf{w}_j = V\mathbf{a}_j$ for some coordinate vector $\mathbf{a}_j \in \mathbb{R}^p$. Stacking these $q$ columns side by side as a single matrix product gives:

$$
W = V A, \qquad \text{where the coefficient matrix is } A = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{a}_1 & \cdots & \mathbf{a}_q \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{p \times q}
$$

Look closely at the shape of the coefficient matrix $A$: **it has $p$ rows and $q$ columns, and we assumed more columns than rows ($q \gt p$)**! By **Theorem 7.1 Part 2** from Lecture 07 (every short-and-wide matrix has a nontrivial nullspace solution), there must exist a **nonzero vector** $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^q \setminus \{\mathbf{0}\}$ such that $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$! Multiplying both sides on the left by $V$ immediately yields:

$$
W\mathbf{x} = (VA)\mathbf{x} = V(A\mathbf{x}) = V\mathbf{0} = \mathbf{0}
$$

This means the columns of $W$ have a nontrivial linear combination that adds to zero—directly contradicting the assumption that $\{\mathbf{w}_1, \dots, \mathbf{w}_q\}$ is a basis (and therefore linearly independent)! Hence we must have $q \le p$. By complete symmetry, swapping the roles of $V$ and $W$ gives $p \le q$, so **$p = q$**. Q.E.D.!
</ProofCollapse>

<PitfallAlert title="Common Pitfalls & Transcript Errata">
1. **Pitfall 1: Copying the pivot columns of $R = \operatorname{rref}(A)$ instead of the pivot columns of the original matrix $A$ when asked for a basis of $\operatorname{Col}(A)$! (The #1 midterm exam disaster!)**
   Circle this in red three times: in the example above, the two pivot columns of $R$ are $(1,0,0)^T$ and $(0,1,0)^T$ (whose third components are both $0$). They span $\operatorname{Col}(R)$ and **do not even lie in $\operatorname{Col}(A)$**! You look at $R$ only to identify the **column indices** of the pivots (Columns 1 and 3), and then **you must go back to the original matrix $A$ to copy Column 1 $(1,2,3)^T$ and Column 3 $(2,6,8)^T$** as your basis vectors!
2. **Pitfall 2: Saying "the dimension of matrix $A$ is $3\times 4$" or "the rank of a matrix equals its dimension"**
   In precise mathematical terminology: **a matrix has a "size / shape ($m\times n$)" and a "rank ($r$)", never a "dimension"**! Only **vector spaces and subspaces** have a "dimension"—for example, "the dimension of the subspace $\operatorname{Col}(A)$ equals the rank $r$ of the matrix $A$."
3. **Pitfall 3: Assuming that "any $d$ vectors in a $d$-dimensional subspace $S$ automatically form a basis"**
   You must still verify *either* "linear independence" *or* "spanning $S$"! However, when the number of vectors **exactly matches the known dimension $d = \dim(S)$**, there is a tremendously useful labor-saving theorem (see Exercise `l09-ex3` below): inside a $d$-dimensional space, $d$ vectors are **linearly independent $\iff$ they span the entire space $S$** (check one half and the other half comes for free!).
</PitfallAlert>

<SolutionIntuition>

1. Stick to the golden rule—"use $R$ to find the column numbers, then go back to $A$ to fetch the actual vectors": the pivot columns are Column 1 and Column 3, so $\dim\operatorname{Col}(A) = 2$ and a basis for $\operatorname{Col}(A)$ is $\{(2, -1, 3)^T,\; (1, 1, 0)^T\}$. <SolutionRigorous>

1. From $R$, the pivots lie in Columns 1 and 3, so $\operatorname{rank}(A) = 2$:
   - $\dim\operatorname{Col}(A) = \mathbf{2}$, and extracting Columns 1 and 3 of $A$ gives a basis for $\operatorname{Col}(A)$:

$$
\mathcal{B}_{\operatorname{Col}(A)} = \left\{ \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \right\}, \qquad
\text{whereas } \mathcal{B}_{\operatorname{Col}(R)} = \left\{ \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \right\}

2. The free variables are $x_2, x_4$ ($4 - 2 = 2$ in total), so $\dim\operatorname{Null}(A) = \mathbf{2}$, and the two special solutions form a basis for $\operatorname{Null}(A)$:

$$
\mathcal{B}_{\operatorname{Null}(A)} = \left\{ \begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 0 \\ -3 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \right\}

   Conservation of dimension: $\dim\operatorname{Col}(A) + \dim\operatorname{Null}(A) = 2 + 2 = 4 = n$.

<SolutionIntuition>

Use an existing basis of $S$ to give each vector $d=\dim(S)$ coordinates. The $d$ candidate vectors then form a square coordinate matrix. <SolutionRigorous>

Let $S\subseteq\mathbb{R}^m$ have dimension $d>0$ and take any list of $d$ vectors in $S$. With a basis matrix $B\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}$ for $S$, write the candidate vectors as columns of $U=BC$ for $C\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$. Because $\operatorname{Null}(B)=\{\mathbf{0}\}$, we have $U\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}\iff C\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}$, so the columns of $U$ are independent exactly when $C$ is invertible.

Every target in $S$ is $B\mathbf{y}$ for some $\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^d$. The equation $U\mathbf{x}=B\mathbf{y}$ holds exactly when $C\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{y}$, again because $B$ has trivial nullspace. Thus the columns of $U$ span $S$ exactly when $C$ is onto $\mathbb{R}^d$, which is also equivalent to invertibility. For $d=0$, the empty list is a basis. In particular, the three independent vectors in the exercise form a basis of $S$.

<SolutionIntuition>

1. Pack the three vectors into the columns of $V = [\mathbf{v}_1 \; \mathbf{v}_2 \; \mathbf{v}_3]$, and the problem immediately reduces to a $3\times 3$ toy block game:
   - For the first set, $W = V M_1$ with

$$
M_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}
$$

     Is this $3\times 3$ transition matrix $M_1$ invertible? Two steps of elimination produce pivots $1, 1, 2$ (all nonzero, so $M_1$ is invertible!). Since neither $V$ nor $M_1$ sends any nonzero vector to zero, the product $W = V M_1$ has only $\mathbf{0}$ in its nullspace—**Set 1 is strictly linearly independent**!
2. Set 2 can be settled at a glance (it is our old friend, the cyclic difference matrix from Lecture 01!): adding the three new vectors directly gives $\mathbf{u}_1 + \mathbf{u}_2 + \mathbf{u}_3 = (\mathbf{v}_1 - \mathbf{v}_2) + (\mathbf{v}_2 - \mathbf{v}_3) + (\mathbf{v}_3 - \mathbf{v}_1) = \mathbf{0}$! The nonzero weights $(1, 1, 1) \neq (0, 0, 0)$ make the combination vanish on the spot—**Set 2 is always linearly dependent**!

</SolutionIntuition>
<SolutionRigorous>

Let $V = [\mathbf{v}_1 \; \mathbf{v}_2 \; \mathbf{v}_3] \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times 3}$. Because $\{\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2, \mathbf{v}_3\}$ is linearly independent, we have $\operatorname{Null}(V) = \{\mathbf{0}\}$.

1. **Set 1 is linearly independent**: Write the matrix product $W = [\mathbf{w}_1 \; \mathbf{w}_2 \; \mathbf{w}_3] = V M_1$, where

$$
M_1 = \begin{bmatrix}
1 & 0 & 1 \\
1 & 1 & 0 \\
0 & 1 & 1
\end{bmatrix}
\xrightarrow{R_2 - R_1}
\begin{bmatrix}
1 & 0 & 1 \\
0 & 1 & -1 \\
0 & 1 & 1
\end{bmatrix}
\xrightarrow{R_3 - R_2}
\begin{bmatrix}
\mathbf{1} & 0 & 1 \\
0 & \mathbf{1} & -1 \\
0 & 0 & \mathbf{2}
\end{bmatrix}
$$

   Since $M_1$ has $3$ nonzero pivots, $M_1$ is invertible ($\operatorname{Null}(M_1) = \{\mathbf{0}\}$). Consequently, if $W\mathbf{c} = V(M_1\mathbf{c}) = \mathbf{0}$, then $\operatorname{Null}(V) = \{\mathbf{0}\}$ first implies $M_1\mathbf{c} = \mathbf{0}$, and the invertibility of $M_1$ then forces $\mathbf{c} = \mathbf{0}$. Hence $\{\mathbf{w}_1, \mathbf{w}_2, \mathbf{w}_3\}$ is linearly independent.

2. **Set 2 is linearly dependent**: Taking the nonzero weights $(c_1, c_2, c_3) = (1, 1, 1)$, direct addition gives

$$
1\cdot\mathbf{u}_1 + 1\cdot\mathbf{u}_2 + 1\cdot\mathbf{u}_3 = (\mathbf{v}_1 - \mathbf{v}_2) + (\mathbf{v}_2 - \mathbf{v}_3) + (\mathbf{v}_3 - \mathbf{v}_1) = \mathbf{0}
$$

   By Definition 9.1, $\{\mathbf{u}_1, \mathbf{u}_2, \mathbf{u}_3\}$ is linearly dependent. The span has dimension exactly $2$: a relation $a\mathbf{u}_1+b\mathbf{u}_2=\mathbf{0}$ becomes $a\mathbf{v}_1+(b-a)\mathbf{v}_2-b\mathbf{v}_3=\mathbf{0}$ and forces $a=b=0$.

</SolutionRigorous>

In Lecture 03 (Theorem 3.1), we proved the subspace inclusion $\operatorname{Col}(AB) \subseteq \operatorname{Col}(A)$. Equipped now with the **Dimension and Rank Theorem** from Lecture 09, complete the following two milestone theoretical derivations (for $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ and $B \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times p}$):
1. First, rigorously prove **subspace monotonicity**: if two subspaces satisfy $S_1 \subseteq S_2 \subseteq \mathbb{R}^m$, then $\dim(S_1) \le \dim(S_2)$, with equality **if and only if** $S_1 = S_2$!
2. Using Part 1, without invoking transposes (even before proving $\operatorname{rank}(M^T) = \operatorname{rank}(M)$!), prove the fundamental rank upper bound for matrix multiplication directly from two perspectives—**column space inclusion** and **spanning via a basis of $\operatorname{Col}(B)$**:

$$
\operatorname{rank}(AB) \le \min\big(\operatorname{rank}(A),\; \operatorname{rank}(B)\big)
$$

And deduce as a corollary: if $P \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times m}$ is invertible, multiplying on the left by an invertible matrix never changes the rank, i.e., **$\operatorname{rank}(PA) = \operatorname{rank}(A)$**!

<DualSolution
  triggerLabel="Show Full Dual Solution (Intuition + Rigorous Proof)"
  intuitionLabel="Geometric Intuition"
  rigorousLabel="Rigorous Proof / Construction"
>
<SolutionIntuition>

1. If a smaller room sits inside a larger room, the smaller room cannot possibly have more independent coordinate axes than the larger room. And the moment the number of independent axes in the smaller room catches up to the dimension of the larger room, Exercise `l09-ex3`'s "dimension-pinning theorem" guarantees that the smaller room fills the larger room completely ($S_1 = S_2$)!
2. Proving $\operatorname{rank}(AB) \le \operatorname{rank}(A)$ takes half a line: $\operatorname{Col}(AB) \subseteq \operatorname{Col}(A) \implies \dim\operatorname{Col}(AB) \le \dim\operatorname{Col}(A)$!
   Proving $\operatorname{rank}(AB) \le \operatorname{rank}(B)$ is equally stunning: no matter how the input $\mathbf{x}$ varies, the intermediate output $B\mathbf{x}$ is spanned by the $r_B$ basis vectors of $\operatorname{Col}(B)$. Feeding just those $r_B$ basis vectors through the matrix $A$ produces $r_B$ output vectors $A\mathbf{w}_1, \dots, A\mathbf{w}_{r_B}$—so the entire space $\operatorname{Col}(AB)$ is spanned by those $r_B$ arrows, meaning its dimension is at most $r_B$!

</SolutionIntuition>
<SolutionRigorous>

1. **Proof of Subspace Dimension Monotonicity**: Let $\dim(S_1) = k$ and $\dim(S_2) = d$, and choose basis matrices $U = [\mathbf{u}_1\;\cdots\;\mathbf{u}_k]$ for $S_1$ and $V = [\mathbf{v}_1\;\cdots\;\mathbf{v}_d]$ for $S_2$. Since $S_1 \subseteq S_2$, there exists a coefficient matrix $C \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times k}$ such that $U = VC$. If $k \gt d$, the wide matrix $C$ has a nonzero vector $\mathbf{z}$ satisfying $C\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{0} \implies U\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{0}$, contradicting the linear independence of the columns of $U$! Thus $k \le d$. When $k = d$, by the result proved in Exercise `l09-ex3`, any $d$ linearly independent vectors in $S_2$ must span all of $S_2$, so $S_1 = \operatorname{span}\{\mathbf{u}_1,\dots,\mathbf{u}_d\} = S_2$. Conversely, if $S_1=S_2$, their dimensions are equal.

2. **Proof of $\operatorname{rank}(AB) \le \min(\operatorname{rank}(A), \operatorname{rank}(B))$**:
   - **Left-factor upper bound**: Combining $\operatorname{Col}(AB) \subseteq \operatorname{Col}(A)$ (Theorem 3.1 Part 1) with the monotonicity from Part 1 and Theorem 9.1 Part 2 immediately yields:

$$
\operatorname{rank}(AB) = \dim\operatorname{Col}(AB) \le \dim\operatorname{Col}(A) = \operatorname{rank}(A)
$$

   - **Right-factor upper bound**: Let $r_B := \operatorname{rank}(B) = \dim\operatorname{Col}(B)$, and choose a basis $\{\mathbf{w}_1, \dots, \mathbf{w}_{r_B}\}$ for $\operatorname{Col}(B)$ (if $r_B = 0$, then $B=0 \implies AB=0$, which holds trivially). For any $\mathbf{y} \in \operatorname{Col}(AB)$, there exists $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^p$ such that $\mathbf{y} = A(B\mathbf{x})$. Since $B\mathbf{x} \in \operatorname{Col}(B)$, there exist scalars $c_1, \dots, c_{r_B}$ such that $B\mathbf{x} = \sum_{i=1}^{r_B} c_i \mathbf{w}_i$. Multiplying both sides on the left by $A$ gives:

$$
\mathbf{y} = A\left(\sum_{i=1}^{r_B} c_i \mathbf{w}_i\right) = \sum_{i=1}^{r_B} c_i (A\mathbf{w}_i) \in \operatorname{span}\{A\mathbf{w}_1, \dots, A\mathbf{w}_{r_B}\}
$$

     Thus $\operatorname{Col}(AB)$ is spanned by at most $r_B$ vectors, which implies $\operatorname{rank}(AB) = \dim\operatorname{Col}(AB) \le r_B = \operatorname{rank}(B)$.

3. **Corollary (Left-Multiplication by an Invertible Matrix Preserves Rank)**: If $P \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times m}$ is invertible, applying the above inequality twice in succession gives:

$$
\operatorname{rank}(PA) \le \operatorname{rank}(A) = \operatorname{rank}\!\big(P^{-1}(PA)\big) \le \operatorname{rank}(PA)
\quad \Longrightarrow \quad \operatorname{rank}(PA) = \operatorname{rank}(A)
$$

</SolutionRigorous>
